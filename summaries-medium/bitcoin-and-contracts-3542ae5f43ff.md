# Bitcoin and Contracts
**Date:** 2018-09-29 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/bitcoin-and-contracts-3542ae5f43ff

## Core thesis
Bitcoin transactions are legally analogous to e-mail and postal mail, not to instantaneous communication, so the existing common-law debates over the "postal rule" and the moment of contractual acceptance apply directly to Bitcoin. Because block inclusion is not instant, Craig argues the same legal framework developed for e-mail (and even NNTP) governs when a Bitcoin transaction counts as sent, received and accepted. The essay closes by reasserting that "Bitcoin is P2P electronic Cash" — a system sitting inside contract law, not outside it.

## Key arguments and claims
- The old e-mail debate over the postal rule maps onto Bitcoin: there are several candidate "moments of acceptance", starting with the message departing the sender's outbox, which "is a situation analogous to the postal rule" and "analogous to the structure of Bitcoin transactions and how these are sent".
- Craig enumerates the acceptance candidates: (1) e-mail leaves the sender's outbox; (2) receipt into the recipient's inbox/server — contrasted with a transaction appearing in the blockchain versus being downloaded to a wallet; (3) collection from the mail server into the client; (4) the moment a wallet "accepts the transaction and allows the user to spend it"; (5) the recipient opening/reading the message.
- Features such as e-mail recall, read receipts and send receipts "further obfuscated the moment that could be considered the time when acceptance was made" — and likewise "the ability to have SPV and other nodes on the network has confused many as to the nature of Bitcoin".
- E-mail is "the digital equivalent of a letter sent through the post", and all postal functions carry over analogously, including invitations to treat (citing *Partridge v Crittenden* [1968]) as well as offers and acceptances — "all of this relates to Bitcoin".
- He quotes Cavazos & Morin (1994): whether the mailbox rule applies to e-mail "is one that the courts have not yet answered", depending on whether e-mail is "more like instantaneous communication than like traditional mail services" — and says this is "the scenario many see us in with respect to Bitcoin".
- Bitcoin "maybe fast, but it is not instantaneous": failed delivery, rerouting, damage and delay arise with both e-mail and Bitcoin transactions, so Bitcoin "may be argued to most closely mirror a postal letter delivery".
- Because "the inclusion of transactions into a block is not instant", "the same laws apply to Bitcoin as we have seen adopted in email and even NNTP".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Reasoning by legal analogy: he takes a settled pre-existing doctrinal debate (contract formation and the postal/mailbox rule in e-commerce law) and maps each procedural stage of e-mail transmission onto a stage of Bitcoin transaction propagation. The method is case-law and secondary-literature driven (citing *Partridge v Crittenden*, Cavazos & Morin 1994), treating law as a fixed frame into which the new technology must be fitted, rather than something the technology displaces.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the "code is law" / extra-legal framing of crypto: Bitcoin transactions are ordinary legally-governed communications subject to contract-formation doctrine, not events outside the legal system.
- Contradicts the popular "instant, irreversible broadcast" mental model: Craig stresses block inclusion is not instant and delivery can fail or be delayed, so Bitcoin mirrors postal delivery law rather than instantaneous electronic settlement.
- Pushes against the SPV/light-client confusion he attributes to the community: the existence of SPV and partial nodes "has confused many as to the nature of Bitcoin" — receipt by the network, by the wallet, and spendability are distinct legal moments.
