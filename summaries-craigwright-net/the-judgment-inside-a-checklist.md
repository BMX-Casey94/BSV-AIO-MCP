# The judgment inside a checklist
**Date:** 2026-10-19 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/the-judgment-inside-a-checklist | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a checklist is useful when it preserves the questions an incident responder must ask, guides a decision, and still leaves a record of unfinished judgment. Completing the sequence, it says, does not settle every interpretation of an event. The evaluation it wants is which decisions become more dependable for particular people in particular circumstances.

## Key arguments and claims
- The opening scene is presented as hypothetical. An analyst investigating an unusual login is interrupted, and a colleague takes over. The article says a useful record lets that colleague separate completed checks, unresolved questions, and a decision still awaiting authority. It later contrasts a record that says only "checks complete" with one that separates observation, interpretation and authorisation, and labels that comparison "explanatory analysis, not a tested product or a report of a new experiment."
- It cites Craig S. Wright and Tanveer A. Zia (2011), *Using checklists to make better best*, 9th Australian Information Security Management Conference, pp. 245–251. The article says the paper examined incident responders using brief checklists they had prepared themselves. A virtual coin toss determined whether a checklist was used. The measured interval ended when the responder determined whether an event was an incident.
- The article states the reported mean times as 14.36020 minutes without a checklist and 14.00188 with one, a difference of approximately 21.5 seconds. It calls that a modest average difference in this setting, and says it does not measure the time to contain an attack, restore a service, or complete recovery.
- It says the study describes repeated observations involving individual responders. A new evaluation, it argues, should make that grouping explicit and distinguish the number of observations from the number of people. The historical result "supplies a question to investigate in another team, rather than a universal forecast of its performance."
- Steve Bellovin (2008), *Security by Checklist*, *IEEE Security & Privacy* 6(2), p. 88, is presented as an expert argument, not a replication of the experiment. The article's reading is that a reminder can be useful while rigid compliance can conceal a trade-off, and that choosing among security objectives requires attention to the system and the consequences. It says the commentary's historical technical examples are not offered as present-day configuration advice, and that remembering to examine a condition differs from establishing that a particular action is appropriate.
- NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3, which the article says was published in April 2025, is described as placing incident response within continuing cybersecurity risk management. The article says Section 2.3 connects procedures to policy and plans, including roles and authority; playbooks are actionable steps for particular situations and their format can improve usability; detailed procedures cannot cover every situation; and common incidents and important emergency processes should be documented and then tested or exercised. It says ID.IM-01 and ID.IM-02, in the Improvement category, call for performance evaluation, and that tests and exercises can expose deficiencies while preparing the people involved.
- The article treats that guidance as a separate source of recommendations. It says the guidance does not establish that the earlier experiment's measured effect transfers to current teams. Someone must maintain the checklist, someone must be authorised to act, and experience must be able to change the procedure.
- The proposed follow-on study is not reported as performed. The article says it could compare procedures under ordinary work, interruptions and handovers, including unfamiliar cases and cases that require escalation, and would need to separate a missed required check, a mistaken interpretation, and a delayed but justified decision. Elapsed time would remain one outcome. Others could include omitted steps, inappropriate escalation, and the information available to the next responder. Observations should stay tied to the person, team and scenario. Maintenance cost is included, because a procedure that is quick to follow may take substantial work to keep accurate as systems and responsibilities change.
- The sources note says the page was published on 19 October 2026, that the handover example and proposed evaluation are explanatory synthesis, and that no new experiment, present-day effect estimate, or operational deployment is reported.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The article stacks a hypothetical handover, a 2011 timing result, an expert commentary, and a standards document, and then refuses to let any one of them stand in for the others. The means are kept at the scale of deciding whether something is an incident. Bellovin is used to separate a reminder from a justified action. NIST is used to place the checklist among roles, authority and revision. The last move specifies the distinctions a later evaluation would have to record.
