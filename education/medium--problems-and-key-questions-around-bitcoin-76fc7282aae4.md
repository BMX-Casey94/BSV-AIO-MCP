# Problems and key questions around Bitcoin — core principles

- **Old money statutes can reach digital tokens.** The Stamp Payments Act of 1862 (18 U.S.C. §336), enacted "to stop the circulation of private tokens that were in competition with federal postage stamps", uses language — "note, check, memorandum, token" — that "may apply to electronic transactions" (citing Vartanian, Ledig & Demianczuk 1996).
- **No issuer, no conventional obligation.** "The peer-to-peer nature of the currency means that obligations are derived in a manner unlike that of other monetary sources" — the absence of a third-party issuer is itself a legal problem to be analysed, not a feature that ends legal analysis.
- **Polymorphism thesis.** "It is not that Bitcoin is currency, a security or a token, it is that the multifariousness of the system allows it to be used as any of these at any time" — like gold, which serves as exchange medium, electronics input and jewellery.
- **Classify the transaction, not the asset.** The difficulty "is not in determining whether bitcoin has value but rather implementing a framework that captures the particular transaction and allows this to be successfully classified" — legal character follows use.
- **Fraud is transformed, not eliminated.** "Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies do not mitigate opportunities for fraud, but transform many old frauds opening up global prospects"; online scammers "close-down their operations quickly … use digital technologies to conceal their identities" (Russell Smith, Australian Institute of Criminology, 2000).
- **Regulatory perimeter already exists.** The research agenda spans securities regulation, FinCEN money-transmitter guidance (FIN-2008-G008) consumer/investor protections, and cross-jurisdictional enforcement (ECB Virtual Currency Schemes, 2012).
