# Service, Stake, and the Curious Case of Misclassification
**Date:** 2026-04-13 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/service-stake-and-the-curious-case
**Subtitle:** Why Proof-of-Work Is Labour, Proof-of-Stake Is Ownership, and How Blind Peer Review Momentarily Allowed Reason to Prevail

## Core thesis
Proof-of-work and proof-of-stake are not variations of one phenomenon but opposite legal categories: PoW establishes service provision (contingent, competitive, external labour), while PoS establishes ownership (constitutive, proportional, internal capital deployment). Regulators who flatten the two produce classifications that are simultaneously overinclusive and underinclusive, with cascading errors across securities law, taxation and financial regulation. The essay announces that Craig's paper making this case has been accepted for conference presentation following blind peer review.

## Key arguments and claims
- The PoW miner brings something entirely external to the asset — computational effort, energy expenditure, capital equipment — and performs a defined, measurable, verifiable task. The protocol behaves "like a particularly austere employer issuing a unilateral contract: perform this task, and you shall be paid." No performance, no reward; no residual claim afterwards.
- The miner need not own the asset he secures, and may separately choose to hold earned tokens as speculation — a distinct activity. "To conflate the two is to confuse a wage with an investment."
- PoS makes participation inseparable from possession: "to validate is to stake; to stake is to commit capital; and to commit capital is to place oneself in a position of entitlement to yield." The validator earns because he has something, not because he has done something — a structure "instantly recognisable to anyone familiar with equity, dividends, or interest-bearing instruments." It is, in essence, a yield on capital.
- The decisive asymmetry: "One can mine without owning; one cannot stake without owning." That single difference reconfigures the entire legal character of the activity — ontological, not merely technical.
- Securities law: under SEC v. W.J. Howey Co. (1946), the PoW miner does not invest in a common enterprise and his return derives from his own computational work, not the efforts of others — "To call this a security is to drain the term of meaning." PoS involves committed capital, an expected proportional return, and dependence on developers and other participants; staking pools aggregate capital and outsource management, resembling investment arrangements closely enough "to warrant serious consideration".
- Tax: PoW rewards are income from services — analogous to business income, with deductible costs (electricity, hardware, maintenance). PoS rewards are income from capital, resembling dividends or interest. Treating them identically ignores economic substance and invites "arbitrage, confusion, and, inevitably, litigation".
- Financial regulation: pooled staking approaches the definition of a collective investment scheme "with disconcerting fidelity" — capital contributed, aggregated, managed by a subset of operators, returns distributed proportionally, fees extracted. Mining pools are different in kind: they aggregate computational effort, not capital — "closer to a cooperative of service providers than to a financial intermediary".
- The corrective proposal is deliberately conservative: apply existing legal categories precisely — PoW as service provision under ordinary business rules; PoS as capital deployment, evaluated against securities and collective-investment frameworks.
- On blind peer review: stripped of his name, the argument "was allowed to stand on its own merits". Had his identity been attached, "certain readers might have found reasons — perfectly sincere, no doubt — to object", since "bias... is the one commodity that remains in inexhaustible supply".
- Misclassification is not academic: it determines how systems are regulated, participants taxed, risks allocated and rights defined. "To misclassify is to misgovern. To misgovern is to distort" — and distortion embeds itself in policy until it no longer appears as error.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Classification by economic substance: he identifies the ontological relationship between participant and asset (external and instrumental versus internal and constitutive), then maps each onto existing legal categories — contract of employment versus share certificate, wages versus dividends, service cooperative versus managed fund. The method is corrective rather than revolutionary: precision in applying law that already exists. The register is satirical, using irony ("legal analysis conducted with the lights dimmed") to expose regulatory intellectual laziness.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Mainstream crypto and its regulators treat PoW and PoS as interchangeable "consensus mechanisms" — a policy neutrality Craig calls a category mistake with "catastrophic consequence".
- The post-Merge Ethereum settlement — that PoS is simply a greener successor to PoW — collapses: staking is capital deployment belonging inside securities and collective-investment analysis, while PoW mining stands outside Howey entirely. The two are not substitutes, so "upgrading" from one to the other changes the legal nature of the system, not merely its energy profile.
- The essay does not attack BTC directly; its target is the regulatory flattening that groups Bitcoin's labour-based consensus with Ethereum's ownership-based one.

## Notable quotes
