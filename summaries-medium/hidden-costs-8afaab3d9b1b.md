# Hidden costs
**Date:** 2018-10-06 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/hidden-costs-8afaab3d9b1b
**Subtitle:** The flaw in stating that economic calculations cannot be conducted is the constant cry that not all costs can be calculated. This is true…

## Core thesis
The objection that economic calculation is impossible because "not all costs can be calculated" is a sophism: subjective valuation does not prevent calculation, it is precisely why the price system is needed. Because resources are scarce and every choice imposes hidden flow-on costs on others, no authority may legitimately spend other people's resources on its own values. Applied to Bitcoin: developers at Bitcoin Core — and now Bitcoin ABC — who alter the base protocol are running experiments "using other people's money", exploiting the network effect, and every such change imposes uncertainty costs on all users.

## Key arguments and claims
- All valuations are subjective, so calling other people's behaviour "irrational" merely privileges your own values over theirs; a man whose goal is to watch sport and drink beer acts rationally toward his own ends.
- Because nobody agrees on the ideal allocation (Thomas More's *Utopia* "was close to my idea of hell"), agreement is impossible and "this is why there is no agreement and why we need the pricing system" — prices are the mechanism for choosing between competing uses.
- Land conservation has hidden flow-on costs: locking away land raises real-estate prices, pushes farming onto marginal land, and "lead[s] to an increase in the cost of food" — with each additional section of land withdrawn becoming progressively more expensive.
- Biofuels repeat the same mechanism: diverting arable land from food to fuel cuts food supply and raises prices; western nations absorb the inflation but in developing countries "food inflation will lead to starvation at the extreme".
- Regulation rests on the premise "that a select group of people know better than all others, that their subjective valuations mean more than the average person", enforced through taxation — a few choosing what is best for the majority.
- The same structure exists in Bitcoin: "first in Bitcoin Core (BTC) and now, in ABC", people "want to try and experiment and they want to do this at others expense" — using the existing network effect rather than competing on their own chain.
- Scarcity is foundational: "there is and never will be enough for all people to do all that they want. This is a world of scarcity. Should it be different, there would be no place for economics."
- Every base-protocol change creates uncertainty and therefore cost: "Some gain, those who want the change, the rest of us pay."
- Ethereum is praised by contrast: "At least Ethereum (for all the flaws) had the balls to split off and compete" — and Craig adds the counterfactual that "if Core had not tried to hijack Bitcoin, Eth would have been a Bitcoin project."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Austrian-school reasoning: subjective value theory, the economic-calculation debate, and opportunity/flow-on costs, argued from general principle to concrete mechanism (land supply → food prices) and then by analogy to protocol governance. The rhetorical mode is polemical and moralised — protocol experimentation is framed as a category of expropriation, spending other people's wealth via a captured network effect. He writes weeks before the BCH/BSV split, positioning a stable, unchanging base protocol as the only honest option.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the layered-scaling orthodoxy that changes and new layers "will not cost anything": Craig insists every alteration of the base protocol carries a real uncertainty cost paid by all users, not just its proponents.
- Contradicts the legitimacy of Core (and Bitcoin ABC) as protocol stewards: their experimentation is recast as free-riding on a network effect built with other people's money — "Likely Yours."
- Inverts the era's "toxic maximalist" narrative around chain splits: the honest path for dissenters is to split and compete (Ethereum is the praised example), not to capture and modify the existing network.
- Implicitly rejects governance-by-developer consensus: since no group's subjective valuations outrank another's, developer committees have no more right to set protocol direction than regulators do to set tax priorities.
