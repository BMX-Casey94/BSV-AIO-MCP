# Who stays in the mining pool?
**Date:** 2026-10-09 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/who-stays-in-the-mining-pool | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a mining pool can coordinate computing power supplied by many independent businesses, and that assessing what the pool could sustain means following the contributors as well as the coordinator: what keeps them there, what could make them leave, and how quickly their decisions can change the capacity available. It presents this as an explanatory account of the author’s published paper *Legal Deterrence in ‘Permissionless’ Consensus*, which extends a protocol-only security benchmark by asking how a pool retains contributed hash power and when an identifiable operator faces consequences outside the protocol. For a double-spend scenario, the article says an analysis needs both the resources initially available and the conditions under which those resources remain committed.

## Key arguments and claims
- The model, as the article describes it, distinguishes computing power owned by the pool operator from power allocated by outside providers. The operator coordinates block production; contributors decide whether the return from remaining compares favourably with the return from switching. That is called an ongoing participation condition. A pool’s nominal size adds those resources together. Its effective retained capacity also accounts for contributor exit. The difference is said to matter when the activity that benefits a coordinator worsens the terms on which other businesses are willing to participate.
- Identifying the decision-maker is treated as part of specifying the security model. A pool’s public identity, its own equipment and the equipment supplied by its members are said to describe different things. Attribution of a coordination business does not, on this account, establish where every contributing machine is located or prove a particular contributor’s knowledge of misconduct.
- Two exit channels are set out. The first concerns earnings: diverting resources into a hidden activity can reduce or delay ordinary payouts, and a contributor observing worse performance may move to another pool without knowing the cause. The mechanism depends on the attractiveness and availability of that outside option. The second concerns expected liability and capital risk: once suspected misconduct becomes visible, remaining with the pool may expose a provider to additional contractual, legal or commercial losses. In the model, that expected exposure can make departure worthwhile even where the immediate payout comparison alone would have favoured staying.
- The article says these channels require different evidence. Payout records and switching behaviour help investigate the first. The second also requires evidence about attribution, applicable duties, the provider’s conduct and the consequences it actually expects. Participation in a pool does not, by itself, establish legal liability. The paper is said to supply an incentive framework within which those questions can be examined.
- The legal term in the paper is said to depend on detection, attribution, effective sanction and the cost of pursuing enforcement. It is set to zero where the model’s expected benefit from enforcement fails to cover that cost. A legal remedy can exist while invoking it remains uneconomic in the circumstances being modelled. Where external enforcement has no expected effect, security must be assessed using the remaining mechanisms. Where a sufficiently valuable, attributable claim makes enforcement worthwhile, the expected consequences enter the calculation.
- The paper is said to illustrate that boundary with alternative parameter combinations. Its resulting transaction-value thresholds range from about US$1.05 million to US$4.21 million. The article says those figures are conditional calculations, not statutory thresholds or measured points at which authorities begin to act, and that different enforcement costs, attribution probabilities or available sanctions change the result. The practical research question is stated as which actor can take which action, on what evidence, at what expected cost and within what relevant period. The answer may vary across organisations and jurisdictions.
- The member-exit simulation, as the article reports it, begins with a pool supplying 252 exahashes per second, of which 25 are assumed to be proprietary. Outside providers supply the remainder. The experiment assigns contributors differing sensitivities to payout deterioration, then introduces legal and capital-risk exposure and network friction. Under the full illustrative specification with a quadratic exit function, mean effective pool capacity after 24 hours is 67.5 exahashes per second, or 26.8 per cent of its starting level. The experiment reports 1,000 Monte Carlo replications and compares alternative exit functions. The article says it shows the behaviour of a specified mechanism under declared choices.
- Several choices are called explicitly parametric: the proprietary share, the number and size distribution of contributors, their exit response, the observation window, the detection lag and the diversion schedule. The reported paths therefore do not, on the article’s account, establish how an actual pool would respond, or how quickly a real attack would fail.
- The starting pool is 31.5 per cent of the simulation’s illustrative network. The article says 26.8 per cent retained means a fraction of that pool’s original capacity, and is not a network-wide share. A majority-attack assessment must separately specify the attacking coalition, the honest remainder and how switching changes each side.
- The next empirical task named is to measure the participation relationship: which resources are proprietary; how contributors observe performance; what contracts and switching costs constrain them; and how a documented change in expected exposure alters their behaviour. Those measurements would help distinguish a plausible channel from a calibrated account of its strength. Protocol rules are said to determine how computing work contributes to a chain; contracts and commercial alternatives help determine who continues supplying that work; enforcement affects expected payoffs only where attribution and institutions make it credible.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He separates the coordinator from contributed capacity, then gives contributors two reasons to leave: worse payouts, and expected liability once suspected misconduct is visible. Enforcement is given its own participation condition and is set to zero in the model when the expected benefit does not cover the cost of pursuing it. The simulation is then read with its denominator, its quadratic exit function and its declared parameters, and the page refuses to treat the resulting paths as a measurement of a real pool or of how quickly a real attack would fail. The close turns the mechanism into a list of facts a later study would have to measure.
