# You Don't Own Your Digital Stuff. NFTs Could Actually Fix That — Without Intellectual Property.
**Date:** 2026-02-11 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/you-dont-own-your-digital-stuff-nfts
**Subtitle:** Why the "right-click save" crowd and the IP maximalists are both wrong, and how a 150-year-old economic tradition explains what digital ownership really means.

## Core thesis
Digital purchases today are revocable licences, not ownership — Amazon's 2009 remote deletion of Orwell's 1984 from Kindles was "the system", not a glitch. Drawing on Austrian economics (Menger, Mises, Rothbard, Hoppe) and conceding Stephan Kinsella's anti-IP argument as correct on its own terms, Craig argues that genuine digital property is possible: NFTs cannot make information scarce, but they can make transferable titles to scarce access — and that distinction dissolves both the NFT maximalist and "right-click save" positions.

## Key arguments and claims
- Austrian property theory grounds ownership in scarcity: Hoppe's argument that property norms resolve conflict over rivalrous resources, and Menger's claim that only goods insufficient to satisfy all wants become economic goods. Non-scarce patterns of information need no property institution.
- Kinsella's Against Intellectual Property is accepted as airtight within its framework: copyright and patent are state-granted monopoly privileges that restrict what you may do with your own genuinely scarce physical property (printers, hard drives, vocal cords).
- The hidden error in the "digital ownership is a fantasy" conclusion is conflating the information with the product — the pattern (infinitely copyable bits) versus the access bundle (servers, bandwidth, cryptographic keys, device enclaves, platform accounts), which is genuinely scarce and excludable.
- "Digital scarcity" names four different things: the pattern (non-rivalrous), the token (a cryptographically unique ledger entry), access to a service (enforceable through authentication), and contractual permission (e.g. commercial-use licences). The right-click critique lands only against the first.
- An economically meaningful NFT is a transferable title to a defined bundle of claims — authenticated access, commercial permissions, community membership, future updates, provenance, royalties — exactly as a house deed is paper whose value comes from an enforcing institutional framework.
- The legitimate core of NFT scepticism: most 2021–22 tokens were certificates referencing centrally hosted content with no enforcement linkage — speculative collectibles, not property. The argument applies only to NFTs integrated with enforcement infrastructure (token-gated consumption and production).
- Piracy cannot be technically eliminated (the "analogue hole"), but that is the wrong question: Spotify, Netflix, live-service games and cloud software show authorised access can be made so superior that copying becomes commercially irrelevant — competitive service provision, not prohibition.
- The precise line between property and monopoly privilege: the NFT holder's claim is a positive claim to specific scarce means, not a negative claim against the world's use of non-scarce information. Two conditions keep NFT property compatible with the strongest anti-IP position: claims attach to scarce means, and enforcement is market-produced (cryptography, contract) rather than state-granted.
- The required architecture includes cryptographic key management, token-gated delivery, device-bound rendering (Apple Secure Enclave, ARM TrustZone), and — critically — secondary market synchronisation so access follows the token, avoiding "empty resales".
- NFTs restore alienability to digital goods — resale, gifting, bequest — re-enabling the secondary-market price signals Mises identified as central to the market process. The 2021–22 bubble (a correction exceeding 90 per cent) was a classic Austrian boom-bust that cleared speculative froth while leaving viable applications intact.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Austrian institutional economics applied with philosophical precision: define property functionally (conflict resolution over scarce means), concede the strongest opposing argument (Kinsella), locate the category error (pattern versus access bundle), and derive exact boundary conditions under which token-mediated ownership is genuine property rather than re-badged IP. Market process and spontaneous institutional emergence (Menger) supply the dynamics.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects the crypto-enthusiast claim that token scarcity alone constitutes ownership of content — a token without enforcement infrastructure is a collectible certificate, however high its market price.
- Rejects the sceptic mainstream ("right-click save") as a category error that addresses only the pattern and ignores tokens, access control and contractual permission.
- Rejects the IP-maximalist position shared by much of the legacy content industry: the solution to digital copying is entrepreneurial service bundling, not state-enforced prohibition or mandated DRM — "the difference between 'our platform requires authentication' and 'the government will prosecute you for circumventing authentication' is the difference between contract and coercion."
