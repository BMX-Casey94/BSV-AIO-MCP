# What is Bitcoin….
**Date:** 2018-10-12 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/what-is-bitcoin-8ee9d3e86674
**Subtitle:** Bitcoin is a "crypto-currency", a form of digital or virtual currency using cryptography to control its creation and operation.

## Core thesis
A short definitional primer: Bitcoin is a "crypto-currency" launched in 2009, created and transferred via an open-source cryptographic protocol on a peer-to-peer network, with mining validating transactions into a shared public ledger and a hard cap of 21 million coins. The piece reads as the opening section of a longer legal-academic document (its footnotes are dated "accessed 12 January 2014") and sets up the law-and-tax essays posted in the following days.

## Key arguments and claims
- Definition: Bitcoin is "a form of digital or virtual currency using cryptography to control its creation and operation" — currency framing from the first line, not "digital gold".
- Launched 2009 "when the Bitcoin algorithm was released online"; the protocol is open-source, "free to download, with users having access to the source code and ability to modify it".
- Validation = mining: the network "updat[es] and archiv[es] transactions in a shared public ledger or log … (known as the 'block chain')", and "new Bitcoin are created with each update to the ledger".
- Each bitcoin "represents an allocation of value which is recorded in the public ledger and exist[s] as a chain of digitally signed transactions" — the ownership-as-signature-chain formulation from the whitepaper.
- Issuance schedule: new coins per ledger update are "halved every few years, such that Bitcoin become more difficult to 'mine' over time"; "the maximum number of Bitcoin that can be mined is 21 million", with "over 17 million … estimated to be in circulation" at writing.
- Divisibility: a bitcoin can be divided "up to 8 decimal points — a unit known as a 'satoshi'", and those sub-units are transferable.
- Sourcing is institutional rather than crypto-native: the US GAO's May 2013 report (GAO-13-516) and The Economist's 2013 explainer are the authorities cited.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Textbook-expository mode with legal footnoting: define the system neutrally, anchor each factual claim (mining, cap, halving) to a citable external authority, and build a vocabulary (crypto-currency, block chain, satoshi) for later legal analysis. The 2014 access dates show he is re-presenting earlier academic writing, positioning himself as having documented Bitcoin's mechanics early.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- No polemical engagement, but the framing choices are loaded for October 2018: Bitcoin is defined as a currency and payment system ("creation and transfer", divisibility to the satoshi) at the height of BTC's "store of value, not medium of exchange" turn.
- Describing the software as open-source with users having the "ability to modify it", while emphasising a fixed algorithmic issuance, quietly sets up his later distinction between the protocol (fixed) and implementations (changeable) — the reverse of the "code is law / consensus can change anything" mainstream view.
