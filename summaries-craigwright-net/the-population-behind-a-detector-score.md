# The population behind a detector score
**Date:** 2026-10-23 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/the-population-behind-a-detector-score | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a detector can perform well on one collection and badly on another, so the research question is whether its validation travels to the language, task and population in which it will be used. Recognising generated prose and establishing prohibited assistance are different targets. Neither target, the article says, becomes clearer merely because the interface produces a percentage.

## Key arguments and claims
- Two departments buy the same text detector. One screens short examination essays; the other screens technical reports written in several languages. They adopt the same threshold. The article says that even if the software has not changed, the evidential meaning of a flag may have changed considerably.
- Craig Wright (2026), *Transcription is not generation: distinguishing non-generative AI tool use from academic misconduct in higher education assessment*, *International Journal for Educational Integrity*, 22, article 24, published 20 August 2026. The article links to https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40979-026-00234-w. The sources box dates this commentary 23 October 2026. The article says the journal article asks institutions to distinguish the operation a tool performed from the intellectual work an assessment measures, and that this distinction creates a second question: what, exactly, was the detector validated to identify?
- Weixin Liang, Mert Yuksekgonul, Yining Mao, Eric Wu and James Zou (2023), *GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers*, *Patterns*, 4(7), 100779; the article links to DOI 10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779. As the article reports that study: seven detectors compared on 91 TOEFL essays and 88 essays by US eighth-grade students; an average false-positive rate of 61.3% for the TOEFL collection, compared with 5.1% for the US collection. The article says the result identifies a serious failure in those tested comparisons and is not an estimate for every non-native writer, every assessment or every later detector.
- Adnan Al Ali, Jindřich Helcl and Jindřich Libovický (2026), *Different Time, Different Language: Revisiting the Bias Against Non-Native Speakers in GPT Detectors*, *Proceedings of the 19th Conference of the European Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics: Student Research Workshop*, pp. 277–291; the article links to https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-srw.20/. As the article reports that paper: Czech-language texts and three detector families; no systematic bias against non-native speakers in its tested setting; their texts do not have the lower perplexity observed in the earlier English-language study; the authors also examine performance across domains. In its separate English check, the commercial detector flags 23.1% of the TOEFL essays and none of the Hewlett student essays as generated (Table 5). The article says the Czech finding is not a declaration that bias has disappeared, and that the two results should not be converted into rival slogans. Language, proficiency, text genre, training data, software version and threshold are parts of the comparison; changing several of them changes the question being answered.
- Explicitly hypothetical screening exercise, which the article says is not an estimate of student behaviour. There are 10,000 texts, of which 100 are wholly generated. A detector flags 80% of generated texts and 1% of human texts: 80 generated texts and 99 human texts, so only about 44.7% of the 179 flagged texts would belong to the generated category. The same detection rate and population, with the false-positive rate raised to 5%: 495 flagged human texts, and generated texts about 13.9% of the 575 flags. The article says neither calculation estimates misconduct; the generated-text category does not encode the institution’s permission rules, and the assumed prevalence is invented. Sensitivity, false-positive rate and the composition of the screened population answer different questions. A balanced test collection can be useful for measurement without representing the prevalence in ordinary submissions.
- The article’s proposed use-validation: begin with a declared assessment, language, type of text and an explicit rule about permitted assistance; use examples whose provenance is independently established. Treating every unflagged submission as human-written, or every flagged submission as generated, would build the detector’s own decisions into its supposed validation. Wholly generated answers, human-authored work with faithful transcription, substantive machine revision and permitted language support need not be collapsed into one category. For each relevant group, record the tool version, threshold, text length, sample size and uncertainty around error rates. Where samples are too small, say that the comparison is unresolved. Pooling can conceal a difference; splitting too finely can produce unstable estimates. Both choices need an explanation.
- A flag may prompt closer examination of contemporaneous notes, original calculations, intermediate versions, disclosed assistance and the student’s explanation of the reasoning. The article says those records also have limitations and are not an automatic certificate of authorship. A faithful conversion can preserve already-authored reasoning; a generated addition can change it. A detector trained on final prose does not necessarily observe that transition, so performance on a text-classification task cannot replace evidence about the actual workflow.
- A proposed study, which the article says is not a reported trial: test whether panels using declared workflow evidence make more accurate and consistent decisions than panels given a detector score alone; measure time and burden as well as errors; examine whether results survive a change in language or assessment. The sources box says the screening counts are hypothetical arithmetic, the evaluation design is a proposal, the comparison does not certify any current commercial detector, and no private correspondence or unpublished manuscript is used.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The article sets two departments against one shared threshold, then places two published detector comparisons side by side and refuses to treat them as rival slogans because the populations, languages and tools differ. A base-rate arithmetic, labelled as invented, shows how the share of generated texts among flags moves when the false-positive rate changes. The rest of the argument specifies what a validation would have to declare, keep distinct and count, and it separates a prose-classification score from evidence of the workflow that produced the work. The panel study is offered as a proposal.
