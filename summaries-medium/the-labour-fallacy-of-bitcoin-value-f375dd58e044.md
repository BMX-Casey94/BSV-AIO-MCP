# The Labour Fallacy of Bitcoin Value
**Date:** 2018-10-05 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/the-labour-fallacy-of-bitcoin-value-f375dd58e044
**Subtitle:** The error made in the argument that free movements of people will "take our jobs" is that of a fixed labour pie.

## Core thesis
The "fixed labour pie" behind anti-immigration rhetoric and the labour theory of value behind "mining energy gives Bitcoin its price" are the same fallacy: value comes from subjective demand and use, not from the labour or energy expended in production. Applied to crypto, this makes BTC — whose narrative rests on sunk energy and speculative scarcity — "a speculative gambling asset, a Ponzi", while Bitcoin Cash has value "through use" as P2P electronic cash that solved double spending. The essay doubles as a free-market polemic against regulation, which Craig casts as rent-seeking that entrenches incumbents and expels small innovators.

## Key arguments and claims
- The lump-of-labour fallacy: "There is an infinite amount of labour. There is always more to be done (and that is productive work and not building pyramids and holes to be filled)" — jobs are not a fixed pie to be divided, so migrants do not "take our jobs".
- "The secret is not to distribute labour, but to allow it to flourish competitively."
- Regulation (as opposed to "emergent rules in markets") "imposes additional barriers to entry. It makes any business more capital intensive. The end result being fewer but larger companies and corporations. It forces the small entrepreneur from the market."
- Small entrepreneurial firms are disproportionately the innovators — "a necessary condition to compete with the larger entrenched player. You create a better mousetrap or a more cost-effective process."
- Regulations are rigid and reactionary: "They change after an event… they do not bend but stay rigid to the point where they break", unlike fluid emergent market rules and the common law; the effect is "to maintain the status quo and slow the process of creative destruction".
- Incumbent businessmen support this because they hold the entrenched position — "This is the state of the US car industry and many of the banks", whose loss-making practices lead to future bailouts: "This is rent-seeking behaviour."
- The "superior technology loses" trope is itself a fallacy: Beta was only better than VHS "for a small section of the community" (VHS won on recording time for films and sport), and "the touted superiority of the alternatives to the qwerty keyboard all failed to be demonstrated in independent experiments".
- His own position: "I am heart and soul a free market proponent as over time I have not seen any intervention that provided a net benefit in the long term."
- The core crypto claim: "Many say that Bitcoin is valuable as it has a lot of energy used in the creation of a block, but this, as with all Marxian fallacies, is flawed in that it is not the creation of a thing that adds value, but the subjective demand for it."
- The CEO-wine reductio: hiring expensive CEOs to make wine by 14th-century methods expends much and produces something "few if any would desire" — likewise "we can spend large amounts making a system that is not valuable and will become a Ponzi. In the case of Bitcoin Core (BTC), we have a speculative gambling asset, a Ponzi that is designed to pump until, well, it doesn't."
- The positive thesis: "So, why does Bitcoin (Cash) have value? It has value through use. Bitcoin (BCH) is a P2P cash system, and it — simply put — solved the problem of double spending. It does not save the world, it is a system of money that is difficult to demure."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Austrian-school subjectivist economics applied by analogy: he identifies a shared structure between two fallacies (fixed labour pie; labour/energy theory of value), refutes each with reductio (pyramids and holes; CEO-made medieval wine), and appeals to revealed market preference (VHS over Beta, QWERTY's rivals failing independent tests) as the arbiter of value. The rhetorical mode is polemical — "Marxian fallacies", "rent-seeking", "Ponzi" — grounded in a stated methodological commitment: long-run net-benefit analysis of intervention versus markets.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Directly contradicts the BTC "energy-backed value" / "proof-of-work anchors value" narrative (e.g. the cost-of-production and "stored energy" theories): energy expenditure is irrelevant to value, which is subjective and use-derived.
- Contradicts the digital-gold/store-of-value-first thesis: value comes from use as cash — a medium-of-exchange-first position that inverts the BTC "hodl" orthodoxy.
- Names BTC "a speculative gambling asset, a Ponzi that is designed to pump until, well, it doesn't" — an explicit rejection of the scarcity-plus-number-go-up investment case.
- Contradicts the crypto-regulation consensus in both directions: he is more anti-regulation than the mainstream (regulation as incumbent protection), yet his anti-speculation stance also opposes the laissez-faire speculation culture — the divergence is framed as economics, not compliance.
