# Nobody Asks Where Your Banknote Has Been
**Date:** 2026-07-06 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/nobody-asks-where-your-banknote-has
**Subtitle:** Cash as an institution of bounded bearer finality: how a lawsuit over a stolen banknote in 1758 built the most successful settlement technology in history, and why the law that made it also limits it

## Core thesis
Cash is an institution of bounded bearer finality: a legal arrangement that manufactures a zone in which strangers settle without producing any information about where the money came from, and that withdraws its protection, by specific mechanisms, where the social cost of that ignorance becomes intolerable. The folk picture — finality as a dial that fades smoothly as the sum rises — is false. Title finality is categorical (it holds wherever the currency rule's conditions are met, at any amount); institutional finality is probabilistic (it erodes with notice, abnormality, the regulatory and forfeiture overlay, enforcement capacity and sovereign power).

## Key arguments and claims
- Cash is not an object or a technology but an institution: a constitutive rule in Searle's sense — delivery of a token, for value, to a good-faith taker counts as the creation of a fresh, clean title. On Ménard and Martino's scheme it is a meso-institution between the macro monetary order and the micro transaction.
- The Alchian–Kocherlakota dispute is resolved by division of mechanism: Kocherlakota (1998) showed money is memory, but bearer cash preserves only the quantity signal and erases every other dimension — "memory of quantity and amnesia of provenance". The amnesia is constitutive: "A provenance-bearing banknote is not a better banknote; it is not a banknote."
- Gorton and Pennacchi / Gorton and Ordoñez: debt circulates because it is information-insensitive; attaching a provenance record to a bearer token makes it information-sensitive by construction — "to build the crisis into the design".
- The genuineness/provenance asymmetry: the institution never waives verification that the token is genuine (a counterfeit passes nothing, at any amount); it economises only on provenance, and only below the bound.
- Against Crawford (2025): registers individuate, and individuation is what money cannot survive — money is "the asset the law has deliberately made unregisterable".
- The doctrine: Miller v Race (1758) settled that a good-faith taker for value acquires good title to a stolen banknote; Fox named the attribute "the currency of money". Lipkin Gorman v Karpnale (1991) shows the pivot is value given, not magnitude — the casino lost because void gaming contracts made it a volunteer.
- One word, four finalities: title finality (clean title against prior claimants), obligation finality (discharge of the debt), operational finality (non-reversal within the system), and institutional finality (whether a court or the state later restores the value).
- High value works through three channels, none title-destroying: evidentially (an unusual all-cash transfer bears on notice), regulatorily (Money Laundering Regulations 2017 duties bind independently of title), and by forfeiture (Proceeds of Crime Act 2002, whose s. 308 reproduces the 1758 protection condition for condition).
- The bound is a pivot, not a slope, and it is dynamic: Knightian uncertainty (via Clarke) explains why the shopkeeper cannot price provenance risk and so economises by not asking.
- Code is not title: the GENIUS Act of 2025 made payment stablecoins redeemable claims on regulated issuers (paired with Executive Order 14178 against a retail CBDC); China's e-CNY framework (effective 1 January 2026) classifies wallet balances as deposit liabilities; the digital euro and digital pound write the bound in via holding caps. Giraudo's "legal bubble" names the error of expecting technical control to be ratified as property.
- Rogoff's case against large notes is absorbed, not refuted: it is a calibration argument about where the bound should fall. Falsifiers are stated: value-based title destruction, equal protection for volunteers, forfeiture reaching the good-faith purchaser, value-indifferent AML, or courts treating ledger depth as self-executing title would each break the theory.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Institutional economics in the Hodgson–Searle–Commons tradition, fused with transaction-cost reasoning (Coase, Williamson) and doctrinal legal analysis. The method is anatomical: separate four senses of finality, assign each to the institutional layer that fixes it, then test against statutes, cases and recent legislation — with an explicit "How this could be wrong" section.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against "code is law": operational irreversibility is neither title finality nor institutional finality; "Technical control is not legal title", and courts act on the person (repayment orders, constructive trusts) while the ledger entry stands.
- Against "blockchain payments are final": the sentence is not a proposition but a question — final in which of the four senses?
- Against token-as-bearer-cash claims: permissionless tokens achieve strong operational finality only; the provenance inquiry the protocol refuses reappears at exchanges and custodians — "The inquiry itself is conserved."
- Legislatures have "conspicuously declined" to place any electronic instrument at the bearer end; every recent framework decided against unbounded electronic bearer finality.
