# The scams in Crypto
**Date:** 2018-10-30 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/the-scams-in-crypto-376e327df2af
**Subtitle:** First, Bitcoin is not based on encryption is uses ECDSA, a digital signature algorithm that sends data as plain text, this is unencrypted…

## Core thesis
ICOs are not innovations but "old frauds and shams in new bottles": unregistered securities offerings that bypass the law "ILLEGALLY and fraudulently". The "utility token" label is a sham whenever the token is traded on an exchange, and the crypto culture of promoting one's holdings — "HODL", "buy the dip" — is legally cognisable as scalping fraud under the US Investment Advisers Act of 1940. Blockchain settlement changes nothing about the legal character of a sale, and no ICO is meaningfully "decentralised".

## Key arguments and claims
- An ICO is simply "a means to engage in a securities offering that bypasses the law" — "A promoter or issuer sells to a purchaser. That is all that matters — not the settlement method."
- Tokenised securities are decades old: "The use of a computer token to represent a security goes back to the 60's." Blockchain adds no novelty that matters legally.
- The utility-token test is tradeability: "To be a utility token means that it is not going to be traded on an exchange. A bus ticket is a case of something that can be a 'utility token'. A movie ticket is a 'utility token'." Anything traded for profit is a security.
- Scalping is defined as purchasing a security — explicitly including "tokens, alt-coins, and any ICO offerings" — before recommending it for long-term investment, then selling into the rise; in the US it is prosecuted under the Advisers Act.
- He anchors the doctrine in SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau (1963), quoting Justice Goldberg at length: scalping "operates as a fraud or deceit upon any client or prospective client" under s 206, and the Court read the Act widely to foster full disclosure and business ethics.
- The sting: "even inadequate disclosure of scalping establishes fraud" — true "where no material misstatements had been issued by the promoter".
- Crypto translation: "In the Bitcoin world, this is known as promoting to HODL" — an adviser buys "the dip", urges others to buy, aiming to sell at a profit; the aim to sell, not realised profit, is what matters.
- He immunises himself from the scammer label: "the difference: I am not trying to get any of you money."
- Against the decentralisation defence: "Bitcoin is an exchange from one party to another. The settlement method, the blockchain, is distributed. An exchange is not decentralised" — legally identical to an 18th-century share sale, and "courts do not care about how you exchange".
- Honest tokens are possible: "Digital security tokens can be created. They are bonds, equities, and other securities" sold without deceiving purchasers.
- Technical side note: "Bitcoin is not based on encryption; it uses ECDSA, a digital-signature algorithm that sends data as plain text — this is unencrypted."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Wright argues doctrinally from a leading US Supreme Court authority, quoting the opinion and statutory text (Advisers Act ss 206, 207) at length before mapping each element onto crypto practice. Layered on top is genealogical debunking — 1960s computer tokens, 1990s internet fraud — to strip ICOs of any novelty defence. The rhetorical mode is prosecutorial and self-consciously whistle-blowing: he positions himself as the disinterested expert ("I am not trying to get any of you money") against a community of promoters.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the 2017–18 ICO orthodoxy that "utility tokens" fall outside securities law: if it trades on an exchange, it is a security, full stop.
- Contradicts the "decentralisation as legal shield" narrative: "no ICO is decentralised", and settlement method is legally irrelevant to the character of a sale.
- Contradicts HODL/promotion culture endemic to crypto social media: buying first and exhorting others to hold is scalping — fraud the SEC seeks to "wipe out".
- Contradicts the lay crypto claim that Bitcoin is "encrypted" and hence outside legal reach: ECDSA signs plaintext, and courts reach the parties regardless.
