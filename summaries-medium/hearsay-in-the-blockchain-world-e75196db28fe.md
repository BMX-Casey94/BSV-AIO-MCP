# Hearsay in the Blockchain world
**Date:** 2018-10-24 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/hearsay-in-the-blockchain-world-e75196db28fe
**Subtitle:** In law, when presenting evidence to a court, there exists a rule on the exclusion of hearsay as evidence.

## Core thesis
The hearsay exclusion rule will be dissolved by the Internet of Things. Because machine-generated records from automated processes are not hearsay and are admissible, a world of correlated, self-recording devices — heading toward "100 billion nodes online at any time" — will produce evidence trails courts can rely on directly. "The future of the courts is one of digital evidence"; the only remaining choice is whether ubiquitous recording is shaped "for the betterment of society" or lets "little brother take the place of big".

## Key arguments and claims
- Hearsay defined (Hoey, 1996): "a statement in court repeats a statement made out of court in order to prove the truth of the content of the out-of-court statement" — so an emailed confession still requires other evidence to prove the fact.
- Digital forensics is governed by the scientific-evidence standard of Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579 (1993): testability of the technique; maintained standards with known error rates; peer review and publication; and "general acceptance" in the relevant scientific community.
- Despite error rates not being established for most forensic tools, electronic evidence has generally been accepted; best practice remains peer review and having a second investigator replicate findings with multiple tools for "reliability and repeatability".
- The IoT tipping point has passed: automated devices and servers already outnumber human internet users, and with IP addressing reaching light bulbs and FMCGs, "we could see 100 billion nodes online at any time" — all interacting and correlatable.
- Correlation is already demonstrable: Skype IP addresses have been correlated with BitTorrent P2P traffic; future purchases will be matched and items used to trace movements.
- The Coke-can scenario: an RFID/IP-tagged can bought with a Visa "Smart wave" chip card at a smart checkout links the can's IP to the card ID; the can later turns up in a bin outside premises the buyer denied visiting — "All of this adds together to form an evidence trail."
- The legal crux: "An email were you stated that you did something may not be admissible, but the business and automated processes surrounding devices are admissible. These are not hearsay." Automated capture removes the hearsay problem because the record is produced by a process, not a person.
- Normative close: ubiquitous cameras and microphones are unstoppable — "the tide has swept in so far that there is no stopping it" — so the task is to make the change work for society's betterment rather than "allow little brother to take the place of big".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal law fused with forensic-practitioner epistemology and futurist extrapolation. The decisive move is categorical: machine-generated records fall outside the hearsay definition, so the proliferation of recording devices mechanically expands the domain of admissible evidence — no new statute required. The rhetorical mode shifts mid-essay from legal memorandum (Daubert factors enumerated) to Orwell-inflected warning, ending on an ethical charge rather than a legal rule.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against crypto-anonymity orthodoxy: correlated, automated records make attribution inevitable, and Craig treats this as an evidentiary opportunity rather than a threat — pseudonymity is a wasting asset.
- Against "trustless means courtless": the endpoint of automated, immutable recording is courtroom evidence; the ledger and the IoT serve state legal process.
- Against the 2018 privacy-tech mainstream (CoinJoin, censorship resistance): total traceability is framed as the future of justice, not a surveillance failure.
- Note: despite the title, the essay is more IoT-forensics than blockchain-specific; the blockchain connection is the admissibility of automatically generated ledger records as non-hearsay business records.
