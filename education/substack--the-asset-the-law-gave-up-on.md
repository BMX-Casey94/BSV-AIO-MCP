# The Asset the Law Gave Up On — core principles

- **Money is the vanishing point of the good-faith-purchase problem.** As identifiability falls, the owner’s incentive to search, the buyer’s incentive to verify, and the deterrent value of any title rule fall with it. At perfect fungibility the efficient rule is fresh title for the honest taker for value — finality.
- **Fungibility in law is a commitment, not just a fact.** Coin had no earmark; when earmarked paper arrived, *Miller v Race* refused *nemo dat* anyway, “on account of the currency of it”. Finality is an active legal decision to treat identifiability as zero even when technically positive.
- **Store-of-value systems verify the object; account-based systems verify identity.** Money is memory made portable. A store-of-value instrument whose acceptability depends on provenance is a self-contradiction.
- **Deterrence of theft does not vanish from the world of money.** As per-transaction recovery becomes infinitely costly, deterrence migrates into the identity-bearing institutions of the account layer. It is vertically integrated into the payments system.
- **Re-engineering money’s identifiability upward re-imports the burdens the currency rule was built to eliminate.** Verification costs, discounts and finality doubts return. The system runs on the extinguishment of prior claims.
- **High-identifiability assets belong on registers; money does not.** A Renoir or a documented classic car pays for search. The stolen laptop among ten million identical units does not. Title rules that do not bite explain why money is the asset the law gave up on.
