# Tax and Bitcoin — Transacting and accounting for Bitcoin
**Date:** 2018-11-02 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/tax-and-bitcoin-transacting-and-accounting-for-bitcoin-f858631f0a89
**Subtitle:** Just with all income and capital taxation issues, Bitcoin based transactions require that suitable record keeping practices are adhered to.

## Core thesis
Bitcoin transacting belongs inside ordinary business record-keeping: because every transaction is public and permanent on the blockchain, businesses should log payer/payee addresses, tax identifiers and transaction IDs alongside the fiat market rate, and submit tax invoices on that basis. The essay's legal conclusion is that "bitcoin is 'money' within the ordinary meaning, and should be treated as money for tax purposes" — giving taxpayers certainty and consistent treatment whatever form of money is used.

## Key arguments and claims
- The blockchain as journal: "Bitcoin transactions are public, and a permanent copy of the transaction is reported in the blockchain and can be viewed publically" — so records should capture payer Bitcoin address, payer TFN/ABN or other identifying information, payee address, payee identifiers, and the Bitcoin transaction ID.
- Worked example on a real transaction (`a508436f…c10e`): address `1ASWXajv…` sent 29.9999 BTC at a market rate of $2,049.00 to `1CmdM12H…`; "Coupled with the market rate for bitcoin in $AUD, the business could submit a tax invoice based on the transaction." The transaction is public "but the payer and payee are not disclosed publically" — record-keeping bridges that gap for audit.
- Trading records should mirror a stock or forex brokerage statement: "date of trade, description of trade, qty & price, and fees."
- Mining: revenue treatment determines when proceeds are recognised as attained, and "all expenses associated with mining needs to be recorded… in concord with normal business accounting practices".
- Commerce: whatever the revenue-recognition method, keep "reference to sales (e.g. cust # / invoice #), amount received (in bitcoins), and date"; where GST is payable, use "a weighted average based exchange rate that existed at the time of sale".
- Payroll: calculate in the functional currency (e.g. AUD), subtract withholdings, then pay the net in bitcoin at the market exchange rate, recording "the exchange rate value… with the transaction ID and proof of payment".
- The money conclusion: treating bitcoin as money "will provide not only certainty to taxpayers, but also simpler and clearer application of tax laws" and "consistent tax treatment of real-world transactions irrespective of the form of money or currency used".
- Growth stakes: "The resolution of governments' tax treatment of Bitcoin is vital to the growth of the Bitcoin industry in their borders, and the opportunity it presents to their economy."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Wright argues as an accountant-lawyer normalising Bitcoin into existing compliance machinery rather than seeking exemptions for it. The method is analogical: map each business activity (trading, mining, commerce, payroll) onto its established fiat record-keeping practice and show the blockchain supplies a superior evidentiary trail — a public, permanent journal keyed by transaction ID. The legal argument proceeds from ordinary meaning: because bitcoin functions as money in use, tax law should treat it as money, with certainty and neutrality across taxpayers as the policy payoff.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the pseudonymity-as-shield ethos of 2018 crypto culture: far from hiding behind addresses, businesses should voluntarily bind TFN/ABN identity data to addresses in their books — the public ledger is an audit asset, not a privacy tool.
- Contradicts the "crypto is outside the state" posture: the essay presumes full tax liability, GST, withholding and invoicing apply, and frames compliance as the industry's growth path rather than its enemy.
- Contradicts the then-common regulatory framing of bitcoin as property/asset for tax purposes (e.g. the US IRS 2014 position): he argues for treatment as money, which would simplify rather than complicate the law.
