# The Chessboard, the Grain, and the Fee Market That Ate Itself
**Date:** 2026-03-15 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-chessboard-the-grain-and-the
**Subtitle:** On the mathematical certainty of ruin, and the people too fashionable to notice

## Core thesis
On a fixed-capacity chain, price appreciation is not neutral to usability. Because fees are denominated in the coin, every doubling of the coin's price doubles the fiat cost of an unchanged fee; and because appreciation attracts more demand against a fixed block-size ceiling, the satoshi fee itself rises too. These two compounds multiply rather than add, so a celebrated doubling sequence mechanically prices users out of transacting — and "store of value" is merely the redefinition of that failure as success.

## Key arguments and claims
- The chessboard parable supplies exact numbers: over a million grains by square twenty, over a trillion by square forty, and 18,446,744,073,709,551,615 grains by square sixty-four — roughly five hundred years of global rice harvest. Human inability to grasp exponential growth is "a structural limitation", not a character flaw.
- First compound: a fee of 10,000 satoshis costs $0.10 with the coin at $1,000, $0.20 at $2,000, $0.40 at $4,000, and $6.40 at $64,000. The fee "sat perfectly still in its native unit" while its purchasing-power cost doubled with every doubling of the coin.
- Second compound: a higher-priced coin attracts more holders and more transaction demand, which meets a fixed supply of block space, so users outbid each other and the satoshi-denominated fee itself rises.
- The compounds multiply: coin doubles plus satoshi fee doubles means the fiat cost quadruples; three doublings of each yields a sixty-four-fold cost increase. Worked chessboard: square one ($10,000 coin, 10,000-satoshi fee, $1 transaction), square two $4, square three $16, square four $64; by square six a transaction costs over $1,000.
- The holder's net position never improves: portfolio up sixty-four-fold, per-transaction cost up sixty-four-fold — "his ability to use his wealth has not improved by a single cent."
- "Store of value" is named the fallacy of the captured audience: a restaurant that cannot serve food becomes a "dining concept"; a payment system that cannot process payments becomes a "store of value". The thesis requires, as a precondition, that the holder never actually moves the coins.
- The intermediary irony: the holder fled bank fees and gatekeeping and received miners he has never met, a fee auction he does not control, and a block size chosen by developers — he "has exchanged one set of intermediaries for another—and the new ones charge more."
- Detection asymmetry: gains are denominated in the unit holders watch (dollars), costs in the unit they ignore (satoshis), so each celebrated doubling obscures the simultaneous doubling of usage cost.
- The fix grows costlier per square: at square three, increase the block size and let throughput grow with demand; at square eight, a hard fork, a community war and the alienation of every developer with a business model built on fixed scarcity; by square twelve the user base has stratified into institutional settlors and excluded retail, and the institutions have no incentive to reduce fees that protect their position.
- The original specification contained the scaling path — block size could increase, throughput could grow with demand, the fee market could clear at stable prices — and it was abandoned not for technical failure but because "the people who controlled the repository" preferred fixed base-layer scarcity with excess demand routed to secondary systems they would build, operate or fund.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Reductio by arithmetic: take the mainstream's own success metric (the doubling coin price) and mechanically trace its consequences for fees under fixed capacity, using an exact numerical skeleton dressed as satire. A named fallacy then reclassifies the "digital gold" defence as a marketing manoeuvre rather than an economic argument, and a escalating-cost-of-repair schedule turns the block-size controversy into a question of timing.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects the store-of-value thesis outright: it is a consolation prize that functions only while coins are never moved, and collapses the moment the holder transacts.
- Rejects the fee market as a healthy price signal: it is a compounding mechanism whose fiat cost multiplies with the coin's own appreciation, independent of anyone's decisions.
- Rejects small-block conservatism: capacity was designed to grow with demand, and fixed base-layer scarcity is an architectural preference imposed by repository controllers, not a technical necessity.
- Rejects the disintermediation narrative: users swapped banks for miners, auctions and developer gatekeepers — at higher cost.

## Notable quotes
- "The grain on the chessboard is not the fee. The grain on the chessboard is the coin."
- "The two compounds multiply. They do not add."
- "A payment system that cannot process payments becomes a “store of value.”"
- "The chessboard does not require an audience. It merely requires that no one increase the size of the granary."
