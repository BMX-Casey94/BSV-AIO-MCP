# On Decentralisation
**Date:** 2020-02-24 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/on-decentralisation-e761949d7e5c
**Subtitle:** In a recent statement by SEC Commissioner Peirce, it was explained…

## Core thesis
Craig takes SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce's "safe harbour" statement — that a network escapes securities classification only if it is "not controlled and... not reasonably likely to be controlled, or unilaterally changed" by any single group — and argues that the only system meeting this test is Bitcoin as BSV, because its protocol is "set in stone". Every other system (BTC, ETH, BCH/ABC) has developers who alter the protocol, which by Peirce's definition makes them centralised and their tokens securities. He further argues that developer groups like Bitcoin Core are common-law partnerships with joint liability, regardless of whether they turn a profit. The Medium post is an excerpt; the body continues on craigwright.net.

## Key arguments and claims
- Peirce's safe-harbour test defines decentralised as "not controlled and is not reasonably likely to be controlled, or unilaterally changed, by any single person, group of persons, or entities under common control" — Craig reads this as requiring a protocol that is "set in stone and stable".
- "The only decentralised system that fulfils the described condition is Bitcoin, as defined by BSV"; every other system fails because its developers demonstrably change the protocol.
- BTC Core has "radically changed the protocol" and is moving towards Lightning "as a means to increase its value in illicit trade where records can be destroyed"; Ethereum developers "have unilaterally changed the core protocol"; Bitcoin ABC is "continuously engaged in the process of modifying the core protocol".
- Protocol mutability is itself the centralisation vector: developers who can change the base protocol can withhold information, impose "selective lockout of competing groups", and stifle innovation because commercial software-development times are not instantaneous.
- Developer groups are partnerships at common law: they earn income (donations count as income, "developer groups are not charities"), run support websites, hold meetings, and grant hierarchical source-code access — "the failure to make a direct profit from the association does not preclude the existence of a partnership".
- Common-law partners are "jointly and equally liable for the actions of other partners", unlike corporate shareholders with limited liability — a direct legal threat aimed at named Core contributors.
- A token need not pay a dividend to be a security: "nearly 50% of Fortune 500 companies in the USA do not give out dividends, and even Facebook does not provide dividends".
- Conclusion: "Every single system currently in existence other than Bitcoin (BSV) is, by the same definition, not decentralised."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He works by definitional capture: take a regulator's own words, fix one interpretation (decentralisation = protocol stability), then show every rival system fails it by its developers' observable behaviour. The method is legalistic rather than technical — partnership law, securities law, and liability rules are the operative frameworks, with protocol engineering treated as evidence of legal control. Rhetorically he moves from a quoted authority (Peirce) to an exhaustive enumeration of rivals' deviations, ending in an absolutist either/or verdict.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the "rough consensus" / volunteer-developer model: mainstream BTC treats a loose, non-hierarchical contributor set as the essence of decentralisation; Craig argues that same structure is a liable partnership exercising unilateral control.
- Contradicts the "no single point of control" claim of Core: he asserts the ability to ship protocol changes (SegWit, Lightning direction) is itself proof of centralised control, so BTC fails the very test its community invokes against BSV.
- Contradicts the "code is speech / developers aren't issuers" defence: donations are income, websites and meetings are partnership indicia, and tokens issued by a changeable protocol are securities.
- Contradicts the activist-fork ethos (UASF, governance by implementation): any group capable of altering the base protocol disqualifies its system from being decentralised at all.
