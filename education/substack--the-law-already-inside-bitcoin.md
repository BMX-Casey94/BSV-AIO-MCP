# The Law Already Inside Bitcoin — core principles

- **The flow-cost security model assumes a legally untouchable attacker.** The rigorous economic critique — proof-of-work security requires ongoing expenditure proportional to value at risk — is mathematically correct only under its load-bearing phrase "with no rule of law": an anonymous, consequence-free attacker. That assumption is factually wrong for every economically significant transaction today.
- **Mining is an identifiable oligopoly.** As of March 2026 the five largest pools control over 70% of hashrate; Foundry USA and AntPool together account for nearly half; MARA Pool's operator MARA Holdings is NASDAQ-listed with 400,000 disclosed rigs, 53 exahashes per second and a Bitcoin treasury above four billion dollars. Less than 2% of hashrate comes from pools that cannot be publicly identified via coinbase tags, corporate filings or disclosed operators.
- **Jurisdictional concentration makes miners reachable.** US-linked pools hold about 42% of hashrate and China-linked pools about 41%; a majority attack at this scale requires controlling more than 400 exahashes per second sustained — feasible only through the pool layer, where coinbase tags identify the attacking pool and the defrauded counterparty (an exchange with counsel, insurance and regulatory relationships) is exactly who the pool depends on for fiat monetisation.
- **Legal irrelevance scales against transaction value, not with it.** Below a threshold in the low single-digit millions, legal action costs more than recovery; above it, a double spend is wire fraud and computer fraud that prosecutors, insurers and exchanges pursue. Calibration: Binance paid $4.3 billion to the DOJ, FinCEN and OFAC; FTX and Alameda settled with the CFTC for $12.7 billion; BitMEX for $100 million — all for compliance failures, less serious than deliberate fraud.
- **Pooled attacks are structurally fragile.** Contributed hash is mercenary and mobile: a hidden attack degrades payout quality (lower payouts, higher variance, more stale shares), so contributors leave without even detecting the attack; once detection occurs, exit accelerates to avoid taint. Simulations show a pool starting at 31% of hashrate can lose most contributed hash within hours, converging to its small proprietary base.
- **The longest-chain rule is itself the exclusion mechanism.** A failed attack loses everything spent on the secret chain; honest miners need do nothing special while the attacker's coalition bleeds members.
- **Capital specificity converts security from a flow-cost to a stock-cost problem.** A SHA-256 ASIC computes nothing else — excluded from Bitcoin mining it is scrap metal with a power connector. MARA alone discloses over five billion dollars in fleet plus holdings, against a successful double spend netting tens of millions. Stock-cost deterrence was approximately absent in 2012 (rentable hash); it exists now, and the economic model has not caught up.
- **Bitcoin runs two complementary security regimes.** Protocol-only security covers small transactions — the vast majority by count, compatible with high throughput at low per-transaction cost — while a law-and-organisation regime covers large transactions, where expected sanctions, exchange freezes, monetisation friction, reputational destruction, capital impairment and coalition erosion slash attack profitability.
- **Security depends on being embedded in law, not beyond it.** Law and protocol are complements, each covering the range the other cannot; no valuable economic system — banking, securities, insurance, telecoms, the internet — has ever operated outside legal institutions, and Bitcoin is no exception. The miners regulated themselves into visibility by the simple economic logic of pooling, specialisation and scale.
