# Good Title Is Not a Key
**Date:** 2019-08-23 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/good-title-is-not-a-key-7342b6327f
**Subtitle:** Anarchist anti-government and anti-law promoters of Bitcoin will tell you that if you don't have your keys, you don't have your bitcoin…

## Core thesis
The Medium body is an excerpt (with a marked "(…)" truncation) of a longer craigwright.net essay. Craig argues that "not your keys, not your coins" is legally false: possession of a private key is not ownership, because non-repudiation cannot exist in law and property rights are determined by title, not by cryptographic access. Bitcoin was designed to be recoverable through legal tracing, and because mining necessarily concentrates in corporate data centres subject to court orders, the system becomes ever more aligned with law over time.

## Key arguments and claims
- "Keys are not law." Private keys "make access to funds difficult" but "don't prove ownership of property rights, which never was the intention of Bitcoin."
- Non-repudiation is impossible: "Non-repudiation does not exist." Law always admits "error, intoxication, theft, duress", so any act — including signing with a digital key or transferring bitcoin — can be repudiated; coders have chased this "pipe dream" since 2008.
- "Bitcoin will never act outside of the real world. Nothing will." Every system is tied to the real world; "there is a person at the end of every keyboard", and "courts can order people to do acts."
- Car-key analogy: a stolen digital key to your car doesn't transfer ownership of the car; "through actions of tracing and recovery, you are able to use the legal system to recover the asset. Bitcoin is no different."
- "Bitcoin is recoverable." The equitable doctrine of tracing allows recovery of assets; miners and exchanges, as corporations, can form "reciprocal agreements" implementing "the international recovery of assets."
- His 2008–2010 prediction that Bitcoin "would end in server farms" and that not everyone would run a node entails a professional corporate system with "costs and benefits" — one benefit being recovery.
- Miner compliance is economically enforced: a corporate miner defying a court order is "in contempt" and risks losing a "multi-hundred million-dollar investment"; a miner processing a frozen transaction produces a block "that other miners will reject" and loses money.
- "The survival of the fittest miner comes with the one that stays within the law" — criminal-friendly miners are "slowly and inevitably driven away from the market. Such is the Bitcoin that I created in 2008 and released in 2009."
- Jurisdictional practicality: at the time of writing "74.1% of the BTC hash rate is located within the jurisdiction of China", and major exchanges sit in the USA — so freezing orders should be sought across the UK, China, and the USA.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Craig argues from legal doctrine (title vs possession, tracing, contempt, freezing orders) rather than from code, and uses everyday analogies (house and car keys) to separate access from ownership. He then closes the loop with an economic-incentive argument: mining's capital intensity makes miners hostage to courts, so protocol-level enforcement of property law is not a patch but an emergent equilibrium — "a system that becomes more and more aligned with the law over time." Throughout he asserts design intent as Bitcoin's creator ("Such is the Bitcoin that I created").

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Head-on rejection of the "not your keys, not your coins" mantra — the era's defining self-custody orthodoxy — recasting keys as mere access mechanisms subordinate to legal title.
- Contradicts the code-is-law / non-repudiation ideal cherished by smart-contract and crypto-anarchist thought: "non-repudiation cannot exist in law."
- Contradicts the "Bitcoin resists seizure and censorship" narrative: court-ordered freezing and recovery are presented as feasible and designed-in.
- Contradicts the home-node egalitarianism of BTC culture: server-farm mining is the intended and necessary end state, and it is precisely what makes legal enforcement effective.
