# Tax and Bitcoin — Income tax implications of Bitcoin as money
**Date:** 2018-10-31 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/tax-and-bitcoin-income-tax-implications-of-bitcoin-as-money-d29498766d83
**Subtitle:** 1.1 Overview of the income tax system

## Core thesis
A section-by-section application of the Australian Income Tax Assessment Acts to Bitcoin treated as money: mining, holding, using and trading bitcoin all slot into existing income-tax categories — trading stock, ordinary income, CGT assets, foreign-currency realisation and the TOFA regime — with no new law required. Throughout, the operative premise is that "Bitcoin is money (in the form of intangible property) and thus capable of ownership". The piece reads as an extract from a formal tax advice or submission, cross-referencing a companion GST analysis.

## Key arguments and claims
- Framework: Australian residents are taxed on worldwide income; taxable income is assessable income less deductions; assessable income divides into ordinary income (s 6-5 ITAA 1997) and statutory income (s 6-10, e.g. net capital gains under Div 102).
- Mining status is factual: whether a miner carries on a business turns on "the level of investment… intention to make a profit… size and scale of the operations"; a GST "enterprise" would likely also be an income-tax business.
- Hobby mining escapes ordinary income: coins awarded to a hobbyist "will not be income under ordinary concepts" (citing Evans v FC of T (1989) 20 ATR 922 — gambling lacking "system or organisation").
- Business miners hold bitcoin as trading stock under s 70-10(1): "anything produced… or acquired that is held for the purposes of manufacture, sale or exchange" — and since the Commissioner reads "anything" as property capable of ownership, "Bitcoin is money (in the form of intangible property) and thus capable of ownership".
- Trading-stock consequences: mining/acquisition costs deductible under s 8-1; an excess of closing over opening value of coins on hand is assessable (s 70-35(2)), a shortfall deductible (s 70-35(3)); closing value becomes next year's opening value (s 70-40).
- Valuation: each bitcoin on hand valued at cost, market selling value or replacement value (s 70-45(1)); foreign-currency purchases translated under Div 960 — at the acquisition-date rate for cost value, the year-end rate for market/replacement value.
- Spending bitcoin: the recipient includes the amount in assessable income; the payer may deduct under s 8-1 unless the outgoing is capital, private/domestic, or related to exempt income; spending is also a disposal with CGT consequences.
- Trading as a business: coins are trading stock and gains are ordinary income with losses deductible; outside a business, disposal triggers CGT event A1 (s 104-10) — Bitcoin is a CGT asset under s 108-5 as intangible property / chose in action, with s 118-20 preventing double counting.
- Forex translation: s 960-50 requires amounts in "foreign currency" to be translated to AUD, and "Bitcoin arguably already meets that definition (being arguably a 'currency' other than the Australian dollar)"; rates from Bitcoin exchanges, XE or Oanda are acceptable.
- Forex realisation (Div 775): transacting in bitcoin can generate assessable forex realisation gains/losses attributable to a "currency exchange rate effect" — "irrespective of whether or not they trade bitcoin on a bitcoin exchange" — though mere holding in isolation is not an event; volatility makes the exposure significant.
- TOFA (Div 230): bitcoin alone is not a "financial arrangement" (it is not an "arrangement"), but s 230-530 deems foreign currency to be one — so if Bitcoin is "foreign currency" under s 995-1, large taxpayers (aggregated turnover over $100 m) or electing taxpayers must bring gains and losses, including unrealised ones, to revenue account.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The method is orthodox statutory construction in the voice of a professional tax opinion: take each bitcoin activity, classify it under the correct ITAA 1997 provision, and support the classification with case authority (Evans), dictionary definitions (Macquarie on "anything"), and the Commissioner's known views. There is no polemic — the argument is that existing doctrine is already sufficient, which is itself the intervention. Cross-references to a parallel GST analysis ("4.2(a)") show it was carved from a larger structured paper.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the crypto-community folk belief that Bitcoin sits outside or resists taxation: every activity — mining, holding, spending, trading — is already captured by existing provisions, down to unrealised gains under TOFA.
- Contradicts the era's regulatory scepticism that Bitcoin is neither money nor property: his load-bearing premise is that "Bitcoin is money (in the form of intangible property)".
- Contradicts the "crypto is a new asset class needing bespoke rules" position common in 2018 policy debate: ordinary doctrines — trading stock, CGT event A1, forex realisation, TOFA — do the whole job.
