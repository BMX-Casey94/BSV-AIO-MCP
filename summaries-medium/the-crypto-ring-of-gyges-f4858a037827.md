# The crypto-ring of Gyges
**Date:** 2018-09-02 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/the-crypto-ring-of-gyges-f4858a037827
**Subtitle:** Plato covered the problems of true anonymity in The Republic. In this story, he detailed a fictional character called Gyges of Lydia who…

## Core thesis
Plato's ring of Gyges shows that "no man is moral without view": perfect anonymity corrupts absolutely, which is why Bitcoin is pseudonymous rather than anonymous. Craig draws a hard distinction — privacy is transacting without being traced while retaining the ability to prove payment, ownership and source of wealth; anonymity is the absence of accountability and serves only criminals and corrupt governments. Privacy is a wallet-implementation problem, not a protocol change, and the essay closes with the BSV pledge: "Bitcoin is cash and it does not change (BSV)."

## Key arguments and claims
- Plato's Republic 360b–d (Jowett translation) is quoted at length: given invisibility rings, "the actions of the just would be as the actions of the unjust"; a man is just "not willingly… but of necessity, for wherever any one thinks that he can safely be unjust, there he is unjust". H.G. Wells's "The Invisible Man" is cited as the modern retelling.
- Design conclusion: "This is the reason why bitcoin is pseudonymous. Anonymity does not help people become more private for privacy is not the absence of control."
- Privacy vs anonymity at the point of sale: "When you make a purchase at the store, you do not seek to have complete anonymity but rather a level of privacy that allows you to make a purchase without being traced" — while keeping the ability to prove the payment.
- Contract-law argument: "All contractual exchange requires offer and acceptance with consideration. This requires proof of the exchange is available and that cannot be done using a completely anonymous coin." Without proof, a merchant can deny receipt of an overpayment and "trade becomes less likely".
- Fungibility redefined against the privacy-coin usage: "Fungibility is not about an unknown but rather a proof of valid ownership."
- Source-of-wealth compliance as a feature: "All governments have source of wealth laws. With bitcoin you can prove the source of your income and yet remain private to the greater world." Physical cash already works this way — "serial numbers and receipts".
- "The myth of censorship resistance requires the ability to prove where a transaction originated from" — untraceable coins can simply "be stopped and filtered" at exchanges.
- Motive attribution: "Anonymity only helps corrupt governments and criminals… It is not privacy they seek, but the ability to defraud others." Black operations and political slush funds need untraceable money; "It is a corrupt government who needs anonymity most — people need privacy — aka pseudonymity."
- The privacy failure in practice is an implementation bug, not a design flaw: "BIP32 for instance is a hierarchical system that allows the determination of every branch of keys. The problem with bitcoin is not that it is pseudonymous but that the implementations in wallets are poorly configured."
- Therefore privacy "comes from the implementation of a wallet, not a change to the protocol" — and anonymity-seeking protocol changes are rejected outright.
- Closing: "Bitcoin is cash and it does not change (BSV)."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He frames the technical question inside classical moral philosophy (Plato via Jowett, Wells), then splits the contested concept into two — privacy (provable, accountable) versus anonymity (unaccountable) — and shows that commerce and law require the former. Legal doctrine (contract formation, source-of-wealth statutes) supplies the functional tests, and the opposition's position is defeated by motive attribution: anonymity advocacy is recast as advocacy for corruption. The mode is Socratic-moralist with a compliance-positive conclusion unusual in crypto discourse.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the cypherpunk/privacy-coin orthodoxy (Monero, Zcash, CoinJoin culture) that stronger on-chain anonymity is a good: anonymity is reframed as a tool of "corrupt governments and criminals".
- Contradicts the "censorship resistance through untraceability" thesis: traceability and provable origin are what make coins acceptable and unfilterable.
- Contradicts the fungibility-requires-unlinkability argument: fungibility is redefined as "proof of valid ownership".
- Contradicts protocol-level privacy enhancement (a live BTC/L2 debate): privacy belongs in wallet implementation, "not a change to the protocol" — consistent with his immutability stance in "Limited change to bring stability".
- Aligns Bitcoin with audit and legal process (court evidence, receipts, source of wealth) against the anti-state reading of mainstream crypto.
