# A Fundamental Misunderstanding
**Date:** 2019-11-05 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/a-fundamental-misunderstanding-60e788cfcc1
**Subtitle:** A few weeks ago, at CC Forum in London, Bobby Lee asked me to review his new product. In effect, it's a paper wallet printed on a piece of…

## Core thesis
An excerpt (the body continues on craigwright.net) reviewing Bobby Lee's Ballet metal card wallet, which Craig uses as a case study in how the industry misunderstands Bitcoin. His verdict: Ballet is economically and physically insecure (keys extractable for a few dollars), sits in a regulatory dead zone already mapped by 1990s electronic-cash law, and serves no legitimate market — at best ignorance, at worst a money-laundering product. The excerpt ends as he pivots to Bitcoin's real purpose: an electronic cash system built for micropayments.

## Key arguments and claims
- The white paper's own guidance condemns the product's model: "a new key pair should be used for each transaction to keep them from being linked to a common owner" — a single fixed-key bearer card violates Bitcoin's privacy design.
- Physical-security economics doom it: from "having analysed both physical and IT security systems for several decades", he estimates "the cost of counterfeiting and of taking a key and reapplying the protective coating... at bulk would be between two and four US dollars for each card", so attackers could sell cards with pre-extracted keys and recoup any balance over ~$4 later; the card's cost also makes it "infeasible that it will be used for anything under $20".
- Users must additionally "entrust the company developing the product with never saving the keys" — a custodial trust assumption Bitcoin was built to remove.
- Historical precedent: 1990s Mondex prepaid smart cards were "quite secure", allowing non-destructive on/off transfers with well-protected keys; a FATF report from the '90s classified e-cash into "net-around money" (online, e.g. DigiCash's eCash) and "walk-around money" (face-to-face, e.g. Mondex). "Ballet acts in a form that tries to be both and satisfies neither use case."
- Regulatory point: "The type of system is already covered under anti-money laundering (AML) rules" — Mondex's introduction "led to the introduction of laws covering the use of such products 20 years ago".
- Dark suspicion: "I'm starting to come to the belief that individuals seeking to create such products are actively engaged in creating solutions for the purpose and market of money laundering" — yet it fails even there, since "individuals will need to record all of the payments to the card" and the key "can be extracted for as little as two US dollars".
- Charitable fallback: "I seek to try and believe that people are not doing such things maliciously and that they're doing them out of ignorance" — either way it shows "a fundamental misunderstanding and incomprehension as to the nature of Bitcoin".
- The excerpt closes by opening the positive case: "Bitcoin is designed to be an electronic cash system" using digital coins based on a template saved to the blockchain — the micropayments argument continues on his blog.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Practitioner threat-modelling dressed as product review: he prices the attack ($2–4 per key extraction), identifies the trust assumption (manufacturer key retention), and then maps the product onto a 1990s regulatory taxonomy (FATF's net-around vs walk-around money) to show the law already anticipated it. Authority is grounded in the white paper's key-reuse passage and his claimed decades of security analysis; the mode is world-weary expert correcting enthusiastic amateurs.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the bearer-instrument/"be your own bank with cold storage" orthodoxy of the BTC era: a physical Bitcoin bearer card reintroduces custodial trust and single-key linkability — the very things Bitcoin's key-rotation model avoids.
- Contradicts the industry's "hardware wallets are the answer" security culture by pricing physical key extraction and showing the coating is theatre.
- Contradicts the "regulation hasn't caught up with crypto" trope: AML law covered this product category two decades ago, so novelty claims are ignorance of financial-crime history.
