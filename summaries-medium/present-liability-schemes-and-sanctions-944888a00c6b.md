# Present Liability Schemes and Sanctions
**Date:** 2018-11-16 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/present-liability-schemes-and-sanctions-944888a00c6b
**Subtitle:** The actor with the best capability to thwart most forms of internet-related misconduct is the primary malfeasor. The release of malware…

## Core thesis
Drawing on internet-liability scholarship, Craig argues that law targets the actors best placed to stop misconduct — and that in crypto this means the people behind ICOs and "decentralised" exchanges, who remain fully liable regardless of where settlement occurs. His conclusion: "no ICO can be 'decentralised'", and an ICO is simply a security under long-standing law, "not even remotely novel."

## Key arguments and claims
- Cheapest-cost-avoider principle: "The actor with the best capability to thwart most forms of internet-related misconduct is the primary malfeasor" — a worm's creator is best placed to prevent its harm; pornography requires both viewer and commercial site; gambling needs an interactive operator, so "direct control of either of these actors will thwart much of the social harm."
- Direct regulation of end users fails online: actors are often "judgement-proof," transactions are numerous and low-value, so "the direct regulation of individuals… remains ineffective in diminishing undesired conduct" — citing Lemley & Reese and William W. Fisher III's *Promises to Keep* (2004).
- Hence "Internet intermediaries are commonly seen as both the target of opportunity and the answer to judgement-proof actors" — the law routes liability through chokepoints.
- Applied to crypto: "When it comes to a DEX or a 'smart-contract-based exchange', it is again the people in the system; code does not create itself and launch. The result is, no ICO can be 'decentralised' as many try and (falsely) claim."
- Liability follows the creators: "The primary malfeasance is from those who create the ICO. They remain liable."
- Settlement rails are legally irrelevant: "The exchange remains between people, and it is settled on a blockchain. The process of settlement has no impact on the legality of a security."
- ICOs are unoriginal: "an ICO is just a security under law as all securities have been through history — from Pink sheets to web IPOs; ICOs are not even remotely novel."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The method is law-and-economics: import the cheapest-cost-avoider / intermediary-liability framework from internet-regulation scholarship (with footnoted academic sources), then apply it syllogistically to DEXs and ICOs. The key move is to dissolve the "decentralisation" defence by re-centring human agency — "code does not create itself and launch" — so existing securities law attaches unchanged. The register is academic-legal rather than polemical, matching its origins in his liability research.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Demolishes the 2017–18 ICO-era orthodoxy that token sales and DEXs escape securities law through "decentralisation": the claim is "falsely" made, and creators "remain liable."
- Rejects the "code is law / no one controls the smart contract" defence: humans build and launch code, so legal responsibility never disappears into the protocol.
- Contradicts crypto exceptionalism ("tokens are a new asset class needing new law"): ICOs are continuous with "Pink sheets to web IPOs" — old law already applies.
- Aligns with (indeed anticipates) SEC enforcement orthodoxy, positioning Craig against the crypto-libertarian mainstream rather than with it.
