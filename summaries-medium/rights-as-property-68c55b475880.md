## Core thesis
A bitcoin transaction is, at base, a ledger entry reassigning value from the holder of one private key to a new key pair and address — and that simple form maps cleanly onto existing money-transfer and currency rules. The legal difficulty, and the real research agenda, lies in the "exotic use cases": multi-signature arrangements, conditional smart contracts, tokenised real property, theft, and cross-jurisdictional assignment. The post is a short research abstract whose substantive content sits in three dense footnotes on contract formation and jurisdiction.

## Key arguments and claims
- The simple bitcoin transfer — "the creation of an entry in a ledger" assigning value to a new cryptographic key pair — is "not difficult to represent against existing money transfer and currency rules"; existing law already accommodates it.
- The research programme targets five hard areas: transactions to/from multi-signature and multi-party addresses; conditional smart contracts; embedding information on-chain and "the tokenisation of real property and chattel goods"; theft and fraudulent procurement; and international, cross-jurisdictional assignments.
- Offer and acceptance in Bitcoin remain unresolved: "does the transmission of a Bitcoin transaction constitute acceptance, inclusion into 6 blocks, etc?" The software-licence model (loading software indicates acceptance of terms) cannot be ported to Bitcoin because the user has no opportunity to view terms before the act.
- Money-transmission classification is live: FinCEN guidance FIN-2008-G008 (10 Sept 2008) applies the money-transmitter definition to brokers and dealers in currency and commodities, while excluding persons "registered with, and regulated or examined by" the CFTC.
- Cross-jurisdictional contract formation turns on where the wallet is accessed and when receipt occurs: parties may reside in a different state from where the wallet is held (Hyde v Wrench 1840; Treitel 2003), and receipt-time rules differ by jurisdiction (Art. 1335 Italian Civil Code; US Restatement 2d of Contracts §56; Germany RGZ 144, 292).
- For a Bitcoin transaction, receipt occurs "when it arrives on the client's wallet or if it is accepted in the blockchain at an agreed depth" — so a contract may be disputed, or even take effect, "in two or more places" (Apple Corps Limited v Apple Computer, Inc. [2004]).

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal legal analysis: Bitcoin is treated as a new fact-pattern to be slotted into the existing architecture of contract and property law, not as a legal vacuum. The method is issue-spotting by footnote — each research area is flagged, then anchored to a specific authority (a FinCEN guidance number, a named 1840 English case, civil-code articles across Italy, the US and Germany). The rhetorical mode is the legal academic's abstract: compressed, citation-forward, jurisdictionally pluralist.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against "code is law" and the extra-legal settlement narrative: bitcoin transactions are presented as fully cognisable under existing money-transfer, contract-formation and property rules.
- Against the borderless/jurisdiction-free myth: the physical location where a wallet is accessed and divergent national receipt rules mean a single transaction can create legal effects in multiple jurisdictions at once.
- Against confirmation-count orthodoxy: the community's "6 blocks" convention is demoted to one candidate acceptance criterion among several — a question of law to be argued, not a technical given.
- Against tokenisation hype: tokenising real property and chattels is flagged as legally hard (theft, fraudulent procurement, cross-border assignment), not as a solved engineering problem.
