# The Theft That Never Was
**Date:** 2026-04-17 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-theft-that-never-was
**Subtitle:** On Abandonment, Illusion, and the Quiet Rewriting of a System That Was Meant to Endure

## Core thesis
Calling the recovery of dormant Bitcoin "theft" is a conceptual error: theft presupposes an owner with control, and coins whose keys are inaccessible are functionally abandoned value, which may lawfully be reintroduced into use. Proposals to freeze such coins pre-emptively — justified by a quantum-computing threat that does not currently exist — are expropriation: a retroactive rewriting of ownership conditions that would reshape Bitcoin into the anonymous, static, ideologically convenient system it was never designed to be.

## Key arguments and claims
- Theft is a defined legal concept, not "infinitely elastic": it requires the unlawful taking of property belonging to another with intent to deprive, and presumes ownership, control, and "the continued existence of a party capable of being wronged. Remove these elements, and the concept dissolves."
- Ownership in Bitcoin is operational, not a historical label: control is exercised through private keys, without which one cannot spend, move, or signal ownership in any way the system recognises. "A coin without accessible keys is not actively held property. It is, functionally, abandoned value."
- Abandonment is a recognised legal concept across jurisdictions: property voluntarily relinquished or effectively surrendered through inaction changes category. "One does not commit theft by recovering abandoned property. One reintroduces it into use."
- The quantum justification is speculation, not engineering: "There has not been a single logical qubit constructed in the sense required for scalable, fault-tolerant computation. Not one." The leap from narrow proofs of concept to breaking elliptic-curve cryptography "is not incremental—it is categorical. It is the difference between a spark and a star."
- Pre-emptive protocol change on that basis "is not prudence. It is panic dressed in technical language" — akin to "redesigning architecture to withstand storms that have yet to form in skies that do not yet exist".
- Freezing dormant coins renders currently spendable outputs unspendable "not by the actions of their holders, but by the decree of those modifying the protocol. This is not preservation. It is expropriation." Its only authority is social-layer consensus — "none that is inherent to the system itself".
- The anonymity narrative is a retroactive mischaracterisation: Bitcoin operates in clear text, with transactions recorded publicly, permanently and in order; addresses are pseudonymous but flows are visible, analysable and attributable. "This is not a flaw. It is a feature." Bitcoin provides privacy (selective disclosure within accountability), not anonymity (untraceability) — the cypherpunk reading recasts Bitcoin "to match the ideology of those who adopted it rather than those who built it".
- The two views generate opposite conclusions: if Bitcoin is anonymous money, dormant coins must be protected in perpetuity; if it is a system of record, extended inaction raises legitimate questions of control and abandonment. "The former view requires belief. The latter requires only observation."
- Satoshi's coins are no special case: the holder can move them, update security, act. "If they do not, then the system proceeds as designed. It does not pause. It does not speculate. It does not infer intent. It records." Intervening on their behalf overrides the system rather than respecting it.
- The deeper issue is control — who decides what constitutes ownership and when it may be overridden. The proposals mark "a shift from law to discretion. From principle to policy." Bitcoin was built as digital cash, not "a vault for forgotten wealth"; converting it into a repository of permanently frozen coins inverts its purpose.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Definitional jurisprudence combined with engineering scepticism: he fixes the legal meanings of theft, ownership and abandonment, shows the discourse violates them, then dismantles the empirical premise (quantum capability) on engineering grounds. A rhetorical unmasking layer tracks how language is weaponised — "theft" stretched to victimless scenarios, "protection" invoked to remove rights, "security" masking behavioural change — diagnosing the freeze proposal as a narrative device whose real object is control.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- The freeze-dormant-coins proposal is a BTC-ecosystem position (framed around "quantum vulnerable wallets"); Craig rejects it as expropriation and a breach of protocol immutability — the system must not rewrite ownership conditions retroactively, whatever the rationale.
- Mainstream crypto's cypherpunk self-image — Bitcoin as anonymous, concealment-oriented money — is "directly contradicted by the structure of the system itself": a public, ordered, attributable ledger.
- The precautionary principle applied to hypothetical quantum threats is inverted: altering fundamental properties now against a non-existent capability is "caution unmoored from reality".
- The mainstream treatment of Satoshi's coins as symbolically untouchable is an "indulgence"; the system does not infer intent, it records.

## Notable quotes

## Connections
Supplies the property-law foundation for Craig's broader digital-asset-recovery position: if dormant coins are abandoned value, court-ordered recovery (DAR) is lawful reintroduction, not theft. The quantum scepticism recurs across his technical writing, and the privacy-versus-anonymity distinction underpins his claim that Bitcoin was designed for legal accountability — the thread connecting his Satoshi-history and law-regulation essays.
