# Your Token Is Not Your JPEG — And That Distinction Is the Entire Point
**Date:** 2026-03-12 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/your-token-is-not-your-jpeg-and-that
**Subtitle:** On the legal void between recognising digital property and actually protecting it

## Core thesis
Every NFT dispute involves three distinct objects — the off-chain work, the on-chain token, and the allegedly wrongful conduct — and collapsing them produces the confusion saturating the field. China and Singapore have both recognised a proprietary dimension in digital assets, but neither has determined which tortious remedies attach when a token itself is interfered with; the result is a concrete remedial void that must be closed narrowly, protecting token-level control without inflating property remedies to cover every digital economic grievance.

## Key arguments and claims
- The three objects: the work (infinitely reproducible, protected by copyright), the token (an on-chain ledger entry — unique identifier, contract address, owner address — controlled exclusively through the private key, exclusive, rivalrous, self-executing), and the conduct (which may target the work, the token, or neither). The relationship between work and token is referential, not constitutive: owning the token confers no copyright; owning the copyright confers no rights over the token.
- The Hangzhou Internet Court's *Fat Tiger* ruling — the first Chinese NFT decision — characterised the token as having property interests (财产权益) distinct from the artwork's copyright, then resolved the case on copyright grounds and left the property question untouched.
- Recognition without remedy: Singapore's High Court in *CLM v CLN* held cryptocurrencies satisfy the four *Ainsworth* criteria for property, and the Court of Appeal in *Quoine* saw "much to commend" in assimilating crypto into property concepts — yet no Singaporean court has extended the tort of conversion (historically requiring tangibles) to digital tokens, so no conversion claim can currently be brought.
- In China the gap is legislative: Article 127 of the Civil Code delegates "network virtual property" (网络虚拟财产) to future legislation that has never arrived, and the numerus clausus principle makes courts reluctant to create new property-right categories. If a token is not a "thing" (物) under Articles 114–115, holders lose access to the no-fault possessory remedies of Articles 235–237 (return, elimination of obstruction, restoration) and are left with fault-based tort, contract, or copyright.
- Three families of conduct, only one of which engages property-interference liability: passive copying of publicly displayed content (copyright); access-control circumvention such as credential stuffing or API abuse (computer misuse — "wrongful means do not create possessory wrongs"); and token-level interference — unauthorised transfer, freezing, custodial refusal, burning to an inaccessible address — the sole residual category.
- The indirect-interference argument fails: systematic scraping that destroys a token's commercial environment leaves the token on the ledger under the same key-control; reducing market value through competing content is market competition, not property interference.
- Adjacent doctrines fail precisely where token-level interference is most serious: contract requires privity (a hacker has none), unjust enrichment reverses gains rather than vindicating possession, unfair competition protects market order and requires a competitive relationship, equitable tracing follows proceeds not the token, and criminal prosecution punishes without restoring.
- Remedial architectures diverge: China's modular menu (Articles 235–238) permits return without proof of fault, bifurcating possessory relief from damages; Singaporean conversion is all-or-nothing strict liability with a forced-sale measure — the defendant acquires the token on paying its market value, "commercially incoherent" for a hacker, and prone to valuation problems in volatile NFT markets.
- The formalist objections are constitutive, not quaint: numerus clausus fixes property categories by legislation; tangibility is part of conversion's institutional identity. The functional rejoinder is that a key-controlled token burdens third parties no more than a chattel in a locked room, and is more susceptible to possessory control than many physical chattels.
- A five-step routing test, applicable in both jurisdictions: identify the layer; identify the object (Civil Code "thing" / Ainsworth criteria); assess impairment of exclusive control; assess seriousness (fault for damages in China, permanent deprivation for conversion in Singapore); route the claim. The test's most important function is exclusion. The analysis extends beyond NFTs to tokenised securities, DeFi positions, digital identity credentials, and on-chain governance rights.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Comparative doctrinal private law: precise classification of objects and conduct, functional interrogation of formal barriers (asking what each doctrine's rationale actually requires), and honest statement of the opposing formalist case — "the ordinary, rigorous, unglamorous work of doctrinal classification" rather than terror or intoxication at the technology.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
No direct engagement — this post is a comparative private-law analysis of digital-asset remedies in Chinese and Singaporean doctrine, aimed at the crypto-commentariat's layer-confusion rather than at BTC/Core positions.
