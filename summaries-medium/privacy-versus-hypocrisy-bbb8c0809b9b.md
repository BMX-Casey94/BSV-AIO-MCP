# Privacy versus hypocrisy
**Date:** 2019-03-21 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/privacy-versus-hypocrisy-bbb8c0809b9b
**Subtitle:** Many of those arguing for anonymity are truly arguing for power and the control of others. If we take into account the criminal…

## Core thesis
Anonymity campaigners are hypocrites: they claim to fight for freedom while violating the privacy of others, because privacy is the property-like right to exclude — the foundation of markets in information and of all labour rights. Craig then turns the lens on himself: his own privacy has been stripped since 2015, court proceedings will soon force him to prove his role in Bitcoin's creation, and he warns that the resulting disclosures and legal actions will be "painful" for his enemies and for "the sham Bitcoin BTC".

## Key arguments and claims
- The BotFaketoshi Twitter bot bypassing his blocks "represents a crime according to the DMCA"; its operators "argue that they are doing them to seek more privacy, and yet they are willing to violate the privacy of others".
- Free speech is the right to speak in public where people may listen — "not a right to fence the men and make them listen, nor is it a right for an audience"; "free speech is a right to live in a world without trolls."
- Privacy grounds markets: exclusion lets us "set a rate on information" and charge for access, and the rate need not be monetary — "It can be as simple as being polite."
- Lockean labour theory: "We own the rights to all of our labour. The current socialism of the mind is a pernicious attack on the rights of man"; an ox or horse gains nothing from field work — "it is the human mind which creates all value", so attacks on intellectual property are attacks on labour.
- Creative commons culture made social media "the cesspit it is": trolls operate "at the same level as the euridite"; such parties are "Sybils... parties without value", and "It is what Bitcoin cures."
- On his 2015/2016 outing: "at no point did I agree to sign publicly on a web page, and nor does it say so" — a direct rebuttal of the public-signature demand.
- Janus imagery: "I have had many faces and worn many masks... Janus was the god of beginnings, time, and passages; like with Bitcoin, he looked both to the past and the future."
- Court is coming: "I'm going to have to go into court and verify properly my involvement with Bitcoin"; "in the next 12 to 18 months, there will not be much left in secret about the start of the system"; "it was my right to privacy and not your right to know."
- Threatens market consequences: vested interests have been propping up "the sham Bitcoin BTC that has been airdropped due to my failure to help with scaling and not splitting off miners in 2017"; "BTC is not even close to Bitcoin and very simple to stop."
- Organisational menace: "We don't publish patents until we need to"; the organisation "can act with or without me now"; "We will start with one or two and move from there, and doing so will lead to the collapse of the whole Ponzi as the dominoes fall one after another."
- "Decentralisation is about power. The only way to decentralise power is to affix and set the protocol immutably and allow no change. A system that can be altered is not decentralised."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The argument runs on a rights-based, Lockean frame — privacy and IP as labour-derived property — extended to a market logic of exclusion and pricing. He then shifts register from philosophy to memoir to threat, using classical allusion (Janus, Canens' Tiber swan song) and courtroom-destiny narrative. The reasoning is less evidentiary than positional: claims of hidden resources, patents, and multi-decade planning ("I plan and plot over the decades") function as deterrence rather than demonstration.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Cypherpunk orthodoxy treats anonymity/pseudonymity as the core crypto virtue; he recasts it as a power grab and a violation of others' privacy rights.
- The skeptic community's demand that "Satoshi should just sign publicly" is rejected on privacy grounds — he owes the public nothing.
- "Information wants to be free" / creative-commons culture is blamed for degrading discourse; he defends paywalls, exclusion, and IP maximalism.
- Platform-neutrality libertarianism is rejected: platforms may set policy, "but once it does, the enforcement of the rights must be equal", and hosting criminality is not free speech.
- BTC's claim to be Bitcoin is dismissed as an airdropped sham kept alive by vested interests — a direct attack on the 2019 market consensus.
