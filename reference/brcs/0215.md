# BRC-215: Block-Height Deadline Covenant Templates (OP_PUSH_TX)

RexStarBSV (RexStarBSV@protonmail.com)

## Abstract

This BRC defines a deadline gate: a fixed sequence of script opcodes, parameterised by one block height `D`, that lets an output be spent through it only by a transaction that has that one input, whose `nLockTime` is a block height of at least `D`, and whose input's sequence number is `0xFFFFFFFE`, so that lock-time enforcement is in force. The gate reads these from the transaction's own signature preimage, which the unlocking script pushes and the gate authenticates with the Push TX technique of [BRC-21](./0021.md). It refuses the two values that make a bare `nLockTime >= D` comparison worthless: an input sequence number of `0xFFFFFFFF`, which makes the transaction final at once, and an `nLockTime` of 500,000,000 or more, which consensus reads as a Unix time. Consensus then refuses to mine the spend before height `D + 1`. The one-input rule and the one sequence number keep a spend through the gate, broadcast early and held by nodes as non-final, from blocking the other branch's spend until the deadline. Three templates use the gate. Two place it in one branch of a two-branch script, beside a branch that spends at any time: one key now and another key after `D` (a vault's recovery key and its time-gated hot key, an owner and a beneficiary), or `m` of `n` keys now and one key after `D` (a pooled fund that its contributors can reclaim); the third is the gate on its own, for a lock with no early exit. Every byte of the gate and the templates is specified, so a wallet can build them and recognise them, and the test vectors were checked against two interpreters that share no code.

## Motivation

For an output created after the Genesis upgrade, `OP_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY` and `OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY` are `OP_NOP2` and `OP_NOP3`: they enforce nothing, and a node's default policy refuses a script that executes them ([GENESIS]). What remains is the transaction-level lock: a transaction whose `nLockTime` lies in the future, with at least one input whose sequence number is below `0xFFFFFFFF`, cannot be mined until the chain reaches it. That lock serves two parties who sign a transaction together in advance. It cannot serve an asymmetric arrangement, in which one party may spend at any time and another only after a height, because the second party would sign alone, after the fact, and nothing in a plain locking script stops them choosing `nLockTime = 0`.

Push TX ([BRC-21](./0021.md)) closes that gap. A locking script that authenticates the spending transaction's signature preimage can read `nLockTime` and the input's sequence number from it and refuse a spend whose lock does not reach the deadline. BRC-21 describes the technique and names time-based restrictions as one use, and it leaves the script to each implementer. A deadline check written by each implementer invites three errors, none of which fails visibly. The first two turn the deadline off; the third shuts the other branch until the deadline:

1. **The finality bypass.** Consensus ignores `nLockTime` when every input's sequence number is `0xFFFFFFFF`. A gate that compares `nLockTime` with the deadline and stops there is satisfied by a transaction carrying the deadline in `nLockTime` and `0xFFFFFFFF` in its one sequence number. That transaction is final and can be mined at once.
2. **The type confusion.** Consensus reads an `nLockTime` of 500,000,000 or more as a Unix time. A gate that compares `nLockTime >= D` against a block height `D` is satisfied by 500,000,001, a moment in 1985 that has long passed, so the transaction is final at once. [BIP65] avoided this in `OP_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY` by requiring the two values to be of the same type; a gate in script has to state that rule itself.
3. **The pin.** A node holds a transaction that is not yet final in a pool of its own, relays it, and refuses a final transaction that spends one of its inputs unless that transaction spends exactly the same inputs, every one with sequence number `0xFFFFFFFF`, and it charges every such replacement, of the held transaction by one with the same inputs and higher sequence numbers included, to an allowance per held transaction ([NONFINAL]). If the gate allows other inputs, the holder of the after-deadline key can broadcast, long before `D`, a spend through the gate that also spends a coin of their own: every node that holds it refuses the anytime branch's spend, which cannot spend that coin. If the gate allows any non-final sequence number, they can instead keep re-sending a one-input spend with rising sequence numbers until its allowance is spent, and the anytime branch's spend, itself a replacement, is refused while it is. At `D` the held spend becomes final and is mined. The deadline still holds in consensus, and the anytime branch, which exists to act first (a clawback, a check-in), is shut until it has passed.

Other standards already need an output that "cannot be spent before a stated height": [BRC-190](../apps/0190.md) section 4.5 grades a reader by the satoshis they have locked until a height, and [BRC-210](../apps/0210.md) section 7.4 lets a score require a timelocked mark. Neither says which script makes the lock genuine. A shared template, specified to the byte, lets a wallet build such an output, lets an indexer or a client recognise one and read its deadline and keys, and lets every implementation carry the three guards above because the template carries them.

## Scope and conformance

Section 2 specifies the deadline gate, section 3 the three templates that use it (two with an anytime branch, and the gate on its own), section 4 how each branch is spent and when the spend can be mined, sections 5 and 6 how a deadline is moved and which of the gate's two forms to use, and section 7 lists every requirement by role. Out of scope are how the parties exchange keys and deadlines, what the outputs of a spend pay to, the fee rate, and any record that announces a template output to an overlay. The least value a template output needs in order to be spendable at all (section 3.5) is in scope, because an output funded below it is stranded.

The gate enforces two things: that a spend through it has that one input, and that it carries a block-height `nLockTime` of at least `D` and leaves that lock in force. It does not constrain the outputs of the spend, and the branch beside it constrains nothing beyond its signatures. The templates therefore give the after-deadline key a delay and the anytime keys a window in which to act first; they do not stop a holder of the anytime keys from spending at any moment.

The key words MUST, MUST NOT, SHOULD, SHOULD NOT and MAY are to be interpreted as described in RFC 2119 and RFC 8174 when, and only when, they appear in capitals. Three roles are used: a **builder** creates a template output, a **spender** builds a transaction that spends one, and a **reader** recognises template outputs and reports their terms (an indexer, a client checking a BRC-190 timelock, the receiving side of a payment).

## Relationship to existing standards

### Push TX: BRC-21

[BRC-21](./0021.md) standardises the technique: the unlocking script pushes the signature preimage, the locking script computes a signature over it with a key it knows and checks that signature with `OP_CHECKSIG`, which succeeds only if the preimage is the real one. This BRC is one application of that technique, with its script fixed to the byte. Section 2.1 is the Push TX step; sections 2.2 and 2.3 are what this BRC adds.

### Covenants that use Push TX: BRC-226, BRC-171 and BRC-172

[BRC-226](../tokens/0226.md) uses Push TX to make a token re-create itself and pay fixed royalties on every transfer. [BRC-171](../state-machines/0171.md) keeps a covenant's state in its own locking script, and [BRC-172](../state-machines/0172.md) lets a covenant pay its own fees and observes that a covenant has no clock but `nLockTime`. All three constrain what a spend creates. The gate here constrains when a spend can be mined, and it composes with them: a covenant that carries its own state can include the gate in a branch, as the templates of section 3 do.

### An in-script height gate inside a token family: BRC-197

[BRC-197](../tokens/0197.md) lets anyone retire an expired listing with an authenticated `nLockTime` of at least the expiry height and below 500,000,000 and a non-final sequence number on the listing input, and states the same strict finality boundary as section 4.3. That gate is part of one token family's rules. This BRC states the gate on its own, adds the one-input rule, and fixes its bytes so that other templates can carry it.

### Outputs locked until a height: BRC-190 and BRC-210

BRC-190 section 4.5 requires a timelock condition to read an output "encumbered so that it cannot be spent before a stated height" and to verify that the encumbrance is genuine. A two-key template output whose anytime key is the reader's own and whose after-deadline key is also theirs locks nothing (the anytime branch spends at once), so the template a BRC-190 lock can use is the gate alone, followed by the reader's key (section 3.3). A reader that recognises it (section 3.4) can read the height and the key from the script.

### Prior art outside the BRC series

[BIP65] defines `OP_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY` for chains on which it is enforced, including the type-match rule that section 2.3 restates. [WP-1605] is the paper behind BRC-21 and describes the construction of section 2.1: a signature with a fixed private key and a fixed nonce, so that its `r` is a constant and its `s` is an affine function of the preimage's hash that a script can compute. This BRC fixes the private key at 2 and the nonce at 3.

## Specification

### 1. Terminology

- **Preimage**: the byte string whose double SHA-256 is the message an ECDSA signature for an input signs, for the signature hash type `SIGHASH_ALL | SIGHASH_FORKID` (`0x41`), laid out as in [SIGHASH]: version (4 bytes), hashPrevouts (32), hashSequence (32), the input's outpoint (36), the scriptCode with its length, the input's value (8), the input's sequence number (4), hashOutputs (32), `nLockTime` (4) and the hash type (4), integers little-endian. With no `OP_CODESEPARATOR` in the locking script, the scriptCode is the whole locking script.
- **Deadline `D`**: a block height, an integer with `1 <= D < 500,000,000`.
- **Gate**: the script of section 2 for a deadline `D`, written `GATE(D)`.
- **Anytime branch**: the branch of a template (section 3) that spends without the gate.
- **After-deadline branch**: the branch of a template that spends through the gate.
- **`NUM(x)`**: the minimal push of the integer `x` as a script number: `OP_0` for 0, `OP_1` to `OP_16` for 1 to 16, otherwise the shortest little-endian sign-magnitude encoding, pushed with the shortest push opcode.
- **`PUSH(b)`**: the minimal push of the byte string `b`.

Hex strings below are byte strings in the order they appear in a script.

### 2. The deadline gate

`GATE(D)` expects the preimage on top of the stack and leaves one boolean in its place: true if and only if its `nLockTime` lies in `[D, 500,000,000)` and its sequence number is `0xFFFFFFFE`. The script fails at once if the preimage is not the genuine preimage of the input being evaluated, or if the transaction has another input. It is the concatenation

```
GATE(D) = OP_DUP || PART_A || ONE_INPUT || X || OP_BIN2NUM || NUM(D) || NUM(500000000) || OP_WITHIN || OP_VERIFY || Y
```

where `PART_A` (section 2.1) and `ONE_INPUT` (section 2.2) are fixed, and `X` and `Y` are fixed for each of the two forms of section 2.3. A conforming gate contains exactly these bytes.

#### 2.1 Part A: authenticating the preimage

`PART_A` consumes the copy of the preimage that `OP_DUP` made and leaves the stack as it was before the gate, or fails. It computes, in script, the ECDSA signature over the preimage with private key `d = 2` and nonce `k = 3`, and checks it with `OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY` against the public key `P = 2G`. The interpreter checks that signature against the real signature hash of the input it is evaluating. With `r` fixed, a passing check means the double SHA-256 of the pushed bytes equals that signature hash, short of a SHA-256 second-preimage of a fixed target (the Mathematical and cryptographic basis gives the precise statement), so the preimage is genuine. The constants are:

```
N  = fffffffffffffffffffffffffffffffebaaedce6af48a03bbfd25e8cd0364141   (the secp256k1 group order)
r  = f9308a019258c31049344f85f89d5229b531c845836f99b08601f113bce036f9   (x-coordinate of 3G, mod N)
A  = aaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaaa9d1c9e899ca306ad27fe1945de0242b81   (k^-1 mod N)
B  = 50cb06abb6e5d76030cd8a595068e1718fe6e68c1d32310c6ebb2bde3883643b   (k^-1 * r * d mod N)
P  = 02c6047f9441ed7d6d3045406e95c07cd85c778e4b8cef3ca7abac09b95c709ee5
```

`PART_A` is, in order:

1. `OP_HASH256`, which leaves the 32-byte digest `z`.
2. `REVERSE32`, which is `(OP_1 OP_SPLIT)` repeated 31 times followed by `(OP_SWAP OP_CAT)` repeated 31 times, then `PUSH(00) OP_CAT OP_BIN2NUM`: together these read `z` as an unsigned big-endian integer `Z`.
3. `NUM(A) OP_MUL NUM(B) OP_ADD NUM(N) OP_MOD`: `s = (Z * A + B) mod N`.
4. `OP_DUP NUM(N >> 1) OP_GREATERTHAN OP_IF NUM(N) OP_SWAP OP_SUB OP_ENDIF`: the low-S form of `s`, which is also below `2^255`, so that the next step yields a positive 32-byte value.
5. `PUSH(20) OP_NUM2BIN REVERSE32`: `s` as 32 bytes, big-endian.
6. 31 repetitions of `OP_1 OP_SPLIT OP_SWAP OP_DUP PUSH(00) OP_EQUAL OP_IF OP_DROP OP_ELSE OP_SWAP OP_CAT OP_ENDIF`, which remove leading zero bytes (a byte comparison, since `0x80` is a negative zero as a number).
7. `OP_DUP OP_1 OP_SPLIT OP_DROP PUSH(80) OP_AND PUSH(00) OP_EQUAL OP_NOT OP_IF PUSH(00) OP_SWAP OP_CAT OP_ENDIF`: a zero byte in front when the leading byte has its top bit set, as DER requires.
8. `OP_SIZE OP_2 OP_SWAP OP_CAT OP_SWAP OP_CAT`, then `PUSH(02 21 || 00 || r) OP_SWAP OP_CAT`, then `OP_SIZE PUSH(30) OP_SWAP OP_CAT OP_SWAP OP_CAT`, then `PUSH(41) OP_CAT`: the DER signature `30 len 02 21 00 r 02 slen s` and the hash type `0x41`.
9. `PUSH(P) OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY`.

Its 942 bytes are, in hex, with SHA-256 `a7689b1f7f38b3a10b1581998d4ffa957a72ebdd371d0a723a3caab29fbbd42b`:

Step 3 multiplies two 256-bit numbers, so the product can need 65 bytes as a script number. An interpreter or a policy that caps script numbers at 64 bytes refuses about one honest spend in four (section 7, requirement R7).

#### 2.2 The one-input rule

`ONE_INPUT` leaves the preimage on the stack, or fails. It requires the preimage's hashPrevouts (bytes 4 to 35) to equal the double SHA-256 of the input's own outpoint (bytes 68 to 103), which holds exactly when the transaction has this one input:

```
OP_DUP OP_4 OP_SPLIT OP_NIP PUSH(20) OP_SPLIT PUSH(20) OP_SPLIT OP_NIP PUSH(24) OP_SPLIT OP_DROP OP_HASH256 OP_EQUALVERIFY
```

which is `76547f7701207f01207f7701247f75aa88` (17 bytes). With the sequence rule of section 2.3 it is what keeps the anytime branch open (Motivation, item 3; section 4.3): a spend through the gate that is broadcast early can only be a one-input transaction with no valid replacement, and a node replaces it with a final one-input spend of the same output.

#### 2.3 Part B: the deadline and the finality guard

After `ONE_INPUT` the stack holds the preimage. Part B reads its `nLockTime` and the input's sequence number from their fixed offsets at the end of the preimage: `nLockTime` is bytes `-8` to `-5`, the sequence number bytes `-44` to `-41`. It requires

```
D <= nLockTime < 500,000,000      and      sequence number == 0xFFFFFFFE
```

The first is one `OP_WITHIN`, whose upper bound is the type-match rule of [BIP65]: below 500,000,000, consensus reads `nLockTime` as a block height, as `D` is. `OP_BIN2NUM` reads the four bytes as a sign-magnitude number, so a value with its top bit set comes out negative and fails the lower bound. The second keeps the transaction's lock in force: a transaction is final whatever its `nLockTime` when every input's sequence number is `0xFFFFFFFF`, and this input's is not. It takes one value, the highest below `0xFFFFFFFF`, so that a spend through the gate held as non-final has no replacement: the only higher sequence number is `0xFFFFFFFF`, which the gate refuses.

Two forms are defined. They differ only in how they cut the fields out:

| Form | `X` | `Y` | Opcodes beyond Genesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| split | `8254947f758254947f` | `820120947f758254947f7704feffffff87` | none |
| chronicle | `7658b554b4` | `012cb554b404feffffff87` | `OP_LEFT`, `OP_RIGHT` |

In opcodes, the split form's `X` is `OP_SIZE OP_4 OP_SUB OP_SPLIT OP_DROP OP_SIZE OP_4 OP_SUB OP_SPLIT` (drop the hash type, cut `nLockTime` off the end) and its `Y` is `OP_SIZE PUSH(20) OP_SUB OP_SPLIT OP_DROP OP_SIZE OP_4 OP_SUB OP_SPLIT OP_NIP PUSH(feffffff) OP_EQUAL` (drop hashOutputs, keep the sequence number, compare). The chronicle form's `X` is `OP_DUP OP_8 OP_RIGHT OP_4 OP_LEFT` and its `Y` is `PUSH(2c) OP_RIGHT OP_4 OP_LEFT PUSH(feffffff) OP_EQUAL`. The constants are pushed minimally: `OP_8`, not a one-byte push of `08`, because a node's default policy requires minimal pushes in a version 1 transaction ([CHRONICLE]).

The middle part, `OP_BIN2NUM NUM(D) NUM(500000000) OP_WITHIN OP_VERIFY`, is `81 || NUM(D) || 040065cd1d || a5 69`. For `D = 840000`, `NUM(D)` is `0340d10c`.

#### 2.4 Building a gate

A builder MUST refuse a deadline outside `1 <= D < 500,000,000` (R1). It MUST emit `NUM(D)` minimally (R2); a reader that compares bytes (section 3.4) recognises no other encoding.

### 3. Templates

Public keys in the templates are 33-byte compressed secp256k1 keys, pushed as `21 || key`. `OP_IF` is `63`, `OP_ELSE` `67`, `OP_ENDIF` `68`, `OP_VERIFY` `69`, `OP_CHECKSIG` `ac` and `OP_CHECKMULTISIG` `ae`.

#### 3.1 Two-key

```
OP_IF <K_now> OP_CHECKSIG OP_ELSE GATE(D) OP_VERIFY <K_after> OP_CHECKSIG OP_ENDIF
```

`K_now` spends at any time; `K_after` spends from `D`. Two uses fix the roles. In a vault, `K_now` is a recovery key kept offline and `K_after` the everyday key, so a thief who takes the everyday key must wait for `D`, during which the recovery key can move the coins. In an inheritance, `K_now` is the owner's and `K_after` the beneficiary's, and the owner moves the deadline later by spending the output into a new one (section 5).

#### 3.2 Threshold

```
OP_IF <m> <K_1> ... <K_n> <n> OP_CHECKMULTISIG OP_ELSE GATE(D) OP_VERIFY <K_after> OP_CHECKSIG OP_ENDIF
```

with `1 <= m <= n <= 16`, `m` and `n` pushed as `OP_1` to `OP_16`. The anytime branch needs `m` of the `n` keys; `K_after` spends from `D`. In a pooled fund, each contribution is an output of its own, the `n` keys belong to the fund's approvers, who can release it at any time, and `K_after` is the contributor's, who can take their contribution back if the approvers have not released it by `D`.

#### 3.3 The gate alone

```
GATE(D) OP_VERIFY <K> OP_CHECKSIG
```

The output can be spent only by `K` and only from `D`. This is the shape for a lock with no early exit, such as a BRC-190 timelock.

#### 3.4 Recognising a template

Recognition has two independent axes: which template kind an output is (two-key 3.1, threshold 3.2, or the gate on its own 3.3), and which of the two forms (split or chronicle, section 2.3) its gate is written in. A reader MUST recognise an output by comparing bytes: it reads the keys, `m`, `n` and the bytes of `NUM(D)` at their positions, decodes `D`, rebuilds the template kind from them in each of the two forms, and accepts the output if one rebuild equals the locking script byte for byte (R8). It MUST NOT accept an output that differs in any byte, and it MUST report the template kind, the form, `D` and the keys (R9).

A reader MUST recognise, in both forms, every template kind it is built to report. A reader whose purpose is a single kind need recognise only that kind, in both forms: a reader that reports [BRC-190](../apps/0190.md) timelocks, for example, reads the gate on its own (3.3) and need not recognise 3.1 or 3.2. A general indexer that reports every template SHOULD recognise all three kinds. A reader that reports a deadline MUST report it as a block height and MUST NOT convert it into a date ([BRC-210](../apps/0210.md) section 4.1 states why).

#### 3.5 Minimum value

An after-deadline or reclaim spend has one input and pays its fee from it (section 4), and it is large: it carries the input's whole preimage, which contains the whole locking script, so it is about 1,300 to 1,500 bytes (Security Considerations, "Size and fee"). A builder MUST refuse to create or fund a template output whose value cannot cover that spend's fee, at the prevailing rate, plus one output at or above the network's dust limit (R13); below that the output is consensus-spendable but relay-dead, and the coin is stranded. The sole output of an after-deadline or reclaim spend MUST itself be at least the dust limit (R14).

### 4. Spending

#### 4.1 The anytime branch

The unlocking script is `<sig_now> OP_1` for the two-key template and `OP_0 <sig_1> ... <sig_m> OP_1` for the threshold template, the signatures in the order of their keys in the script and the leading `OP_0` the dummy element `OP_CHECKMULTISIG` consumes. Nothing about the transaction is constrained beyond the signatures. A spender SHOULD spend the template output alone, as the only input, with sequence number `0xFFFFFFFF`, and pay the fee from it (R11): only such a transaction replaces, at a node, a spend through the gate that is already held there (section 4.3).

#### 4.2 The after-deadline branch

The unlocking script is `<sig_after> <preimage> OP_0`, or `<sig> <preimage>` for the gate alone. A spender MUST build the transaction so that (R3, R4, R5, R12, R14):

1. it has one input, the template output, and pays its fee from it;
2. its `nLockTime` lies in `[D, 500,000,000)`;
3. that input's sequence number is `0xFFFFFFFE`;
4. the pushed preimage is the input's `SIGHASH_ALL | SIGHASH_FORKID` preimage, built with the whole locking script as scriptCode;
5. its sole output is at least the network dust limit (R14; section 3.5).

The spender SHOULD sign `sig_after` with `SIGHASH_ALL | SIGHASH_FORKID` too, so that the signature and the gate commit to the same transaction, and SHOULD check before broadcasting that the double SHA-256 of the preimage equals the signature hash its signing library computes (R6). The gate does not read the transaction version; version 1 and version 2 spends are both valid (the vectors include each).

#### 4.3 When the spend can be mined

The gate is a script check. It decides whether a transaction is valid, and the transaction's own lock decides when it can be mined. A transaction with `nLockTime = L` below 500,000,000 and an input whose sequence number is below `0xFFFFFFFF` is final only in a block whose height is greater than `L`; a node compares with the strict `<` ([FINAL]). So a spend with `nLockTime = D` can first be mined in block `D + 1`, and a node accepts it into its main pool once the chain tip is at `D`. (The Genesis specification's text says "greater or equal"; the node implements the strict comparison, and so does this BRC.)

A node that receives the spend earlier holds it in its non-final pool and relays it, unless it is configured to keep no such pool, in which case it refuses it; it never mines it early ([NONFINAL]). It checks that pool for transactions that have become final every ten minutes by default, so a held spend can reach the main pool some minutes after the tip reaches `D`. While it is held, the node refuses a final transaction that spends the same output, except one that spends exactly the same inputs with every sequence number `0xFFFFFFFF`, which replaces it. Because of the one-input rule, the anytime branch's spend (R11) is such a transaction, and because of the sequence rule the held spend can have had no earlier replacement, so the anytime spend is its first and its allowance is untouched.

The anytime branch and the after-deadline branch can both be valid at the same moment from block `D + 1`. Whichever spend is mined first takes the coins.

### 5. Moving the deadline (informative)

The deadline is an absolute height written into the locking script. No script can read the current height, and a spender chooses `nLockTime` freely, so a delay counted from a later event (a withdrawal "N blocks after it is requested") cannot be enforced against the after-deadline key. The holder of the anytime key moves the deadline instead: they spend the output, through the anytime branch, into a new template output with the same keys and a later `D`. A wallet that does this for an inheritance gives the owner a check-in; for a vault, it keeps a stolen everyday key facing a delay. A party that watches the old output (the beneficiary's wallet, for example) can follow the move by reading the spending transaction. A wallet that moves a deadline SHOULD create exactly one output that is the same template with the same keys, so a follower can identify the new coin without ambiguity: that output is the new coin, and its `D` is the new deadline. A follower that finds no such output treats the coin as spent away, not moved. A follower that finds more than one SHOULD keep watching every candidate rather than abandoning the record as terminal, because the move cannot be told from a payment to a look-alike output by the script alone; which candidate is the true continuation is resolved out of band, and a re-import of the watched record MUST be able to revive it while the coin is not genuinely spent away.

### 6. Choosing a form

The split form uses only opcodes that every post-Genesis interpreter runs. The chronicle form is ten bytes shorter and uses `OP_LEFT` and `OP_RIGHT`, which the Chronicle upgrade re-enabled ([CHRONICLE]); it assumes those opcodes occupy the code points `0xb4` and `0xb5`. A node runs them only in the script of an output created at or after the Chronicle activation height (mainnet 943,816), for a spend of any version. On a chain where `0xb4` and `0xb5` are still `OP_NOP5` and `OP_NOP6`, and in the script of an output created before Chronicle, they do nothing: the field-extraction steps that should cut `nLockTime` and the sequence number out of the preimage become no-ops, the `OP_WITHIN` bound and the sequence comparison then read the wrong stack items, and the gated branch fails closed rather than passing wrongly. The failure is safe, in that no coin is spent early, but it leaves a chronicle-form coin unspendable there; a node's default policy in any case refuses to run the re-enabled opcodes in an older output's script. An interpreter without `OP_LEFT` and `OP_RIGHT`, such as one a wallet uses to check an unconfirmed incoming transaction before it is mined, cannot evaluate a chronicle-form spend at all and must defer to the node. For these reasons the chronicle form is not interoperable: a builder SHOULD use the split form as the default (R10), and SHOULD use the chronicle form only where it controls the validator and knows the opcodes are enforced. A reader MUST recognise both forms (R8).

## Implementations

A reference implementation exists, in a wallet whose vaults, inheritances and pooled funds use the templates of sections 3.1 and 3.2, in both forms; it does not build section 3.3's template, which the vectors cover. A gate without the one-input rule was built on a regtest node to demonstrate the pin of the Motivation: a two-input spend through the gate, broadcast before the deadline, was held, the anytime spend was refused (`bad-txn-update`), and at the deadline the held spend was mined in block `D + 1`. With the gate of this BRC, the node refused the two-input spend (`Script failed an OP_EQUALVERIFY operation`) and a spend with sequence number `0xFFFFFFFD`, held a one-input one, refused to let the after-deadline key re-send it with a higher sequence number, and replaced it with the anytime spend, which was mined, in both forms. Its after-deadline spends of the two-key template (both forms) and of the threshold template were broadcast to SV-Node regtest chains with Genesis and Chronicle active. Spends with a timestamp `nLockTime` before the deadline, and spends one block early once the chain had passed it, were refused with a script evaluation failure on an `OP_VERIFY`. Honest spends at the deadline confirmed, in version 1 and version 2 transactions and with `PART_A` products of 64 and of 65 bytes, on a node with permissive policy settings and on one with the default relay policy (script numbers up to 10,000 bytes, scripts up to 500,000 bytes). The wallet also moves an inheritance's deadline through the anytime branch (section 5), confirmed on a regtest node, and the beneficiary's wallet follows the move and claims after the new deadline.

## Mathematical and cryptographic basis

This section states what a passing spend proves and on which assumptions. Write `z*` for the real signature hash of the input being evaluated, the double SHA-256 of its genuine `SIGHASH_ALL | SIGHASH_FORKID` preimage read as a big-endian integer, and `Z` for the double SHA-256, read the same way, of the bytes the unlocking script pushed.

### The forced signature authenticates the preimage

`PART_A` (section 2.1) computes in script `s = (Z * A + B) mod N`, with `A = k^-1 mod N`, `B = k^-1 * r * d mod N`, the fixed private key `d = 2`, the fixed nonce `k = 3`, and `r` the x-coordinate of `3G` reduced mod `N`; it puts `s` in low-S form, encodes `(r, s)` as DER, and checks it with `OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY` against `P = 2G`. `OP_CHECKSIG` verifies a signature with this `r` against `z*` exactly when `s = k^-1 * (z* + r * d) mod N`. The script set `s = k^-1 * (Z + r * d) mod N`. The two agree when `Z = z* mod N`. The low-S fold of step 4 maps `s` and its complement `N - s` to the one value `OP_CHECKSIG` accepts, so the check also passes for the digest whose script `s` is that complement, `Z = -z* - 2 * r * d mod N`; the gate passes for `Z` in these two residue classes mod `N` and no others.

`Z` and `z*` are 256-bit double-SHA-256 digests and `N` is within about `2^128` of `2^256`, so two distinct 256-bit values in one residue class mod `N` differ by exactly `N`, which forces the larger into a top band of about one digest in `2^128`. Each passing class is a single fixed 256-bit target, so any pushed bytes other than the input's genuine preimage that pass the check hash, under double SHA-256, to a fixed value, which is a second-preimage. Under the second-preimage resistance of SHA-256 none can be found, so the pushed bytes of a passing spend are the genuine preimage, and the gate's reads of `nLockTime` and the input's sequence number (section 2.3) are reads of the real transaction. The private key and nonce are public, which is sound: their only role is to make `s` computable, and forging a pass would still require a double SHA-256 second-preimage of a fixed target.

### The deadline holds in consensus

Given a genuine preimage, the gate enforces `D <= nLockTime < 500,000,000` and the input's sequence number `= 0xFFFFFFFE`. Consensus reads an `nLockTime` below 500,000,000 as a block height, and a transaction with an input whose sequence number is below `0xFFFFFFFF` is final only in a block of height strictly greater than its `nLockTime` ([FINAL]). So a spend through the gate is mined no earlier than block `D + 1`. `OP_BIN2NUM` reads the four `nLockTime` bytes as a sign-magnitude number, so a value with its top bit set is negative and fails `D <= nLockTime`, and the upper bound rules out a timestamp-typed value; those are the two values that would otherwise make the comparison worthless.

### Each guard is necessary, and the one-input rule is exact

The guards are independent, and each of the four ways to weaken them (the sequence guard has two) is exhibited by a Test Vector that then passes where it must fail. Without the `OP_WITHIN` upper bound, `nLockTime = 500,000,001` is a past Unix time and the spend is final at once. With a sequence rule that admits `0xFFFFFFFF`, the transaction is final whatever its `nLockTime`. Without the exact `0xFFFFFFFE`, a held gated spend keeps a replacement path that exhausts a node's replacement allowance while the anytime branch, itself a replacement, is refused. Without the one-input rule, the gated key's holder adds a coin of their own to a spend held as non-final, and the anytime branch, which cannot spend that coin, is refused until `D`. The one-input rule is `hashPrevouts = HASH256(outpoint)`, which holds exactly when the transaction has that one input, under the collision resistance of SHA-256.

### Reader agreement

A reader recognises a template by rebuilding it byte for byte (section 3.4), so it accepts an output only when the output equals one of the finitely many rebuilds for its keys, `m`, `n` and `D`. Two conforming readers therefore report the same template, form, deadline and keys for every output either accepts, and neither accepts a near match.

## Security Considerations

**What the gate guarantees.** A spend through the gate cannot be mined before block `D + 1`. That rests on three facts: the preimage is the transaction's own (section 2.1), its `nLockTime` is a block height of at least `D` and its input's lock is in force (section 2.3), and consensus does not mine a transaction whose lock is in force before its height ([FINAL]). The gate's private key and nonce are public; they make the forced signature computable in script and protect nothing. An error in `PART_A` makes honest spends fail; it cannot make a false preimage pass, because `OP_CHECKSIGVERIFY` checks against the real signature hash.

**What it does not guarantee.** The gate does not constrain the spend's outputs. The after-deadline signature does, when it signs with `SIGHASH_ALL`, so a spender who uses another hash type for it lets others change what the spend pays. Nothing in the anytime branch is constrained, so the templates protect the after-deadline party only against early spends by that party's own key; the anytime keys can spend at any time, including after `D`.

**Absolute heights.** `D` is fixed when the output is created. A vault or an inheritance whose deadline has passed is open to the after-deadline key until the anytime key moves it (section 5). Wallets that hold the anytime key SHOULD warn before a deadline passes.

**The guards are the point.** A conforming gate carries all three guards, and each is a MUST: the upper bound of `OP_WITHIN` (`nLockTime` below 500,000,000), the sequence rule (the input's sequence number exactly `0xFFFFFFFE`, not merely other than `0xFFFFFFFF`) and the one-input rule (`hashPrevouts` equal to the double SHA-256 of the input's own outpoint). An implementation that rewrites the gate, for size or for style, MUST keep every one. Without the bound, or with a sequence rule that admits `0xFFFFFFFF`, every output it builds can be spent at once; without the one-input rule, or with a sequence rule that admits other non-final values, its anytime branch can be shut until the deadline. Nothing fails visibly until someone does so. The vectors' `a timestamp-type nLockTime`, `finality bypass`, `another non-final sequence` and `a second input` cases exist to catch that.

**A two-guard gate is non-conforming.** A gate that carries only the bound and the sequence rule, without the one-input rule, is non-conforming. An adversary who holds the gated (after-deadline) key can broadcast, long before `D`, a spend through the gate that also spends a coin of their own, and every node that holds it then refuses the anytime branch's clawback, which cannot spend that extra coin, until `D` (Motivation, item 3). The one-input rule is what forecloses that, so a conforming gate always carries it.

**Pinning, and what the gate leaves.** The one-input and sequence rules close the pin of the Motivation for an anytime spend that follows R11. An anytime spend that adds an input of its own, to pay its fee for example, is refused by a node holding a pending spend through the gate, as before. A threshold template's approvers who release several contributions in one transaction meet the same refusal if any one of them has a pending reclaim; they release that contribution on its own. A node's handling of conflicts is local policy: a miner that never received the pending spend mines the anytime spend whatever its inputs, and a miner can be configured to keep no non-final pool.

**Interpreter limits.** Section 2.1's product can need a 65-byte script number (R7). An interpreter with a lower cap fails honest spends at random, about one in four, depending on the transaction's hash. A chronicle-form spend cannot be evaluated by an interpreter without `OP_LEFT` and `OP_RIGHT` (section 6), so a wallet that checks unconfirmed transactions before accepting them cannot accept one until it is mined.

**Malleability.** A third party can change a broadcast transaction's id without invalidating it where the unlocking script admits another encoding. In a block at or after the Chronicle height, a transaction of version greater than 1 is exempt from the low-S, null-fail and push-only rules, and from the minimal-push and clean-stack policies ([CHRONICLE]), so a spend of either branch can be mined under another txid with the same effect. A spender that tracks its spend SHOULD look for the spend of the outpoint, not for one txid.

**Size and fee.** A template is about 1,060 to 1,150 bytes, and an after-deadline spend carries the preimage, which contains the whole locking script, so it is about 1,300 to 1,500 bytes. The fee is paid from the coin (R11, R12).

**Dust and stranding.** Because an after-deadline spend is large and has one output, a template output funded with too little value cannot produce a spend that both clears the fee and leaves an output at or above the dust limit. Such an output is valid in a block, but no node will relay its spend, so the coin is stranded: consensus-spendable, relay-dead. A builder MUST refuse to create or fund one (R13; section 3.5), and every after-deadline or reclaim spend's sole output MUST clear the dust limit (R14).
## Privacy Considerations

A template output is public in full from the moment it is created: its locking script names every key and the deadline `D` in the clear, and an after-deadline spend carries the whole locking script inside its pushed preimage, so the terms are legible to anyone watching the chain, before and after a spend. The gate's forced-signature key `P = 2G` is the same in every gate built to this BRC, so it is not a per-output fingerprint, though the template is still distinguishable from an ordinary output by its size and its fixed `PART_A` prefix. A party that needs the existence of a deadline arrangement to stay private should not place it on chain in a template. Moving a deadline (section 5) links the old output to the new one through the spending transaction by construction, because that link is what lets a watcher follow the move.
