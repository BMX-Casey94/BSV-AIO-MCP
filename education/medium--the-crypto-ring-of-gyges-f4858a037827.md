# The crypto-ring of Gyges — core principles

- **Perfect anonymity corrupts absolutely.** Plato's Republic 360b–d (the ring of Gyges, Jowett translation): given invisibility, "the actions of the just would be as the actions of the unjust" — a man is just not willingly but of necessity, "for wherever any one thinks that he can safely be unjust, there he is unjust". This is why Bitcoin is pseudonymous rather than anonymous.
- **Privacy and anonymity are distinct design goals.** Privacy is transacting without being traced while retaining the ability to prove payment, ownership and source of wealth; anonymity is the absence of accountability — "the truth of anonymity is that it is not privacy".
- **Commerce requires provable exchange.** "All contractual exchange requires offer and acceptance with consideration. This requires proof of the exchange is available and that cannot be done using a completely anonymous coin" — without proof, a merchant can deny receipt of an overpayment and trade becomes less likely.
- **Fungibility is proof of valid ownership, not unlinkability.** "Fungibility is not about an unknown but rather a proof of valid ownership."
- **Source-of-wealth provability is a feature.** All governments have source-of-wealth laws; with Bitcoin "you can prove the source of your income and yet remain private to the greater world" — as physical cash already does via serial numbers and receipts.
- **Provable origin is what resists censorship at chokepoints.** Untraceable coins can simply be stopped and filtered at exchanges; a coin whose provenance can be demonstrated when required remains acceptable.
- **Privacy is a wallet-implementation problem, not a protocol change.** BIP32 is a hierarchical system allowing determination of every branch of keys; "the problem with bitcoin is not that it is pseudonymous but that the implementations in wallets are poorly configured" — privacy comes from wallet implementation, "not a change to the protocol".
- **Anonymity demand is a motive tell.** "It is a corrupt government who needs anonymity most — people need privacy — aka pseudonymity" — black operations and political slush funds need untraceable money; legitimate commerce needs provable privacy.
