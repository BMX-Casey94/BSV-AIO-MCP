# Prevention is the key
**Date:** 2018-11-12 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/prevention-is-the-key-5c74d098c53a

## Core thesis
Most Internet fraud could be prevented if ISPs and payment intermediaries properly monitored their networks — but the compliance and litigation costs of doing so are passed to consumers. Bitcoin is presented as the designed solution: "the killer app" of sound, cash-like money that cannot be copied or replayed like card data, with 2-of-3 escrow contracts protecting both merchant and buyer, eliminating the fraud problem rather than expensively policing it.

## Key arguments and claims
- Opening claim: "The vast majority of illicit activity and fraud committed across the Internet could be averted or at least curtailed, if destination ISP and payment intermediaries implemented effective processes for monitoring and controlling access to and use of their networks."
- Cites Denning (1999), *Information Warfare and Security*: "even if an offensive operation is not prevented, monitoring might detect it while it is in progress, allowing the possibility of aborting it before any serious damage is done".
- Bitcoin as the answer: "This was the killer app, so to say, in Bitcoin: sound money that does not leave the merchant or user out of pocket." Unlike Visa/MasterCard, "it is just like spending cash... Cards are simple to copy and replay. Bitcoin is not."
- Escrow mechanism: "If a single 2-of-3 contract is used, a user can have a payment locked to a merchant that is released only when the goods are delivered. If the delivery is lost, the escrow comes into play based on what was negotiated in the contract." Merchant payment certainty and buyer delivery assurance in both directions.
- Intermediaries' economic arguments against monitoring are "short-sighted": rising litigation against Internet intermediaries and "greater calls for corporate responsibility" (citing Hazen 1977; Gagnon, Macklin & Simons 2003; Slawotsky 2005) make insecure systems the costlier option.
- PCI-DSS as enforcement lever: "Visa and MasterCard set to issue fines of $25,000 (US) per day for non-compliant organisations", making monitoring costs "insignificant" by comparison, plus a due-care defence against tort claims; the UK "Creative Britain" strategy adds further pressure.
- The pivot: "Yet, all of this increases the costs to the consumer. Opening Bitcoin as the solution is what it was designed to be." — a system "that has none of these flaws is ready for wide adoption".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
A cost-benefit and deterrence framework drawn from the security-economics literature: he quotes Denning on monitoring, then stacks regulatory instruments (PCI-DSS contractual fines, tort liability, corporate-governance norms) to show the card system's fraud costs are structural and consumer-borne. Bitcoin is then introduced as the engineering fix that removes the attack surface (replayable card credentials) rather than policing it, with script-level escrow replacing institutional trust. The mode is half academic citation, half sales pitch.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the "trustlessness removes the need for governance" narrative: Craig argues for monitoring, escrow agents, and regulatory enforcement as features — Bitcoin's value is fraud *prevention*, not censorship resistance.
- Contradicts the "Bitcoin is digital gold / not for payments" drift of BTC in this era: the essay's entire premise is that Bitcoin is consumer payment cash competing with Visa/MasterCard on fraud cost.
- Contradicts the anti-compliance ethos: PCI-DSS fines and litigation are treated approvingly as sticks that align incentives — an unusually pro-regulation stance for a 2018 crypto essay.
