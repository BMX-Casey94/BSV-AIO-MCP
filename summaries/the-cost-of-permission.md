# The Cost of Permission
**Date:** 2026-07-27T12:29:16.246Z | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-cost-of-permission
**Subtitle:** Permissionless systems let anyone in. They do not let anyone decide.

## Core thesis
“Permissionless” conflates two distinct properties: open admission (genuinely achieved — anyone may run a node or deploy a contract) and governance over rule change (held by a client-development coalition, ratified by validators and exchanges, and imposed on everyone else). Firms that sink chain-specific investment are therefore exposed to classic hold-up. His Journal of Institutional Economics paper established the mechanism; this essay asks the follow-up question — what did it cost? — and reports two empirical probes showing that the number does not yet exist.

## Key arguments and claims
- Entry and rule change are separate questions: “Conflating them is where the word ‘permissionless’ does its damage.” Rule changes “originate with a client-development coalition, get ratified by validators and exchanges, and arrive at everyone else as a fact. The firms that built on the protocol are consulted. They do not decide. When the rules change, they rebuild or they leave.”
- The argument was made at length in his paper “Beyond ‘permissionless’: Governance, commitment, and rule change in public blockchains” (Wright, 2026, Journal of Institutional Economics, 22, e21): open entry and governance over rule change are distinct properties, and rule-changing coalitions can revise the protocol after complementors have sunk chain-specific investment.
- The mechanism is “ordinary” hold-up: specific investment, plus residual control held by somebody else, plus an ex post change in terms (Klein, Crawford & Alchian, 1978; Williamson, 1979; Grossman & Hart, 1986). What is not ordinary is the setting: “These systems were designed specifically to remove the party who could do this… governance concentrated in client teams, miners and stakers reconstitutes exactly the residual claimant the design was meant to eliminate. The problem was not solved. It was relocated.”
- The Ethereum Merge (15 September 2022) is the case study: proof-of-work to proof-of-stake, “executed cleanly, widely praised as an engineering achievement. It was one.” But it “obsoleted an entire category of capital equipment and an entire category of firm overnight, on a schedule those firms did not set.” This is architectural change in the Henderson and Clark (1990) sense — components survive, the way they fit together does not, and incumbent competence evaporates; Iansiti and Khanna (1995) documented the same pattern in firm capabilities.
- Probe one — audited filings: he queried the SEC’s full-text search and XBRL endpoints directly. The population of Ethereum-mining-exposed registrants is roughly a dozen, and most are not 10-K filers: HIVE files 40-F; Bit Digital, BIT Mining, SOS and Nano Labs file 20-F; HIVE reports under IFRS while Bit Digital, Core Scientific and Sysorex report under US GAAP, with differing taxonomies. Core Scientific — the most complete impairment tagging — was predominantly a Bitcoin miner and filed Chapter 11 three months after the Merge, so its writedowns say nothing useful about proof-of-stake. The deeper problem: an impairment charge is not an adaptation cost; 2022 writedowns confound a crypto price collapse, an energy price shock, a financing squeeze and the Merge simultaneously. “Reading a protocol-change cost out of an impairment line is not measurement. It is inference dressed as observation.”
- Probe two — the developer record: he pulled the GH Archive hour beginning 12:00 UTC on Merge day (the transition finalised around 06:42). Result: 206,070 events globally in that hour; 175 on Ethereum client and tooling repositories (counted directly from the hourly file, 27 July 2026). Scale: one hour is 101 MB compressed, about 2.4 GB a day, so a six-month window is roughly 440 GB — “a BigQuery job, not a laptop”. The keyword filter matched two Cloud Foundry repositories because “cloudfoundry” contains “foundry” — illustrating that defining which repositories constitute firms building on Ethereum is not a free step; GitHub gives repository names and account logins, not firm identity; and “a push event is not a wage bill. Activity is not effort, and effort is not cost.”
- The industrial-organisation framing: Greenstein (1997) measured lock-in and switching costs among mainframe buyers by asking what the buyers themselves saw — “the correct posture”, not yet adopted for protocol change. Adjacent literature: Farrell and Saloner (1985, 1988) on standardisation and committees “describes protocol governance with unnerving accuracy”; Boudreau (2010) on granting access versus devolving control is his distinction in another vocabulary; Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne (2011) on platform envelopment; Argyres, Nickerson and Ozalp (2023) on complementor adjustment, transaction and opportunity costs.
- The crypto-economics literature circles the problem without landing on cost: Budish (2025) on economic limits of trust at scale; Biais, Bisière, Bouvard and Casamatta (2019) show consensus supports multiple equilibria — “forks are not accidents, they are on the menu”; Davidson, De Filippi and Potts (2018) and Alston, Law, Murtazashvili and Weiss (2022) treat blockchains as institutions. “None of them counts what a rule change costs the firms downstream.”
- Explicit epistemic limits: “I do not have the number.” The two data sources each capture something adjacent to a cost and neither is a cost; joining them requires a repository-to-firm mapping that does not exist, and validating it requires a subsample where true cost is observable — “about four companies under two accounting standards”. “The mechanism is established. The magnitude is open. Anyone who tells you they know what the Merge cost the ecosystem is guessing, and so far that includes me.”
- He closes with a call for data: anyone who ran a business through a protocol transition — “the Merge, SegWit, Taproot, a chain split, any of it” — and knows the cost in engineering months or written-off hardware should get in touch; “That is the missing piece, and it does not exist in any database I can reach.”

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The machinery is institutional economics — hold-up theory, asset specificity and residual control rights from the property-rights tradition (Klein-Crawford-Alchian, Williamson, Grossman-Hart) — joined to industrial-organisation methods (switching costs, platform envelopment, complementor economics) and an unusually disciplined empirical posture. He maps a slogan (“permissionless”) onto two separable institutional properties, identifies the mechanism by which the eliminated residual claimant reconstitutes itself, and then attempts measurement — stopping exactly where the data stops, naming confounds (price collapse, energy shock, financing squeeze versus the Merge) and refusing to let inference masquerade as observation. The essay is deliberately framed as “an idea, not the paper”, with probe-stage evidence and stated uncertainty.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against “permissionless” as a settled virtue: open admission “is a claim about entry. It is not a claim about governance” — the word itself “does its damage” by conflating the two.
- Against governance-as-decentralisation: client teams, miners/stakers, validators and exchanges exercising rule-change power “reconstitutes exactly the residual claimant the design was meant to eliminate”.
- Against upgradeability as an unalloyed feature: the Merge was an engineering achievement and an overnight expropriation of “an entire category of capital equipment and an entire category of firm… on a schedule those firms did not set” — both claims held at once.
- Against forks and upgrades as costless community choice: citing Biais et al., “forks are not accidents, they are on the menu”, and each rule change imposes uncounted hold-up costs on downstream firms — with SegWit and Taproot named alongside the Merge as instances of the same phenomenon.
- Against the adequacy of existing crypto-economics: the literature treats blockchains as institutions but “None of them counts what a rule change costs the firms downstream.”

## Connections
The essay’s only self-reference is to his Journal of Institutional Economics paper (Wright, 2026), which it summarises and extends empirically. It names neither of the other two essays in this set. Thematically, it supplies the institutional-economics foundation for the fixed-protocol stance argued architecturally in “The Protocol No Gatekeeper Wants” (where developers who can alter validity rules “are governors” and rule-changing coalitions exercise institutional power) — that dependence is visible from the two texts’ shared hold-up/governance vocabulary, but is not stated as a cross-reference here.
