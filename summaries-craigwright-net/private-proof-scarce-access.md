# Private proof, scarce access
**Date:** 2026-10-15 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/private-proof-scarce-access | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a service can limit access without learning a permanent customer identifier from every proof of eligibility. Digital scarcity, in this account, is a question about the information needed to enforce a limit as well as about the limit itself. Cryptography can hide an input and bound forgery; the article says the scarce object remains the entitlement that the whole arrangement defines and enforces.

## Key arguments and claims
- The customer example is general: a provider needs confidence that each request is authorised, while the customer may want the pattern of use kept apart from the account that established eligibility. The article says a design that exposes the whole relationship at every interaction can collect more information than the access decision requires. It points to the IETF Privacy Pass specifications as the technical form of the problem.
- RFC 9576, *The Privacy Pass Architecture* (June 2024), is described as distinguishing four roles: a client seeking access, an attester checking eligibility, an issuer creating tokens, and an origin deciding whether to accept them. An attester might establish that an account is valid without the origin receiving those credentials. The article says the checked property and the trust relationships still need specification, and that a valid token does not by itself establish that its holder is a unique person, has paid a particular price, or owns a transferable asset.
- RFC 9578, *Privacy Pass Issuance Protocols* (June 2024), is described as specifying a publicly verifiable issuance protocol using blind RSA signatures: the client blinds its input, the issuer processes the blinded request, and the client finalises a token that can be checked with the issuer's public key. The article says the standard also defines a privately verifiable protocol using an oblivious pseudorandom function, and that the two arrangements should be evaluated separately.
- RFC 9474, *RSA Blind Signatures* (IRTF, October 2023), is cited for two properties. Blindness protects the client's input from the signer under the applicable variant's conditions. One-more-forgery security limits the ability to manufacture additional valid signed messages beyond the signing interactions obtained. The article says the security statement rests on specified cryptographic assumptions and a stated model. Hiding the input can protect the relationship between issuance and use. Preventing extra forgery can protect the issuing boundary. The issuer still chooses how many legitimate requests to honour. The article says cryptographic protection cannot supply a restrictive business policy the operator has never adopted.
- A hypothetical information service sells an allowance of ten retrievals. Eligibility checking could occur when the allowance is acquired, with later retrievals using separately issued tokens. The article says this is a proposed commercial design, not a claim that Privacy Pass implements payment, a ten-use allowance, or a complete account system. The operator would have to define what one retrieval buys, constrain aggregate issuance to the purchased allowance, stop a replacement process from silently creating extra usable allowances, and decide what happens to unused access when terms change.
- It separates a rule limiting issuance from a rule governing consumption. RFC 9577, *The Privacy Pass HTTP Authentication Scheme* (June 2024), section 5.2, is said to recognise settings where replay is acceptable and to recommend anti-replay protection when redemption has side effects. The article says an application selling a consumable allowance must make its own use limit effective, and points to an earlier article on the same site, "The second redemption", for that consumption boundary.
- RFC 9576, section 7.1, is cited for the statement that tokens can be shared and are not intrinsically bound to the original client. An aggregate allowance, the article says, does not establish a per-person use limit. Possessing a token does not settle transferability, the provider's obligations, or the treatment of a disputed request.
- RFC 9576's privacy model, section 3.3, is said to include information revealed during attestation, issuance and redemption. IP addresses and other distinguishing context can reduce a client's anonymity set. Separating roles on a diagram, the article says, does not establish that their operators cannot correlate observations. RFC 9577, section 5.5, is cited for a timing analysis: a fresh context can place issuance and redemption close together and enable correlation when the surrounding events are distinctive; unique contexts prevent use of cached tokens and can add delay. The same specification is said to discuss excessive challenges that exhaust a client's tokens or issuance capacity. An account cookie attached to each retrieval could reveal the relationship a blinded token was intended to protect.
- The institutional source is Craig Steven Wright, *Beyond ‘permissionless’: governance, commitment, and rule change in public blockchains*, *Journal of Institutional Economics* 22 (2026), e21, 1–15, published online 1 June 2026, doi:10.1017/S1744137426100599. The article says the paper separates open admission from authority over rule changes, and examines how participants become exposed when they make protocol-specific investments while other actors retain discretion to revise the conditions those investments depend on. It uses that distinction as an analogy: a token may remain cryptographically valid while a service changes which issuers it accepts, what a retrieval includes, or which clients can obtain replacements. It says the blockchain paper does not establish the performance or governance of Privacy Pass deployments. The assessment it suggests is to map issuance, acceptance and change authority, identify commitments to users, and examine what users retain if an intermediary withdraws.
- On cost, the article says that for a small digital purchase the cost of obtaining and presenting proof can consume a meaningful share of the transaction's value. That cost, it says, includes service delivery, eligibility checks, issuance, verification, failure handling and the customer's time. Some customers may value a service differently when their sequence of uses is linked to an account. The comparison it proposes would hold the entitlement, workload and abuse target fixed, and measure completed legitimate requests, wrongful refusals, customer effort, operating cost, and the information each party can associate with a user, including clients who cannot satisfy a favoured attestation method. It says the specifications do not supply an empirical estimate of demand, operating savings or welfare for the hypothetical service.
- The sources note, dated 15 October 2026, says the ten-retrieval service, the economic interpretation and the proposed evaluation are explanatory analysis, and that no new protocol, deployment measurement, commercial saving, or separate manuscript acceptance is claimed.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The article reads the specifications role by role and property by property, then asks which commercial rule each property leaves unset. Blindness and one-more-forgery security are assigned to different parts of the arrangement. Issuance limits are separated from consumption limits, and token validity is separated from traces such as timing, network information and cookies. The blockchain paper is brought in as an analogy about who can change the rules, with an explicit statement that it does not measure Privacy Pass. The last step is a comparison the article says has not been estimated.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
The article does not set up an explicit contrast with BTC-mainstream positions.
