# Death and taxes, it is time to kill off mythical beasts
**Date:** 2018-09-04 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/death-and-taxes-it-is-time-to-kill-off-mythical-beasts-d7dc1dbaa615
**Subtitle:** I have a hard lesson for many people within the bitcoin and crypto currency community and that is that tax does not go away because you use…

## Core thesis
Bitcoin does not abolish taxation — it makes tax easier, faster and cheaper for governments to collect. Wright argues that Bitcoin as it existed in January 2009 already supports automated, smart-contract-based consumption-tax collection at the point of sale, with no protocol changes required. The essay's conclusion is that anti-government crypto ideology is "anti-reality": Bitcoin threatens neither banks, corporations nor the state, and its real value is a "translucent" audit layer that lowers compliance costs while preserving lower-level privacy.

## Key arguments and claims
- "Tax does not go away because you use crypto currency." The two simplest taxes in a modern economy — consumption tax and income tax — both survive Bitcoin intact; VAT/GST "can be collected on sale and exchange".
- He grounds his authority in professional audit experience: between 2006 and 2008 he worked at chartered accounting firm BDO in Australia managing computer-aided audit (CAATs), writing software "designed to automate the analysis of corporate logs, accounts and tax returns".
- The first "myth" to kill: that Bitcoin "in any way stops or minimises the collection of tax by government". In fact it "simplifies corporate compliance and tax reporting allowing governments to collect faster and with less interference" — the goal is not avoiding tax but minimising the interference tax officials impose on business.
- VAT is "the simplest form of taxation for a government in a completely digital economy". Bitcoin-linked accounting records give pseudonymous interaction that preserves corporate privacy "while simultaneously allowing an organisation to submit the correct amount of tax"; today's VAT is overly complex because of input/output credit differentiation.
- Smart contracts automate the whole chain: "each layer of processing could be individually accounted with automated payments based on existing tax laws lodged in software that report instantly and not only report but transact instantaneously with government. Bitcoin does this." Crucially: "Bitcoin as it existed in January 2009 allowed this to occur. In fact, there is no way to stop this."
- Worked example: differential food taxation (bakery vs tinned goods) can be encoded so that "the transaction as a smart contract exchange between a consumer and a merchant can be recorded and completed with the correct amount of tax instantly transferred to government".
- Government would maintain "a hierarchical set of wallets determined in advance" so a registered taxpayer transmits VAT directly from point-of-sale software; invoices attach to transactions for instantaneous reporting and refunds, eliminating "messy paper records for many years".
- Consumption tax has desirable distributional effects: it "favours those who invest over those who consume" — "The idle wealthy who do little more than spend what they have inherited are forced to either invest or to pay down what they have in taxes."
- Income tax persists via PAYG withholding: employers remit employee tax because otherwise "they do not obtain a deduction. It is not in the employer's interest to pay an employee under the table." He concedes current bitcoin wallets "do not offer much privacy" but blames poor system design, claiming private payroll in bitcoin is "extremely simple".
- Corporate tax is near-impossible to evade on Bitcoin because firms must keep financial records regardless, and inter-company payments give government "a complete and up-to-date record of all of these transactions nearly instantaneously".
- Political remedy, not technical revolt: "Bitcoin does not remove government, that is something people in a democratic society vote on. If you want lower taxes, vote for lower taxes. If that does not work for you move to a different country."
- Privacy coins are dismissed: "The idea that privacy coins as they called will ever take traction is asinine. Government can control access to payments and exchange and require that any transaction involving a media they do not like become immediately void." Bitcoin survives because it "walks a very fine line that allows it to operate as money. That is its strength and that is why bitcoin is cash."
- Tax evasion is reframed as fraud against honest people: "Evading tax is not noble, it is simply cheating and fraud… it means those who cannot, who will not and who are honest are punished for your transgression."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Wright argues from professional authority (his BDO audit-automation years) and from mechanism: he walks through exactly how VAT, income tax and corporate tax map onto Bitcoin transactions and smart contracts, insisting the 2009 protocol already suffices. The rhetorical mode is myth-busting ("the first myth we need to expose") aimed at the crypto community's anti-state consensus, closing with a normative-legal frame — democracy, voting, exit rights — and a jurisprudential flourish citing Judge Learned Hand's "sunshine principle" for aggregate auditability with private detail.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the cypherpunk/crypto-anarchist thesis that Bitcoin is a tool of tax resistance and state subversion: Wright asserts "There is nothing within bitcoin that even remotely threatens banks, corporations or government."
- Contradicts the privacy-coin value proposition (Monero/Zcash logic): governments can simply void transactions in media they dislike, so privacy coins "will never take traction".
- Contradicts the "Bitcoin needs upgrading to be useful" development narrative common to both Core and fork camps: no changes are needed — "Bitcoin as it existed in January 2009 allowed this to occur."
- Contradicts the sound-money-vs-state framing of much BTC advocacy by recasting Bitcoin's soundness ("not easy to debase") as fully compatible with — indeed helpful to — state tax collection.
