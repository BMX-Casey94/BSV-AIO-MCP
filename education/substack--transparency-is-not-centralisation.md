# Transparency Is Not Centralisation — core principles

- **Published participation rules are a unilateral contract, not control.** A network access rule published before participation binds by acceptance-through-performance (Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893] 1 QB 256; The Satanita [1897] AC 59 — participants "sail into" contract by entering under published rules) and constrains the steward as much as the participants; no miner is compelled to join.
- **Court-ordered asset recovery can operate inside an unchanged protocol.** The DAR sequence: plaintiff litigates ownership; obtains a freeze order or injunction; a notary translates the order into machine-readable form broadcast to the network; miners activate the freeze upon consensus among honest nodes — "the Association does not decide who owns what; courts decide."
- **Foreign judgments must be domesticated first.** Recognition in England or Switzerland (Hague Convention 2005; Adams v Cape Industries plc [1990] Ch 433) precedes any on-chain effect, so the mechanism inherits the safeguards of two mature legal systems.
- **Procedural reality disciplines the mechanism.** English freezing injunctions (CPR Part 25; Mareva; American Cyanamid) require a good arguable case, real dissipation risk, a 7–14 day return date and a cross-undertaking in damages; with trials at 12–36 months and costs of £50,000–£150,000 for a contested injunction, the process has economic gravity towards high-value claims.
- **Distinguish three categories of action.** (1) Altering consensus rules, (2) altering transaction-validity conditions, (3) enforcement within an unchanged protocol — judicially triggered recovery operates exclusively in category (3); conflating it with (1) and (2) is the standard error in "centralisation" critiques.
- **Court-order compliance is not securities-law "control".** Under the CLARITY Act's maturity test ("not controlled by any person or group of persons under common control", §101(31)), transparent judicially mediated enforcement is no more control than courts' existence makes a legal system "controlled"; a locked protocol cannot be altered by any party, steward included.
- **Informal, unpublished developer authority is the real concentration.** A handful of maintainers with commit access shipping consensus changes without a published governance framework or disclosed funding is less accountable than published, court-constrained rules — Angela Walch's "In Code(rs) We Trust" (OUP, 2019) reaches the fiduciary conclusion independently.
- **On-chain voting is the dangerous alternative.** Hashpower signalling permits a 30% veto and 51% imposition, and pool coalitions can coordinate invisibly; the accountable architecture is the opposite — external judicial trigger, fixed public rules, observable exercise, judicial reviewability.
