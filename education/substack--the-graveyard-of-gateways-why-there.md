# The Graveyard of Gateways: Why There Can Only Be One Blockchain — core principles

- **General-purpose networks converge on one standard.** The 1978–1994 protocol wars (TCP/IP against OSI, SNA, DECnet, IPX/SPX, VINES, XNS) ended by 1994 with the assessed-best standard winning; network markets are "tippy" (Shapiro and Varian, 1999) and do not settle into comfortable pluralism.
- **Compatibility is what converts potential complementarity into actual value.** Katz and Shapiro (1985) on network externalities, Farrell and Saloner (1985) on excess inertia, Arthur (1989) on increasing-returns lock-in and Economides (1996) supply the mechanism: fragmentation is a cost, not diversity.
- **Losing standards survive by encapsulation over the winner.** IPX tunnelled inside UDP/IP and SNA estates carried over IP are the template: a chain that needs gateways to reach the dominant network has already lost the standards contest.
- **Every boundary is a fee; every crossing is a risk.** A multi-chain architecture re-multiplies the trusted third parties Bitcoin was designed to remove — "the exchange becomes the bank, the bridge becomes the clearinghouse, the wrapped token becomes the promissory note, the relayer becomes the correspondent."
- **Bridges are empirically the weakest component.** Zhang et al. (RAID 2024) catalogued 35 bridge attacks across ten vulnerability types; over $2.8 billion stolen (≈40% of all Web3 value hacked), including Ronin (~$600 million), Wormhole ($325 million), BNB Bridge ($570 million) and Nomad ($190 million); no interoperability mechanism achieves native single-chain security (Belchior et al., 2021).
- **Five forces drive convergence.** Network effects (liquidity, counterparty density, tooling, compliance simplicity); economies of scale (duplicated validation, indexing, wallets and exchange integration); fragmentation transaction costs (spreads, hedging, custody, timing mismatch, bridge risk); the knife-edge (equal functionality ⇒ price decides allocation); and positive feedback once tipping begins.
- **The one-chain claim is testable, and builders should test it.** If one ledger verifiably delivers payments, data, smart contracts, tokenisation and enterprise workloads more cheaply at scale — by throughput benchmarks, cost comparisons and sustained production workloads — then fragmentation is an artificial tax and convergence follows from standard network economics.
- **The competition that matters is the contest to become the standard.** Perpetual incompatibility is not healthy competition; specialisation is real but historically transitional, because the general standard absorbs niche functions over time.
