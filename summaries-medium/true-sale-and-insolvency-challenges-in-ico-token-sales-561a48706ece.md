# True Sale and Insolvency Challenges in ICO Token Sales

## Core thesis
Analysed under English securitisation law, ICO token sales carry two structural legal risks: re-characterisation of the purported "sale" as a loan secured by a mortgage of receivables — a security "void for want of registration" — and challenge as a transaction at an undervalue or a creditor-defrauding transaction under the Insolvency Act 1986. Where the whitepaper, website and promotion show fundraising intent rather than a genuine sale of goods or services, the "utility token" sale is a sham and the token an unregistered security that ought to have been registered under the Companies Act.

## Key arguments and claims
- The two threshold risks of any true-sale analysis: re-characterisation as a secured loan (the resulting security being "void for want of registration") and an Insolvency Act challenge to antecedent transactions, specifically under s 238 (transactions at an undervalue).
- Re George Inglefeld, Ltd supplies the sale-versus-mortgage test via Romer LJ's three indicia: (1) in a sale the vendor cannot recover the subject matter by returning the price, whereas a mortgagor can redeem; (2) a mortgagee can recover any shortfall from the mortgagor, whereas a purchaser bears resale losses; (3) a mortgagee must account for any surplus, whereas a purchaser keeps the profit.
- Welsh Development Agency v Export Finance Co (the Exfinco case) applied Inglefeld: a transaction structured as a sale will be upheld for Companies Act charge-registration purposes unless it is in substance a mortgage arrangement, or it is a "sham".
- Substance is found in the whole documentary matrix: "the court will refer to the provisions of the contract and whitepaper and attached conditions of the ICO token as a whole" — any term inconsistent with a sale of goods or services undermines the utility characterisation.
- Crucially, none of the Inglefeld indicia is necessarily inconsistent with a sale, so "the material risk of such a re-characterisation… remains within the purview of the court".
- The sham finding: a court will find the transaction a sham where the documents — "including the ICO whitepaper and website, and the capital raising and offer material" — do not represent a genuine sale and "the stated purpose of the ICO token creation is to raise funds rather than to provide a path to the delivery of the sale".
- Two fact patterns are fatal: a firm that "has pivoted or altered the course of the firm" after the ICO, and "the promotions of a token for use in exchange", which "leads strongly to reject the assertion that the token has been developed for the purpose of a sale".
- Registration consequence: "As a capital-raising exercise the token exists as an unregistered security that ought to have been registered under the Companies Act."
- Undervalue exposure: a token that "subsequently drops on market" can justify an undervalue challenge, assessed against the sale documents plus "websites, promotional material, and the whitepaper, as well as any social media touting and the market performance" — opening the door to scrutiny of "deferred consideration and related profit extraction devices".
- Defences to undervalue exist where the seller was not insolvent at the time, acted in good faith for business purposes, and had reasonable grounds to believe the transaction would benefit it.
- Separately, s 423 Insolvency Act ("transactions defrauding creditors") gives token purchasers their own avenue of challenge.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Wright applies City-of-London securitisation doctrine as a wrecking analysis: take the ICO's own preferred legal characterisation — a true sale of goods or services — seriously, then show it fails the sale-versus-mortgage indicia, the sham test, and the insolvency challenges. The method is authority-driven legal opinion writing, with pinpoint citations to 1930s Court of Appeal authority, the Insolvency Act 1986 and the Companies Act, rather than economic or technological argument.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the 2017–18 ICO orthodoxy that "utility tokens" escape securities law by being sales of goods or services: the very structure invites re-characterisation, sham, undervalue and registration attacks under ordinary English law.
- Contradicts the industry treatment of whitepapers and websites as non-binding marketing: courts will construe the whole matrix — whitepaper, website, offer material, social-media touting — as the substance of the transaction.
- Contradicts the jurisdictional-evasion narrative (offshore entities, "no issuer" claims): ordinary insolvency and companies law reaches token sales, and token purchasers themselves gain standing as defrauded creditors under s 423.
