# Service, Stake, and the Curious Case of Misclassification — core principles

- **Proof-of-work is service provision; proof-of-stake is ownership.** The PoW miner brings something entirely external to the asset — computation, energy, equipment — and performs a defined, measurable, verifiable task; the protocol behaves "like a particularly austere employer issuing a unilateral contract: perform this task, and you shall be paid."
- **The decisive asymmetry is ontological.** "One can mine without owning; one cannot stake without owning" — mining is contingent, competitive labour with no residual claim afterwards, while staking is constitutive capital deployment earning a proportional yield, a structure instantly recognisable from equity, dividends and interest-bearing instruments.
- **Mining income is wages, not investment returns.** A miner need not own the asset he secures, and choosing to hold earned tokens is a separate speculative activity — "to conflate the two is to confuse a wage with an investment."
- **Under Howey, PoW mining stands outside securities law.** The miner does not invest in a common enterprise and his return derives from his own computational work, not the efforts of others (SEC v. W.J. Howey Co., 1946); PoS — committed capital, expected proportional return, dependence on developers and other participants — warrants serious consideration under the same test.
- **Tax treatment follows economic substance.** PoW rewards are income from services with deductible costs (electricity, hardware, maintenance); PoS rewards are income from capital resembling dividends or interest — treating them identically invites arbitrage, confusion and litigation.
- **Mining pools and staking pools are different in kind.** A staking pool aggregates capital under managed operation with proportional returns — approaching a collective investment scheme "with disconcerting fidelity" — while a mining pool aggregates computational effort, "closer to a cooperative of service providers than to a financial intermediary."
- **Classification determines governance quality.** How systems are regulated, participants taxed, risks allocated and rights defined all follow from the initial category — "to misclassify is to misgovern"; the corrective is conservative: apply existing legal categories precisely rather than inventing new ones.
