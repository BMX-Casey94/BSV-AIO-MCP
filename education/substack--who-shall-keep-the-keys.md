# Who Shall Keep the Keys? — core principles

- **Abolishing the middleman is not abolishing power.** Trustless coordination relocates trust to those who write the code, hold the keys, make the hardware and run the infrastructure. Power, like matter, is conserved. The task is to make that relocated power visible, accountable and chosen.
- **Self-enforcing rules abolish the gap between rule and enforcement — and with it equity.** Discretion, efficient breach, fair use and appeal live in the slack of human law. A self-executing rule does what it was told in situations never contemplated, and the oracle problem returns trust wearing the mask of a data feed.
- **Rent is relocated, not abolished.** Escrow agents, clearing houses and platforms lose their toll; it flows to protocol authors, governance key-holders, infrastructure operators and hardware makers. The middleman has changed costume, not vanished.
- **Cash beyond the state cuts both ways.** No stealth debasement or frozen dissidents — and also no countercyclical policy, no sanctions, no tracing of genuine crime. A serious society must decide how much monetary power to place beyond reach. One does not legislate arithmetic out of existence.
- **Engineered scarcity reopens enclosure as a political choice.** Because a copy's marginal cost is zero, every toll above zero turns away a willing user. That static loss must be weighed, good by good, against the dynamic gain of paying creators. Deliberately preserve fair use, resale, inheritance and the public domain.
- **"Decentralised" can be a smokescreen.** Abolishing formal structure renders power informal and unaccountable. Bind the new sovereigns with transparency, contestability, exit and voice. The abolition of the visible sovereign is an achievement only if it is not also the enthronement of an invisible one.
