# The second redemption
**Date:** 2026-10-12 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/the-second-redemption | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a digital entitlement can be genuine and still be presented twice, and that scarcity becomes operational when the services honouring it can agree which use counts, what remains available, and who bears the cost when that agreement fails. It places a hypothetical double presentation of one purchased computation inside a Digital Scarcity research programme, and connects published distributed-systems research, Internet standards and institutional economics to redemption, recovery and the economics of digital commerce. The page describes the piece as an analytical synthesis and a proposed evaluation, and states that it is not a report of a newly deployed system or of new experimental results.

## Key arguments and claims
- The opening hypothetical: a customer buys one computation of a defined size; the provider issues a signed entitlement accepted at two processing centres; the customer sends it to both; each centre verifies the signature and begins the job. The document was authentic on both occasions, but one purchased job has produced two accepted claims.
- A one-use voucher, a transferable account balance and a subscription are different promises. The article says that calling each object a token does not specify the constraint. For one completed job of a defined size, the invariant is that one entitlement cannot authorise two distinct completed jobs, and copying its representation should not enlarge that allowance. This is said to concern the provider’s service boundary, and to say nothing about making an already delivered output impossible to copy.
- The article cites RFC 6749, Section 4.1.2, as requiring an OAuth authorisation code to be short-lived and as rejecting its reuse. It says that requirement applies to authorisation codes, rather than making ordinary access tokens single-use, and that a specification defines intended behaviour while implementation evidence must show how a service handles competing requests. The sources note dates RFC 6749 to 2012.
- Authenticity, current authority and consumption are treated as three separate checks. Passing the first is said not to complete the other two. The article cites the Bitcoin white paper: Section 2 on why signatures alone do not tell a payee whether an earlier owner has double-spent, and Sections 4 and 5 on proof of work and network rules for a history in which previously spent inputs cannot be spent again. It says the security analysis is conditional on the stated computational assumptions. The sources note also lists Section 11 of Satoshi Nakamoto’s *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System* (2008), alongside Sections 2 and 4–5.
- The article draws a narrower inference for a service entitlement: evidence that a transaction was included in a history does not, by itself, establish that a separate provider delivered the promised service once. The provider is said to need an explicit connection between the recognised entitlement, the consumption decision and its own execution record.
- RFC 7662 is cited for token introspection (Section 2.2) and for a security discussion (Section 4): caching responses reduces traffic and server load, while stale responses can allow use after revocation. The sources note dates RFC 7662 to 2015. Even without a cache, the article says a status query is not a consume operation: both centres might ask whether the entitlement is unused before either records acceptance, and both answers can be accurate when issued.
- For the design under discussion, accepting a job and claiming its allowance must form one protected decision, or occur under authority allocated exclusively beforehand. An atomic claim is defined as competing requests being unable to each succeed against the same remaining allowance. The service must also define how a repeated request is recognised as the same job.
- Peter Bailis and colleagues’ *Coordination Avoidance in Database Systems*, *PVLDB* 8(3), 185–196 (2014), is cited for the claim that which operations can preserve specified invariants without coordination depends on the operations, the permitted states and the method of combining independently produced results, and that coordination can impose latency and availability costs. In the two-centre example, each local record can look admissible while the combined records show two uses of the same allowance. Exclusive allocation to one centre is said to remove the other centre’s authority to redeem it, with the commercial consequence that a customer at the other centre may have to wait, be redirected or obtain an authorised reassignment. The article calls that a design choice to evaluate, rather than evidence that every scarce digital object requires global consensus for every action.
- Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch’s *Perspectives on the CAP Theorem*, *Computer* 45(2), 30–36 (2012), is cited for the tension, under their model, that atomic read/write behaviour and a response to every request cannot both be guaranteed in partition-prone systems. The article says this does not prevent useful disconnected work, and that it requires care about which decisions remain final while communication is unavailable.
- RFC 7009 (2013), Sections 2.1 and 3, is cited for a contrast between self-contained credentials and references to server-held authorisation data, for backend interaction and short-lived tokens as ways of managing revocation, and for propagation delay. The article says the commercial implication is to specify the delay between a change of authority and its effective enforcement at every accepting service. Short validity can limit the life of an old credential, subject to clock and validation assumptions, and is said not to prevent two uses inside that life. A record of cancellation is called incomplete operational evidence if a disconnected service continues to honour the cancelled permission.
- Two failure orders are distinguished. Recording the entitlement as spent and then failing before the job is completed can leave a consistent consumption register while the customer receives nothing. Delivering and then failing before recording can leave an apparently unused entitlement available for another job. The article calls for a recoverable fulfilment process: a durable job identifier, recorded progress, a way to retrieve a completed result, and explicit refund or reissue rules, whose adequacy depends on the actual execution system. It says that naming an operation idempotent does not make a separate physical or computational action automatically repeat-safe. Reconciliation can expose two admissions already granted at disconnected gates; it cannot retrospectively withdraw the service people have consumed.
- Named applications are prepaid computation, event admission, metered data services and transferable rights to a defined service, with different economic purposes: a ticket allocates limited venue capacity; a computing allowance assigns a budget against costly execution; charging for another copy of a digital file is described as a different question about pricing and access. These examples are said to require separate assessments.
- For a commercial comparison, the article says it would count the cost of verification and communication alongside waiting, rejected legitimate requests, recovery work, duplicate delivery and dispute handling, and would identify who bears each loss. A provider’s lower processing expense is said not necessarily to be a lower total cost if customers absorb more failures or administrators perform the missing reconciliation.
- The institutional discussion draws on Craig Steven Wright, *Beyond ‘permissionless’: governance, commitment, and rule change in public blockchains*, *Journal of Institutional Economics* 22 (2026), e21, 1–15, published online 1 June 2026, DOI 10.1017/S1744137426100599. The article says that paper distinguishes open admission from authority over rule changes, and that applying the distinction here suggests examining who can change redemption limits, override a consumed balance or replace the operator. It states: “This application is an interpretation, not a measured result about voucher markets.”
- The proposed evaluation would submit the same entitlement concurrently to multiple accepting services, repeat requests after uncertain responses, interrupt execution between recording and delivery, and test transfers or revocations while a service is disconnected, recording the final customer outcome as well as the state of the register. Measurements named: distinct benefits delivered per entitlement, unresolved valid claims, time to effective revocation, recovery time, response-time distributions, and the cost borne by each participant. Designs should be compared under the same promised service and failure conditions.
- The sources note says the technical standards specify behaviour and the distributed-systems results hold within their stated models. The computing-centre examples, commercial interpretation and proposed evaluation are called explanatory analysis. It states that no new deployment measurement, empirical welfare estimate or publication status for a separate manuscript is claimed. The page’s own publication date is 12 October 2026.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He begins with one authentic entitlement accepted twice, then separates the consumable unit from checks that can be mistaken for consumption: a valid signature, a fresh status answer, and a cached revocation response. Standards and published models are kept inside the scope the page states for them, and coordination, revocation delay and the gap between recording and delivery are treated as design choices with a named bearer of the loss. The governance paper is applied as an interpretation. The close specifies an evaluation and the measurements it would record, and the page labels that close as a proposal rather than a new experiment.
