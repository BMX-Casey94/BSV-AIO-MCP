# The Theft That Never Was — core principles

- **Theft is a defined legal concept, not an elastic label.** It requires the unlawful taking of property belonging to another with intent to deprive — presuming ownership, control and "the continued existence of a party capable of being wronged"; remove these elements and the concept dissolves.
- **Ownership in Bitcoin is operational, not a historical label.** Control is exercised through private keys, without which one cannot spend, move or signal ownership in any way the system recognises — "a coin without accessible keys is not actively held property; it is, functionally, abandoned value."
- **Abandonment is a recognised legal category.** Property voluntarily relinquished or effectively surrendered through inaction changes status across jurisdictions: "one does not commit theft by recovering abandoned property; one reintroduces it into use" — the legal foundation on which court-ordered digital-asset recovery rests.
- **Freezing dormant coins by protocol change is expropriation.** Rendering spendable outputs unspendable "not by the actions of their holders, but by the decree of those modifying the protocol" rewrites ownership conditions retroactively, with no authority inherent to the system itself — "this is not preservation; it is expropriation."
- **Protocol immutability is the property-rights guarantee.** A system that can rewrite who may spend what, on any rationale, has shifted "from law to discretion, from principle to policy" — the fixed protocol is precisely what prevents collective adjudication of private holdings.
- **Bitcoin provides privacy, not anonymity — and the difference is structural.** The ledger operates in clear text: transactions recorded publicly, permanently and in order; addresses are pseudonymous but flows are visible, analysable and attributable. "This is not a flaw. It is a feature" — selective disclosure within accountability, not untraceability.
- **The two readings generate opposite policy.** If Bitcoin were anonymous money, dormant coins must be protected in perpetuity; as a public system of record, extended inaction raises legitimate questions of control and abandonment — "the former view requires belief; the latter requires only observation."
- **The system records; it does not infer intent.** No class of coins is a special case: "it does not pause. It does not speculate. It does not infer intent. It records" — intervening on behalf of dormant holdings overrides the system rather than respecting it, and builders should treat any proposal to do so as a constitutional change, not a patch.
