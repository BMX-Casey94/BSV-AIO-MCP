# The Defence That Halves — core principles

- **Proof of work converts a current expense into a defence.** Difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks to restore the ten-minute interval; it is a readout, not a store. A chain mined for a decade is no harder to rewrite than one mined for a month at the same rate.
- **Under free entry, aggregate miner expenditure equals aggregate revenue.** Halve revenue and you halve expenditure, hashpower and rewrite cost. Difficulty drops out of the calculation; there is no accumulated or thermodynamic reservoir of security.
- **The subsidy was scaffolding; the fee market was the building.** White paper Section 6 specifies the incentive transitioning entirely to transaction fees. Steady-state security equals the number of transactions multiplied by what each pays — an unwritten throughput specification.
- **A fee buys queue position, not security or value.** Fee revenue depends on capacity, congestion and waiting costs, not the exchange rate. The ceiling is users' outside option, so higher fees relocate users rather than raise revenue.
- **Layer two subtracts from the security budget.** A channel transaction is a fee that never reaches a miner while the value moved still rests on base-layer security. The more successful the overlay of off-chain volume, the smaller the budget protecting it.
- **Hardware capability per dollar doubles on a shorter clock than the subsidy halves.** Cheaper hardware gives defenders volume at the same spend and attackers a discount. Price growth and issuance decay nearly cancel, leaving flat revenue against a rising rewrite-cost requirement.
- **A chain that will not scale cannot fund its own defence.** Only two terms can raise fee revenue — capacity and price — and both are bounded if block size is capped. The electricity leaving the system and the security it was meant to purchase are one object seen twice.
