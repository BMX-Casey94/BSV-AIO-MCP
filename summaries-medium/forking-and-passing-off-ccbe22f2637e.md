# Forking and Passing Off…
**Date:** 2020-02-13 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/forking-and-passing-off-ccbe22f2637e
**Subtitle:** If you consider what most cryptocurrency proponents [2] are trying to do, you will see an attempt to exclude governments and rights and…

## Core thesis
An excerpt of a longer craigwright.net essay (the body continues on his personal blog) arguing that while Bitcoin's software was released under the MIT Licence, the blockchain database was not — and that Craig, as "sole creator of Bitcoin", holds sui generis database rights over it under UK and EU law. Forking the code is legitimate (Litecoin, Ethereum), but BTC ("CoreCoin") and BCH ("BCash") copied the database, altered the protocol, and passed themselves off as the original — a breach he formally puts them "on notice" for, while offering to license the database on his terms.

## Key arguments and claims
- MIT Licence covers code, not content: by analogy, a novel written on Linux and OpenOffice leaves the software open source but the author retains copyright in the work. "Bitcoin is a distributed registry… And the distributed ledger is a database."
- Full issuance at creation: "Bitcoin was formulated as a system with a set number of individual tokens" — 21 million bitcoin, each "an arbitrary verbal representation of 100 million individual and indivisible tokens", issued by him in January 2009 "as director of companies I created in multiple jurisdictions". The block subsidy is payment from that initial issuance, not ongoing issuance.
- Nodes are paid contractual agents, not owners: the original client's "Options->Generate Coins" text and the white paper's six-step network process form a unilateral contract (citing *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company*); nodes are "agents of the Bitcoin network with no individual property rights", remunerated to check double-spends — "the digital equivalent of cheque fraud".
- Statutory basis: the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 and the Copyright and Rights in Databases Regulations 1997; database right subsists on "substantial investment in obtaining, verifying or presenting the contents", vests in the maker, and protects for at least 15 years from publication (mid-January 2009).
- Breach mechanism: "Systematic extraction, and reutilisation" of the blockchain in a forked system competing with the original is actionable; SegWit in BTC (2017) and opcode additions in BCH (2018) are "clear breaches" because "the rules of the system are set in stone".
- Ethereum contrast: when ETH and Ethereum Classic split, the creator changed the protocol, so ETC — which kept the original chain — is the party in breach there; BTC is "unrelated to the original creator of Bitcoin", so its alterations breach instead.
- Supporting doctrine: *Ryanair Ltd v PR Aviation BV* (C-30/14) — a database author may impose contractual limitations on use even absent copyright or sui generis protection; the Computer Misuse Act 1990 and trespass to chattels offer further avenues.
- Jurisdictional strategy: Core and ABC are "global partnerships under law" with senior partners in the UK/EU, and he is moving his trust and companies into British residence and paying UK tax so the dispute "come[s] under British law".
- Passing off, not forking per se: "People can fork my software and make alternative versions. But, they have no rights to change the protocol using the underlying database." He claims he would rather litigate — "I will win as those who are currently challenging me do not know what is about to happen."
- Peer-to-peer redefined: the familiar "completely decentralized" quote is "cherry-picked out of context"; Bitcoin is a hybrid of direct user-to-user exchange plus a separate commercial node network maintaining the registry under contract.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He argues as a property lawyer rather than a cypherpunk: statutes (CDPA 1988, Databases Regulations 1997), case law (*Carlill*, *Ryanair*) and contract doctrine (unilateral offer, agency) are mapped onto Bitcoin's architecture. The method is redefinition — miner becomes "agent", issuance becomes "distribution", the ledger becomes "a database" — so that existing property and contract law swallows the system whole. Rhetorically he pairs menace ("I'm far nicer before the lawyers get involved") with an offer to license, and grounds the technical claims in primary sources (the white paper's six steps, the original bitcoin.org text).

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- "Anyone may fork Bitcoin" orthodoxy: he asserts the ledger itself is owned property, so copying the UTXO set without licence is unlawful even where the code is open source.
- "Miners are the network / no owner" narrative: miners are demoted to paid agents with no property rights; the system has an issuer and an owner.
- Ongoing-issuance monetary account: the standard view of new coins "mined" roughly every 10 minutes is rejected — all tokens existed from genesis and the subsidy is contractual consideration.
- Anti-government ethos: "Bitcoin is not a system that allows you to avoid government… because blockchains are all traceable."
