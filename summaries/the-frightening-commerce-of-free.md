# The Frightening Commerce of Free Persons
**Date:** 2026-05-16 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-frightening-commerce-of-free
**Subtitle:** Why global person-to-person economies without unnecessary intermediation threaten banks, governments, platforms, and every institution that has mistaken obstruction for civilisation

## Core thesis
A global person-to-person economy frightens banks, governments, platforms and regulators because it asks the one question they cannot tolerate: why must exchange pass through you? The modern economy is an economy of permission in which the legitimate need for order has been converted into "an empire of tolls". Direct exchange does not abolish law, tax or records; it merely removes the presumption that every payment must be pre-inspected by authorised intermediaries — and that removal of permission, not any ideology, is what makes it intolerable.

## Key arguments and claims
- Intermediation is not inherently illegitimate — banks, courts, brokers and registrars can reduce risk and transaction costs. The problem begins "when the intermediary ceases to be a servant of exchange and becomes its condition", and the public mistakes the gate for the road.
- The decisive test, drawn from Coase, North and Williamson: does the intermediary reduce friction, or live from friction? "The intermediary's nightmare is not hatred. It is comparison."
- Banks hold positional power over captured flows — wages, invoices, remittances, settlement — monetising float, fees, spreads, custody and informational asymmetry. Direct exchange makes custody optional and forces banks to justify themselves transaction by transaction.
- Governments govern through chokepoints: regulated intermediaries are "the state's extended nervous system". The claim that private digital cash makes taxation impossible is "historically illiterate" — tax was collected under cash dominance using ledgers, audits, customs records and field agents.
- Delay is a business model, not mere inefficiency: whoever controls the interval between instruction and completion controls the relationship, and delayed settlement is "a hidden transfer from the weaker party to the stronger" — the small merchant, contractor, exporter and worker wait while the institution optimises treasury.
- The Global South has most to gain: for the banked, the system is a fee; for the unbanked, a wall. Direct payment threatens remittance margins, correspondent banking, currency controls and financial enclosure used to trap value and monitor dissent.
- Platforms monetise captivity — controlling visibility, payment, reputation and data. Functional micropayments would break the advertising model that has "corrupted much of the digital world": pay for the article, the API call, the song, without making every user a product.
- Surveillance is not civilisation. There is "a crucial difference between records and surveillance": records are created by parties for evidence, contract, tax and audit; surveillance is imposed by an external power for general monitoring. The person-to-person economy "is not anti-law. It is anti-presumption."
- The monetary state fears competition in settlement: alternatives discipline monetary monopolies, and weak or overreaching states defend enclosure most aggressively — "Capital controls become patriotic. Inflation becomes unavoidable. Surveillance becomes safety. Exit becomes subversion."
- Safety rhetoric is examined as control: protection expands the category of danger from fraud to risk to non-compliance to "unacceptable political association". The alternative is protection as service — escrow, identity, insurance, courts used optionally — not protection as sovereignty.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Institutional economics combined with a constitutional-moral argument. Each section states a "predicate" about a class of institution (banks, governments, platforms, regulators), then applies the Coase–North–Williamson cost test to show where intermediation stops serving exchange and starts taxing it. Historical evidence (tax collection under cash, revolutions that replaced ruling classes) does the destructive work; the constructive claim is modest — exchange as an act between parties, with evidence, answerable under law.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
Engagement is mostly indirect, but section XI targets the capture that defines BTC practice: a direct system surrounded by "custodians, exchanges, side layers, platforms, compliance wrappers, tokenised claims, and financial products" leaves the user holding "not money but an account; not settlement but exposure; not cash but a promise" — at which point "the revolution has become a bank with better marketing". The mainstream claim that financial privacy precludes taxation is rejected as historically illiterate rather than merely wrong.
