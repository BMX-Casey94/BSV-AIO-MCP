# Taxing Bitcoin — Ordinary and tax concepts of "Money"
**Date:** 2018-10-29 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/taxing-bitcoin-ordinary-and-tax-concepts-of-money-15f935c60260
**Subtitle:** Ordinary and tax concepts of "Money"

## Core thesis
Bitcoin satisfies the ordinary legal meaning of "money" under Australian (and common) law, and therefore falls within the expanded statutory definitions used for GST and income tax. Craig builds the case doctrinally — from Travelex, dictionary definitions and the negotiability cases — and adds SEC v Shavers as US authority, concluding that formal recognition of Bitcoin as foreign currency by governments is "inevitable". This is the second, case-law-heavy instalment of the Taxing Bitcoin series, focused on Australian law with UK/common-law treatment deferred.

## Key arguments and claims
- Ordinary meaning (Travelex Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [2008] FCA 1961, Emmett J, undisturbed on appeal): "Money is any generally accepted medium of exchange for goods and services and for the payment of debts"; a thing can be money without being legal tender, since "it is common consent and conduct that gives a thing the character of money" (Miller v Race (1758); Moss v Hancock [1899]).
- Money need not be tangible: it is "an abstract concept determined by reference to its use as a medium of exchange", so bank-account balances transferred between persons are plainly money — clearing the way for an intangible like Bitcoin.
- "Currency" has two senses: the medium itself (coins and notes circulating in a polity) and the proprietary regime of negotiability — title to money is exempt from nemo dat quod non habet, so "the right to money is inseparable from the possession of it" and a recipient need not enquire into the payer's title (citing David Fox, Property Rights in Money).
- "Legal tender" is narrower again: that which the polity prescribes by legislation as a lawful mode of payment (Watson v Lee; Proctor's Mann on the Legal Aspect of Money) — "not all money is necessarily legal tender".
- GST Act s.195–1 defines money with "includes" (currency, promissory notes, bills of exchange, negotiable instruments, postal notes, credit/debit-card payments, account credits and debits, debt creation), and per Pearce & Geddes on statutory interpretation "includes" enlarges rather than exhausts the ordinary meaning — so the GST concept is broader than, and contains, ordinary money.
- Income-tax law (ITAA 1936/1997) does not define "money", so the ordinary meaning applies; Commissioner of Taxation v Resource Capital Fund IV LP [2013] FCAFC 118 confirms "money" includes foreign currency and "is not limited to legal tender".
- Applying the tests: bitcoins are a generally accepted medium of exchange increasingly taken by businesses; they need no state issue; they bear money's character "by common consent and conduct"; and wallet-to-wallet transfer exhibits negotiability, making bitcoin "a form of intangible personal property" (quoting Rhys Bollen: "the circulation of valuable rights… transferred irrevocably and immediately… by electronic order").
- US authority: in SEC v Shavers (E.D. Tex.), Magistrate Judge Mazzant held under Howey that "Bitcoin is a currency or form of money" because it buys goods and services and exchanges into conventional currencies — defeating the defence that BTCST investments were not "an investment of money".
- Forward claim: developments in Germany, Switzerland and the UK show "it is inevitable that Bitcoin will be formally accepted as 'currency' (i.e. foreign currency) by governments around the world".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Pure doctrinal legal method: hierarchy of definitions (ordinary → statutory → case-glossed), pinpoint citations, and the interpretive canons of statutory construction ("means" versus "includes"). He reasons cumulatively — money, then currency, then legal tender, then each tax statute — so the conclusion that Bitcoin is money arrives as the residue of settled authority rather than assertion. The rhetorical posture is the expert tax lawyer submitting an opinion ("it is submitted that…"), not the polemicist of his anti-Wormhole pieces.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against the "Bitcoin is outside state categories / money without government" framing: Craig's route to legitimacy runs through the state's own case law — Bitcoin is money precisely because courts' tests (common consent, medium of exchange) already capture it.
- Against the regulatory-sceptic position (common in 2014–2018, e.g. the Shavers defence itself) that Bitcoin is not "money" and so escapes money-law: he embraces and extends the opposite holding.
- Implicitly against HODL-only conceptions: the entire analysis privileges Bitcoin's use as a medium of exchange — money is what "passes freely from hand to hand… in final discharge of debts", not what is hoarded as a collectible (which the GST Act expressly carves out).

## Notable quotes
