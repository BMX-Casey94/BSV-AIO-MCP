# Four clocks inside proof of work
**Date:** 2026-10-06 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/four-clocks-of-proof-of-work | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a random search can operate inside a system that keeps track of its past, and that the different clocks inside proof of work show what a security model needs to remember and when a useful approximation reaches its boundary. It is an explanatory account of the author’s published article *Why Hash-Trial Memorylessness Does Not Extend to the Nakamoto Protocol*. The starting question, as the page puts it, is which part of the system the model is describing: a statement about independent hash attempts has a different scope from a statement about block arrivals, the evolving protocol, or the income a miner ultimately receives.

## Key arguments and claims
- The opening comparison is two mining operations with the same computing power and the same current difficulty. The next successful hash may have the same probability in each. One chain may be approaching a difficulty adjustment while the other has just completed one. One operator may hold rewards close to becoming spendable; another may have only just earned them. The immediate search conditions can match while the decisions facing the operators differ.
- Bibliographic claim: Craig S. Wright (2026), *Why Hash-Trial Memorylessness Does Not Extend to the Nakamoto Protocol*, *International Journal of Cryptocurrency Research*, 6(1), 1–25, published 25 June 2026, DOI 10.67191/IJCCR.6.1.2026.1-25. This explanatory article was published on 6 October 2026 and focuses on Sections 3–5 and the limitations in Section 9. The page says the source article is available under CC BY 4.0, and that this is a new explanatory account, not a reproduction of the paper.
- Search clock: at a fixed target, under the usual independent-trial idealisation, a failed hash attempt does not make the next attempt more likely to succeed. There is no accumulated entitlement to a winning result. The geometric distribution describes the number of attempts until success; a constant-rate exponential approximation describes the corresponding waiting time. Memorylessness here means that having already waited does not, by itself, alter the remaining waiting-time distribution. The article says this is stronger than saying that the outcome is uncertain, because many uncertain processes depend on elapsed time or earlier events. The qualification is that the target and the rate of hashing are held fixed. A model that changes either needs to account for that change rather than carrying the same constant-rate assumption across it.
- Adjustment clock: the paper follows Bitcoin’s 2,016-block difficulty-adjustment structure. The protocol uses the elapsed time for the preceding interval to set the next difficulty. Earlier block arrivals therefore help determine the conditions of later searches. An exponential approximation can remain useful within an interval whose relevant conditions are stable. Across adjustments, one fixed arrival rate no longer describes the whole process. A model needs the current difficulty, the position within the adjustment cycle and the timing information used by the adjustment rule. Equal current computing power does not, on this account, make two differently positioned chains interchangeable over a horizon that reaches their next adjustments. The length of the model’s horizon becomes part of the economic question.
- Reward clock: discovery and spendable income occur at different times. Coinbase maturity requires a newly created reward to wait through 100 blocks before it can be spent. Whether the associated block remains in the accepted history matters to the reward’s eventual realisation. In the paper’s accumulation model, fee-paying transactions arrive while miners search, so the value available in a candidate block can change even when no block has yet been found. The simple closed-form analysis assumes a constant fee-arrival rate; actual transaction selection, congestion and fee behaviour require additional modelling. A miner may face an unchanged chance of success on the next attempt while the economic value attached to success changes.
- Confirmation clock: a discovered block sits within a growing chain of work. Confirmation depth and the position of competing branches affect an assessment of whether it will remain part of the accepted history. In the models considered by the paper, different accumulated work gaps can imply different reorganisation risks. The article says this does not make any particular confirmation count an unconditional guarantee. The calculation must specify the opposing resources, the strategy, the protocol rules and the period over which success is assessed.
- Together, these clocks are said to explain why current mining expenditure alone can leave important parts of the decision unresolved. An expanded account tracks chain-specific difficulty, progress towards retargeting, accumulated work, confirmations and the fee environment.
- The paper is described as a correction to the stochastic description used in an economic security model. It preserves the question of how the ongoing cost of honest participation compares with the value available from an attack. Short-horizon, fixed-difficulty calculations can still be useful when their assumptions fit the question. The practical task is to state what has been simplified and test whether that simplification matters over the chosen horizon.
- The paper’s attack-cost simulations and longer-run projections are called conditional exercises. Their numerical values depend on assumptions about fees, participation, prices and growth; they are not observations of actual attacks or universal security guarantees. This page does not state those numerical values.
- The article distinguishes a memoryless waiting-time distribution from a Markov description. A model can summarise relevant history in a sufficiently specified current state. Calling that model Markov does not mean difficulty, confirmations or accumulated fees can be omitted from the state.
- The broader lesson stated is a discipline of scale: start with the mechanism whose randomness is understood, then identify the rules that carry its outcomes forward. A good approximation earns its usefulness by making clear which clock it describes, which state it retains and which question it can answer.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He fixes memorylessness to the hash trial at a constant target and hashing rate, then adds three further clocks — difficulty adjustment, reward maturity and fees, and confirmation — each of which carries history into a later decision. An exponential or geometric description can remain inside a stable interval; across an adjustment, or once spendability and reorganisation risk matter, the model has to name the extra state. Markov is treated as a sufficient description of the current state, which still has to include difficulty, confirmations and accumulated fees if those affect the question. The attack-cost simulations are labelled conditional, and this page does not print their numbers.
