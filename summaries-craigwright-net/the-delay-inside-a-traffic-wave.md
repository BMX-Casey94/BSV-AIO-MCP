# The delay inside a traffic wave
**Date:** 2026-10-16 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/the-delay-inside-a-traffic-wave | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a queue can form on a road with no junction, accident or narrowing, because each driver's delayed response changes the conditions faced by the next driver. The disturbance can travel backwards while the vehicles move forwards. The article treats the link from a local correction to collective instability as something that has to be modelled and then tested, not read off an average speed.

## Key arguments and claims
- It cites the author's public preprint, Craig S. Wright (2025), *Mathematical Modelling of Oscillatory Dynamics in Circular Traffic Systems*, arXiv:2507.06702v1, submitted 9 July 2025. The sources note says the account draws on Sections 2–5 and 11.3 and "does not claim journal publication or completed empirical validation." The article calls the useful connection one between a local response and the behaviour of the complete loop.
- It reports the experiment of Sugiyama and colleagues (2008), *Traffic jams without bottlenecks*, *New Journal of Physics*, 10, 033001, doi:10.1088/1367-2630/10/3/033001. The article says 22 vehicles travelled around a circular road with a circumference of 230 metres. Drivers began at approximately equal spacing and were asked to aim for about 30 kilometres per hour while following safely. Fluctuations developed, several cars eventually stopped, and a jam travelled against the direction of the traffic. The article says the experiment isolates the possibility that interactions among vehicles can sustain a queue without a fixed obstruction. It says a ring removes entries, exits and many everyday choices, and does not identify every mechanism behind congestion on an open road.
- The preprint, as the article describes it, begins with uniform speed and spacing, then examines disturbances using delay equations and Fourier modes. A mode is a spatial pattern around the ring; the analysis asks whether that pattern shrinks or grows. A delayed response uses an earlier observation of the gap or relative motion, so two vehicles with the same present spacing can respond differently because their recent histories differ. Response strength, spacing and the timing of information are treated as separate parts of the explanation.
- The article calls this analytical research. It says Section 11.3 sets out possible future simulation verification and does not report a completed simulation or a new road experiment. Model conditions, it says, need to remain visible when the proposed stability analysis is interpreted.
- A separate comparison is Stern and colleagues, arXiv:1705.01693, published as *Dissipation of stop-and-go waves via control of autonomous vehicles: Field experiments*, *Transportation Research Part C*, 89 (2018), 205–221, doi:10.1016/j.trc.2018.02.005. The article says they studied circular traffic with more than 20 vehicles and showed that controlling the velocity of one vehicle could dampen stop-and-go waves, comparing speed, braking and fuel-use measures. It says that evidence concerns those controllers under the tested conditions. It does not, the article says, establish that every automated vehicle will smooth traffic, or validate the equations in the later preprint. The link it draws is a research question: which response rules reduce disturbances as they pass from vehicle to vehicle?
- The evaluation the article says would be useful would declare the response rule, the observation delay and the traffic conditions before comparing outcomes, and would follow the disturbance across successive vehicles alongside journey speed, braking and spacing. It says an average speed alone can conceal repeated sharp changes within the same journey. It also separates an initially growing disturbance, close to a reference state, from a persistent wave, which requires examining the subsequent motion. A credible comparison, it says, needs both a clearly specified model and observations that can challenge it.
- The closing question is what information each participant acts on, how old that information is, and what the action transmits to everyone else.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The article sets a ring-road experiment, a delay-equation analysis, and a control experiment side by side, and states what each is not allowed to prove. Sugiyama is used to show that a queue can persist without a fixed bottleneck. The preprint is used to put response history into the model, and is marked as analysis with simulation still prospective. Stern is used as evidence about particular controllers, not as validation of the preprint's equations. The last section lists the observations that would be needed before a response rule could be compared.
