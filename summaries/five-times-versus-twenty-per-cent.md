# Five Times Versus Twenty Per Cent
**Date:** 2026-07-29T05:22:43.125Z | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/five-times-versus-twenty-per-cent
**Subtitle:** Open the edge of a system and innovation multiplies. Open its foundations and almost nothing happens.

## Core thesis
Debates about changeable foundations are conducted in the language of flexibility, but the real question is empirical: where does effort go when one competitive channel is closed? Competition relocates rather than disappears, and the measurement exists — opening a platform's edge to complementors multiplied innovation up to fivefold, while devolving control of the platform itself yielded roughly twenty per cent, an order of magnitude smaller and often insignificant. The operative variable is not fixity but the cost of changing the rules relative to the cost of building at the edge.

## Key arguments and claims
- **Boudreau's number.** Kevin Boudreau studied twenty-one handheld computing systems (1990–2004), separating two things lumped together as "open": granting access to complementors versus devolving control (sharing equity, narrowing vertical scope). Access was associated with up to a fivefold acceleration in new device development; devolved control with roughly twenty per cent — "an order of magnitude smaller, and in several specifications not significant at all". He states the caveats himself: a narrow device-rate measure, a post-establishment sample, incremental variation in control, and the warning that "fixed" and "open" are not the same axis.
- **Parnas convicts himself.** Parnas (1972) decomposed an index producer two ways: by processing steps versus by information hiding — listing the design decisions most likely to change and hiding one per module. A character-packing change hit every module of the first design, one module of the second. The load-bearing passage is his self-criticism: fixing the *order* of shifted lines gave "more information than necessary", restricting the class of buildable systems (e.g. pre-sorted shifts with a no-op sorting module) — a design error. Generalised: every detail exposed in a fixed interface shrinks the set of systems buildable without changing it; commitment enables independent work, over-specification forces renegotiation. Complex table-format interfaces required joint development; abstract interfaces (function names, parameter counts and types) let independent work begin much earlier.
- **Baldwin and Clark.** Designs split into visible *design rules* and hidden modules; Baldwin's consequence: modules "need not be subject to unified governance, hierarchy, or direct authority — except for following the rules". Craig's reading: adherence to a fixed design rule is a substitute for governance — but only while fixed and known; a changeable rule is an object of governance, and coordination costs return as anticipation, lobbying or hedging. Module boundaries are "thin crossing points": entrants "only need to build a better module".
- **Where effort goes instead.** With contestable rules, influence over the architecture becomes an asset. Module returns are incremental and competed away; rule returns are step functions — a clause only your module satisfies "removes your competitors' investment from the board". Three arrangements set the key ratio: a proprietary sponsor revises cheaply; a committee adopting by vote makes rule change cheap for anyone who can field a delegation; a body demanding multiple working interoperating implementations makes rule change expensive while building stays free.
- **Seven hundred people in a room.** In June 1992 at Kobe, facing address exhaustion, the internet's architecture board drafted a proposal to adopt an OSI-derived protocol — eight revisions in under a fortnight. The community "did not debate the technical merits. It revolted": the board "had no right to make such a decision alone"; one participant called it a constitutional crisis; at the July Cambridge meeting roughly seven hundred people demanded the new Internet Society intervene; the board backed down. Vint Cerf stripped to an "IP on Everything" T-shirt; David Clark: "we reject kings, presidents, and voting; we believe in rough consensus and running code." Lessons: a change to a visible design rule lands on every module at once; the crisis was about who decides ("strains in the technical architecture prompted strains in the organisational architecture"); and the resolution made rule change *expensive* (running code), not better-governed.
- **The same derivation, in 2010.** On BitcoinTalk, 17 June 2010, Satoshi Nakamoto — apparently ignorant of this literature — reasoned from the premise that once version 0.1 shipped the core design was "set in stone for the rest of its lifetime". He rejected special-purpose transaction types (each needing code and data fields whether used or not) as ending in "an explosion of special cases", and instead generalised: script as a predicate the network evaluates, nodes understanding it only far enough to check the sender's conditions. Escrow, bonded contracts, third-party arbitration and multi-party signature "had to be designed in at the beginning". That is information hiding arrived at independently, under Parnas's constraint, avoiding Parnas's error. Caveat: this shows fixity was the design premise, not that the design was correct.
- **The strongest objection, which is not his.** Farrell and Saloner (1988) found committees outperform market standardisation even after pricing in speed; their 1985 paper named *excess inertia* — entrapment on inferior standards — with the US National Bureau of Standards' refusal to write computer interface standards as a motivating example. Three reconciliations: they model coordination among uncommitted parties, not revision under sunk commitment (a distinction he notes is theirs); their welfare result excludes the case where private return to controlling a standard exceeds its social coordination value — precisely the rent-seeking case; and their closing caveat is his variable: cheap reversibility can make committee coordination not worth its price.
- **Who actually pays.** Bresnahan and Greenstein named edge activity *co-invention* — users' experimentation, frequently the real bottleneck in progress. In the mainframe-to-client/server shift, adoption sequence tracked co-invention *costs*, not benefits, blocking spillovers. When Novell moved NetWare onto TCP/IP in 1998 it shipped compatibility drivers and migration agents, yet conceded there was no single migration utility — the destruction of accumulated, non-transferable understanding "could not be priced down very far".
- He appends a full source list (Boudreau 2010; Parnas 1972; Baldwin 2019; Russell 2006; Farrell & Saloner 1985, 1988; Bresnahan & Greenstein 1996; Nakamoto 2010; Novell AppNotes 1998–99), stating every source was read and adverse findings reported rather than omitted.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Layered: an empirical anchor (Boudreau's fivefold-versus-twenty-per-cent contrast), a design-theoretic mechanism (Parnas's information hiding; Baldwin and Clark's visible rules and hidden modules), a historical case study of governance failure and resolution (Kobe 1992), a textual exhibit of the same derivation made independently (Satoshi's post), and honest engagement with the strongest formal counter-model (Farrell and Saloner), which he narrows rather than dismisses. The unifying move recasts "fixity versus flexibility" as a relative-cost variable: rule-change cost against build-at-the-edge cost.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- **Fixity was the design premise**, not a later rationalisation: Satoshi's own words — "set in stone for the rest of its lifetime" — frame later base-layer evolution as departure from the design.
- **Generality was designed in so the base need never change** — general predicate script (escrow, bonded contracts, arbitration, multi-party signature) against removed functionality and special-case governance: "an explosion of special cases" is the rejected path.
- **Committee governance makes rule change cheap and invites capture** — a body adopting by vote "with no requirement that anything be built first" versus the internet's answer: make rule change expensive via running code.
- **Base-layer changes tax edge builders.** "When a base layer moves, the party who pays is the co-inventor at the edge" — in destroyed, non-transferable understanding (Bresnahan/Greenstein; Novell 1998).
- **Specialisation requires still ground.** "Where the ground does not move, committing five years to becoming the best in the world at one thing is a bet on your own competence. Where it might move, the same commitment is a bet on a committee."
- **"Open" is not one axis**: access at the edge multiplies innovation; devolving control of the base does almost nothing.

## Notable quotes
- "Opening the edge multiplied the innovation rate. Opening the base did almost nothing."
- "A rule that might change is not a substitute for governance; it is an object of governance."
- "Set the base. Then go and build something at the edge, where the work is — and where, on the only decent measurement we have, the innovation runs five times faster."
- Satoshi Nakamoto: the core design was "set in stone for the rest of its lifetime"; special-purpose types end in "an explosion of special cases".
