# Who Shall Keep the Keys?
**Date:** 2026-06-03 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/who-shall-keep-the-keys
**Subtitle:** On what the trustless machine and the scarce file do to the institutions of trust — the state, the firm, the law, the commons — and why abolishing the middleman is not the same as abolishing power

## Core thesis
Trustless coordination and engineered digital scarcity touch the load-bearing walls of the social order, because most institutions — the state register, the bank ledger, the court, the notary, the clearing house, the platform — exist precisely to economise on scarce trust. The consequences run through law (from commands enforced after the fact to rules enforced in advance), the firm (a redrawn boundary and redistributed trust-rents), money (a strike at the state's deepest monopoly), and property (enclosure reopened as a political choice). Above all: abolishing the trusted third party does not abolish power but relocates it — to those who write the code, hold the keys, make the hardware, and run the infrastructure — and the task is to make that relocated power visible, accountable, and chosen.

## Key arguments and claims
- The economics of trust: Coase (1937) explained the firm as a device to economise on the costs of using the market, largely the costs of arranging trust; North (1990) cast institutions as uncertainty-reducers; Fukuyama (1995) showed prosperity tracks the supply of trust itself.
- Law: self-enforcing rules (Szabo 1997; Reidenberg's lex informatica 1998; Lessig 1999; Werbach & Cornell's "Contracts Ex Machina" 2017; De Filippi & Wright's "Rule of Code" 2018) abolish the gap between rule and enforcement — and with it discretion, equity, efficient breach, fair use, and appeal. The vagueness of human law is how it breathes; a self-executing rule does what it was told in situations never contemplated, and the oracle problem returns trust "wearing the mask of a data feed".
- The firm and the rent: businesses that sell trust (escrow agents, clearing houses, registrars, platforms) lose their toll — but rent is relocated, not abolished, flowing to protocol authors, governance key-holders, infrastructure operators, and hardware makers. The middleman has changed costume, not vanished.
- Money: Hayek's Denationalisation of Money (1976) argued for competing private currencies; May's Crypto Anarchist Manifesto (1988) and Hughes's Cypherpunk's Manifesto (1993) made money beyond state reach a creed — and, to their credit, foresaw the criminal use. The same instrument disarms tyrant and democrat alike: no stealth debasement or frozen dissidents, but also no countercyclical policy, no sanctions, no tracing of genuine crime.
- Property and the commons: by Demsetz's logic the intangible will now be enclosed. Hardin's tragedy of the commons (1968) is answered by Ostrom's Governing the Commons (1990) — real communities govern commons sustainably without fence or state; Boyle's "second enclosure movement" (2003) names the public domain as the casualty. Since a copy's marginal cost is zero, every toll above zero turns away a willing user — a static loss to be weighed, good by good, against the dynamic gain of paying creators.
- Politics: Freeman's "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" (1972) supplies the key analogy — abolishing formal structure renders power informal, unaccountable, and invisible. "Trustless" redistributes trust to chip-makers, code-authors, and key-holders, who are harder to hold to account than a bank because they answer to no charter. The blade has two edges: attestation hardware can guarantee an honest election count or guarantee that your device obeys someone other than you; and rules embedded in architecture are stickier than statutes.
- The prescription: recognise the choices as political; deliberately preserve the slack the old systems preserved by accident (fair use, resale, inheritance, the public domain); and bind the new sovereigns with transparency, contestability, exit, and voice.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Institutional economics and political theory (Coase, North, Fukuyama, Ostrom, Freeman) applied to cryptographic capabilities, with a conservation-of-power heuristic: power, like matter, is moved rather than destroyed. He refuses both refusal and rapture, holding each liberation and its cost in both hands.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contra cypherpunk rapture (May, Hughes): trustless money disarms the lawful sovereign as well as the despot; a serious society must decide how much monetary power to place beyond reach.
- Contra "code is law" absolutism: perfect enforcement abolishes equity, mercy, efficient breach and fair use — the slack in which much liberty has lived.
- Contra decentralisation rhetoric: the word "decentralised" can serve as the smokescreen behind which an unaccountable elite establishes hegemony; stake-weighted governance is power weighted by wealth.
- Contra the equation of the trustless with the trustworthy: removing the designated trustee redistributes trust to less visible parties.
