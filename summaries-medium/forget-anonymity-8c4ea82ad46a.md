# Forget anonymity.
**Date:** 2019-04-10 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/forget-anonymity-8c4ea82ad46a
**Subtitle:** The Fifth Anti-Money Laundering Directive ("MLD5") was published in the Official Journal of the EU on 19th June, 2018, and comes into…

## Core thesis
The EU's Fifth Anti-Money Laundering Directive (MLD5), in force from 20 January 2020, extends AML/CTF obligations to virtual-currency exchanges and custodian wallet providers, which Craig reads as "the beginning of the end for anonymous cryptocurrencies". Because a cryptocurrency only has value if it can be exchanged and integrated with merchants, wallets and payment systems, regulation of those chokepoints will sideline anonymity-focused coins such as Monero. Bitcoin, he argues, was deliberately designed to work within existing law — "private but not anonymous" — and so needs no legislative change to survive.

## Key arguments and claims
- MLD5 was published 19 June 2018 and comes into force 20 January 2020; it "strengthened" the EU AML/CTF framework and "clarified" the provisions around cryptocurrency.
- MLD5 defines virtual currency as any digital representation of value accepted as a means of exchange that can be transferred, stored and/or traded electronically — explicitly a monetary unit "not guaranteed and issued by a central bank or public authority", excluding legally designated currency.
- The directive captures "custodian wallet providers" — any entity safeguarding private cryptographic keys for customers, including threshold, backup and recovery services — which "encapsulates most of the applications, every provider such as Coinbase, and exchanges".
- Beneficial-ownership registers are extended to trusts and must be set up by March 2020, building on MLD4 tax provisions — Craig notes he predicted this.
- Anonymous coins cannot escape through jurisdiction shopping: exchange services between virtual currencies are now covered, and "without the ability to integrate merchant services, custodial wallets, and payment systems, a cryptocurrency has no value".
- The US already mirrors this: he quotes Reuben Grinberg's paper on the Bank Secrecy Act and FinCEN, noting "FinCEN has power to prevent US financial institutions from dealing with institutions that deal with Bitcoin, which would essentially outlaw it in the US".
- Liberty Reserve is his proof of extraterritorial reach: based in Costa Rica (not a FATF member), yet Costa Rican authorities still cooperated with the US to shut it down. "Simply not being located within the US will not save anonymous cryptocurrencies."
- Even exchanges that warn criminal users off remain subject to US law where money laundering or illicit purchases (e.g. drug sales) are suspected.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Craig reasons as a regulatory literalist: he reads the directive's definitions closely, maps them onto real market infrastructure (Coinbase, custodial wallets, exchanges), and identifies the economic chokepoint — exchangeability — through which law reaches otherwise "decentralised" systems. He supports the argument with historical enforcement precedent (Liberty Reserve) and a cited secondary source (Grinberg on the BSA/FinCEN), framing regulation not as a threat to Bitcoin but as a vindication of its design choices. The rhetorical mode is triumphalist and dismissive ("Sorry Monero… Bye bye.").

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the era's "regulation can't touch decentralised protocols" orthodoxy: Craig argues regulation never needs to touch the protocol — capturing exchanges and custodians is sufficient to kill a coin's value.
- Contradicts the cypherpunk premium on anonymity (Monero, and the privacy-enhancement direction of BTC development): he recasts anonymity as a design flaw that guarantees being "sidelined altogether", and privacy-with-traceability as the intended Bitcoin property.
- Contradicts the jurisdictional-arbitrage thesis common in crypto ("just incorporate offshore"): the Liberty Reserve precedent shows US/FATF cooperation reaches non-member states.
