# Account and Transfer Systems.
**Date:** 2018-12-22 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/account-and-transfer-systems-5f713649f158
**Subtitle:** Most payment systems today are based on the deposit of funds and the transfer of credit from one party's account in a bank or similar…

## Core thesis
A doctrinal précis of US funds-transfer law (U.C.C. Article 4A) establishing that conventional payments are transfers of *bank credit* between accounts, structured as chains of payment orders between intermediaries. Craig's payoff is that Bitcoin exchanges and custodial wallets are exactly such account-based systems — because users do not hold their own keys, they sit inside the existing legal machinery of intermediated funds transfer rather than outside it.

## Key arguments and claims
- Most payment systems work by "the deposit of funds and the transfer of credit from one party's account in a bank or similar organisation to another"; under U.C.C. Article 4A nomenclature for wholesale wire transfers (rendered "wholesale wine transfers" in the post), bank credit moves from the "Originator's" account to the "Beneficiary's" account.
- Article 4A categorises payment instructions via two classes of action: the "funds transfer" and the "payment order."
- "Funds Transfer" (U.C.C. §4A-104(a)) is the generic term for "the process, procedure, and collected series of transactions" that together constitute the intended transfer of bank credit.
- "Payment Order" (§4A-103(a)(1)) is an instruction by one party to a receiving party directing it to process part of the funds transfer — so "a 'funds transfer' typically will be comprised of a series of one or more 'payment orders.'"
- "Beneficiary" (§4A-103(a)(2)) is the "person to be paid by the beneficiary's bank"; "Originator" (§4A-104(1)) is "the sender of the first payment order in a funds transfer."
- The balance-sheet mechanics: before the exchange, originator and beneficiary hold account balances at their respective banks; the transfer "reduces the liability of the Originator's Bank to the originator, and creates a corresponding increase in the liability of the Beneficiary's Bank to the Beneficiary" — payments are reallocations of bank liabilities, not movements of stuff.
- Application to Bitcoin: "In Bitcoin, we see such systems in exchanges and custodial wallets, where the account holder does not maintain direct control of their private keys, and hence is subject to the security and controls that are in place at the provider site."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Textbook statutory exegesis: he defines the operative legal terms from the Uniform Commercial Code in sequence, builds the account-based model of payment as a shift in interbank liabilities, and then maps the crypto case onto it by analogy. The method is classificatory — show that the supposedly novel phenomenon (custodial crypto) instantiates an existing legal category, so the existing rules apply.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Undercuts the mainstream-crypto framing of exchanges and custodial wallets as a new kind of institution outside legacy payments law: on Craig's account they are ordinary account-based intermediaries, and "not your keys, not your coins" is not a slogan but a legal characterisation — the custodial user holds a claim on the provider, like any bank depositor.
- Implicitly contradicts the claim that Bitcoin per se eliminates intermediaries: his distinction reserves peer-to-peer properties for direct key control, making intermediation a consequence of custody arrangements rather than of the protocol.
- Era context: published amid the 2018–19 wave of exchange failures and hacks, the piece supports his recurring argument that custodial crypto belongs inside money-transmission and banking law rather than in a regulatory vacuum.
