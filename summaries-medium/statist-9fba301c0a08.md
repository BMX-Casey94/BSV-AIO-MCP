## Core thesis
Anarchy is not libertarianism — it is a collectivist, fundamentally Marxian attack on capitalism dressed in the language of freedom. Following Mises, Craig argues that capitalism requires law and a limited state whose core function is protecting private property and peace, and that Bitcoin serves this order as "an immutable evidence system" providing the rule of law universally. He embraces the "statist" label thrown at him by anarchists, placing himself in the company of Mises, Adam Smith, Jefferson, Franklin and Washington.

## Key arguments and claims
- Opens with Mises's question from *Liberalism* (1927): "In opposing government interventions, is liberalism not implicitly bound to advocate some form of anarchy in the end?" — and answers with Mises that "Liberalism is not anarchism, nor has it anything whatsoever to do with anarchism."
- "There is no such thing as anarcho-capitalism, there is socialism." It was Marx who sought the abolition of the state, so anarcho-capitalism is "Marxian theory in another form" attacking true capitalism.
- Rothbard's private-court anarchism is self-refuting: "Crime is not contractual." Competing courts let the accused shop for jurisdiction, so "the rich and the powerful exceed the right of the victim for redress" — an encoded axiom of "Might is right." He likens Rothbard's system to the Hatfields and McCoys' endless retribution feuds.
- Codification requires a legislature; adjudication requires a judiciary — and both are government, so the anarchist who calls for private courts has reinvented the state while denying it. "If all members of a society subscribe to different and possibly conflicting courts and laws — then there is no law."
- A stateless society ends in corporate oppression: people will band together and "sacrifice personal liberty and freedom to a baron or overlord for protection," and corporations will buy and own courts. "The individual is always going to be less capable than a corporate body."
- Monopoly "exists in every system, it is not something to be expunged completely, but something to be managed" for optimal efficiency — opposing all monopoly on principle is itself "opposed to freedom."
- Government is like a house: left unmaintained it rots. Corruption is stopped by vigilance and participation, not abolition: "It is not the abolition of the state that will set us free, it is the involvement of people in the state."
- Progressives and anarchist "voluntarists" are a collectivist pincer movement: "the anarchist is truly a collectivist at heart," repackaging the same bitter pill.
- Plato's *Republic* (434a): justice is "the having and doing of one's own and what belongs to oneself"; hierarchy is natural, enforced equality is unjust, and "Luck itself is created through hard work."
- Bitcoin's role: "Bitcoin is an immutable evidence system, it provides the rule of law universally to all. It applies to people, corporations, and the state" — but a framework alone is insufficient; "all people need to be vigilant."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He argues by authority and quotation, building the case on Mises's classical liberalism and Plato's conception of justice, then runs a reductio on Rothbard: any mechanism for punishing crime reintroduces government, so anarcho-capitalism contradicts itself. The method is dichotomous — collectivism versus rule-of-law liberalism — with labels aggressively reframed (statist as compliment, anarchist as socialist). Bitcoin is slotted into the political theory as evidentiary infrastructure for honest government rather than as an escape from it.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Directly assaults the crypto community's founding mythology: the cypherpunk/Rothbardian ideal of Bitcoin as a tool to route around or abolish the state. Craig calls that tradition socialist, not libertarian.
- Contradicts the "Bitcoin is anti-bank, anti-government" narrative common to the post-Silk Road era — he insists Bitcoin "applies to people, corporations, and the state" and strengthens law.
- Rejects the "code is law" substitution of private ordering for public law: private competing jurisdictions produce "Might is right," not freedom.
- Inverts the standard insult economy of crypto Twitter: being called a "statist" is worn as proof of alignment with Mises, Smith and the American founders.
