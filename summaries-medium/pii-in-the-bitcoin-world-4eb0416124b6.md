# PII in the Bitcoin World
**Date:** 2019-08-01 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/pii-in-the-bitcoin-world-4eb0416124b6
**Subtitle:** PII stands for personally identifiable information. Right now, I see and hear many people talking about just how easy it would be to take…

## Core thesis
The Medium body is a short excerpt; the full essay continues on craigwright.net (filed under "Law & Regulation"). Craig argues that the debate over personally identifiable information (PII) is misframed: PII protection is not about privacy in the abstract but about preventing unauthorised use of your identity — above all fraudulent credit applications. The real security question is not whether criminals can buy your data (cheaply), but whether they can actually steal money from you; relying on the secrecy of your data ("obscurity") as your only defence deserves no sympathy.

## Key arguments and claims
- People talk about "how easy it would be to take and use PII" and that it "would sell for cents in the dollar" — Craig's response is blunt: "WELL, WHO CARES!"
- If your entire financial security strategy is to "hide your head in the sand and leave trust to obscurity, then you deserve all that such an approach brings with it" — obscurity is not a security control.
- "The most commonly missed issue in security is WHY" — practitioners fail to investigate "the cause and need" behind protecting PII before designing defences.
- PII protection "is not about privacy, it is about stopping unauthorised applications and changes to your credit file" — i.e. stopping people "applying for a credit card or a home loan in your name", with credit-card fraud the main issue.
- The operative risk question is therefore transactional, not informational: "the issue is not whether a criminal can buy your information, but rather if they can steal money from you."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Craig uses a threat-modelling move: define the actual harm (unauthorised credit/financial actions in your name) before valuing the asset (raw PII). This inverts the common instinct — he treats data secrecy as a weak, low-value control and redirects attention to whether the harm is even executable. The rhetorical mode is deliberately abrasive and provocative ("I may seem uncaring and I may come across as cruel"), framing security as personal responsibility backed by process rather than panic over data markets.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the privacy-absolutist orthodoxy (strong in 2019 crypto culture) that secrecy of personal data is the foundational defence: Craig calls that "trust to obscurity" and dismisses it as a non-strategy.
- Implicitly undercuts the "data is the new oil / data breach apocalypse" framing common in infosec discourse: data being purchasable for "cents in the dollar" is irrelevant if the fraud it enables is what must actually be stopped.
- Aligns with his broader anti-anonymity stance: PII is framed as a fraud-prevention and credit-integrity matter (a law/regulation concern), not a cypherpunk privacy right.
