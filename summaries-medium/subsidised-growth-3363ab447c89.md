# Subsidised Growth
**Date:** 2019-07-29 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/subsidised-growth-3363ab447c89
**Subtitle:** When I started the Bitcoin network, the Bitcoin-Qt client was designed to both mine and act as a wallet. As such, it was far from perfect…

## Core thesis
An excerpt of a longer essay that continues on craigwright.net (filed under his "economics" category). Craig recounts Bitcoin's launch economics from a first-person Satoshi perspective: the Bitcoin-Qt client combined mining and wallet functions; IP-to-IP payments — later removed — were essential to making it a cash system; and because coins had no value in 2009 and no commercial player would run a distributed database competitively, the block subsidy was designed to pay early users to bootstrap the network.

## Key arguments and claims
- First-person authorship claim: "When I started the Bitcoin network, the Bitcoin-Qt client was designed to both mine and act as a wallet" — alpha software, "far from perfect."
- Removed features matter: "key peer aspects of cash such as in the form of IP-to-IP transactions that have been removed form an essential part in making it a cash system."
- The 2009 reality: "practically no value at any point to Bitcoin other than a promise of the future."
- Early users bore real costs — downloading the entire blockchain, hard-drive and network capacity, CPU heat and electricity — "with no promise of anything past the dream of Bitcoin being valuable one day."
- Consequence of worthlessness: "many people failed to adequately protect the Bitcoin that they were mining" — his explanation for early lost coins.
- The design problem: "there was no way I could see to bring on a large commercial player who acted in competition with others and to convince them to run a distributed database in competition at the time."
- The solution: "the people using Bitcoin acted as a paid system to allow the system to bootstrap" — the subsidy as a deliberate, temporary bootstrapping payment.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Economic design-history: reconstruct the incentive problem at launch (no commercial miner would competitively run a distributed database for a worthless token), then present the block subsidy as the engineered solution — paying users to bootstrap until the system could stand alone. The method is first-person authorial recollection fused with incentive analysis; the rhetorical payload is that he, as designer, knows what the subsidy was for, and that later removals (IP-to-IP) damaged the cash design.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the "immaculate conception" telling of Bitcoin's launch as purely organic growth — he describes a calculated paid-bootstrap mechanism for a valueless token.
- Against the store-of-value/hodl orthodoxy: he emphasises cash mechanics (IP-to-IP payments) as essential, implying their removal by later BTC developers degraded Bitcoin-as-cash.
- Feeds his wider security-economics argument that the subsidy is temporary scaffolding to be replaced by fee revenue from transaction volume — against the BTC small-block fee-market model.
- Era context: eight months after the BCH/BSV split, this supports the BSV position that later developers deviated from the original design.
