# Stable by design
**Date:** 2018-09-10 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/stable-by-design-e967b93dc147
**Subtitle:** We have a fundamental flaw within the developer led model of Bitcoin. This problem is the same in many aspects of software development…

## Core thesis
The developer-led model of perpetual protocol change is Bitcoin's fundamental flaw: money is critical infrastructure and must be boring, stable plumbing. The base protocol should be locked — "set the system in stone" — patched only for critical issues, with every change treated as an economic risk calculation rather than a fashion-driven pivot. The market wants 20–30 years of predictable sound money, not "cool" features that pad developers' CVs; Ethereum's pivot culture is held up as the anti-model.

## Key arguments and claims
- "We have a fundamental flaw within the developer led model of Bitcoin" — Silicon Valley app mythology ("Pivot and change and pump and alter") turned the concept of stable money into "the 'Shit-Show' of Ethereum".
- "The design of a protocol is a function of the system and organisation it is designed to deliver and it is always an economic function. Bitcoin, is first and foremost cash."
- The goal is "stability and the creation of a platform and protocol that others can use to create Apps and high level languages and use-cases that need an immutable ledger" — decentralised-app hype is a myth the industry helps propagate.
- "Bitcoin is money, and the end goal is to make the protocol as boring as the global plumbing it reflects"; patching, scaling and policy controls matter more than "cool toys".
- Infosec evidence: most breaches come from unpatched systems, off-whitelist applications and poor practice — not zero-days. "Old attacks work. New attacks cost money." APTs and zero-days are "the proverbial nuclear weapons. Once they are used, they are depleted."
- Critical-infrastructure analogy: systems believed "OK as they are" and left unpatched are the vulnerable ones; Bitcoin needs "to lock the base protocol and then patch and monitor it for any needed change" because "Bitcoin is money and that makes it a form of critical infrastructure."
- Banks historically used "Greek and Roman frontages" to convey stability; Bitcoin lacks that signal. "Bitcoin needs to be plumbing."
- Developer incentives are the problem: "Developers want these as they help pad their CV. What the world wants, what the market for money wants, this is just boring, stable and sound money" with "expectations of 20 to 30 years of stability".
- Perfectionism loses: security is "an economic calculation based on risk, some of which are accepted and not all of which can be fixed". "If you aim for perfection, you aim to always lose."
- "Bitcoin works when most people see it as boring. When it is not cool. When, it is simply cash."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He imports an information-security governance frame — patch management, accepted risk, critical-infrastructure doctrine — into protocol politics, and defines protocol design as an economic function of the system's purpose (cash). Architectural semiotics (banks' classical frontages) does work as trust-signalling theory, and the opposing camp is analysed by incentives (CV-padding developers) rather than argument. A Sun Tzu epigraph frames it as knowing one's own flaws and the enemy's.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects developer-led protocol governance outright — the legitimacy BTC mainstream grants Core's ongoing soft-fork stewardship is recast as the system's "fundamental flaw".
- Against the SegWit-era culture of frequent protocol change on BTC, and wholesale against Ethereum's move-fast ethos ("Shit-Show"); the locked-base-layer position became BSV's defining doctrine two months later.
- Contradicts the innovation-maximalist crypto orthodoxy that values extensibility and rapid feature deployment (tokens, DeFi primitives): the base layer should be so stable it is boring, with complexity pushed to platforms built on top.
- Notably, the critique cuts at both camps in September 2018: at BTC Core for changing the protocol, and at BCH actors contemplating continued experimentation — foreshadowing the November split over protocol restoration.
