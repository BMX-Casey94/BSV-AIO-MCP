# The Scoreboard Is Not the Game: Money, Measurement, and the Collapse of Monetary Understanding
**Date:** 2026-06-07 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-scoreboard-is-not-the-game-money
**Subtitle:** Why Menger, Mises, Hayek, and Distributed Digital Cash Expose the Modern Confusion Between Wealth, Value, and Numbers

## Core thesis
Modern monetary discourse commits a category error: it confuses money with wealth, numerical magnitude with value, and measurement with reality. Money is the calculative and informational institution through which wealth is compared and coordinated — and any monetary system whose rules can be altered by developers, committees or administrators is governed money, not decentralised money, because mutability converts money from law into policy and from measurement into power.

## Key arguments and claims
- The Darcy paradox: Austen's £10,000 per annum (Pride and Prejudice, 1813) signalled immense command over land, labour and rents to contemporary readers; moderns misread it because they evaluate nominal figures rather than purchasing power — "the diagnostic test for monetary understanding".
- Ontology of wealth: a farm, factory or trained surgeon is wealth; a monetary balance is only a claim upon production. Smith (1776) refuted the mercantilist version of the same error; today's equivalents are worship of reserves, indices, nominal GDP and housing valuations. "Printing claims upon bread does not bake bread."
- Menger (1892): money emerges through exchange as the most saleable good — an emergent solution to indirect exchange, not a legislative gift or metaphysical substance called "value".
- Mises: the regression theorem ties purchasing power to prior market valuations; the 1920 calculation argument shows that without market prices for capital goods, rational allocation among heterogeneous inputs is impossible. Money compresses heterogeneity into calculable form.
- Inflation is "a degradation of economic language", not merely rising prices: an unstable unit introduces noise into every contract, wage, pension and investment — "epistemic vandalism", like engineers building aircraft with rulers whose length changes by parliamentary debate.
- Hayek (1945): prices are compressed packets of dispersed knowledge (the tin example); corrupting money corrupts the language of prices. Hayek (1976): monetary competition disciplines issuers because users can abandon inferior currencies.
- Nominal society: Kuznets (1934) warned against conflating national income with welfare; asset booms can reflect monetary expansion rather than productivity; house-price inflation converts "future buyers into debt servants" — "The scoreboard rises; the game becomes uglier."
- Distributed digital cash: replication is not autonomy. "A monetary system in which developers can change the protocol is not meaningfully outside governance. It has governance by developers."
- "Not your keys, not your coins" is incomplete: control over the rules defining what a signed transaction means matters more than key possession — "Possession without stable rules is not sovereignty. It is tenancy under technical administration."
- Maintenance versus revision: a bridge can be maintained without redefining the metre; a protocol can be maintained without granting implementers authority over the monetary constitution. "Sound money must be boring to the ambitious."
- Four tests for sound money: Mengerian (emergence through exchangeability), Misesian (supports calculation), Hayekian (transmits dispersed knowledge), constitutional (rules stable enough that users need not price insiders' ambitions).

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Austrian monetary theory — Mengerian emergence, Misesian calculation, Hayekian knowledge — fused with Randian epistemology (symbol versus substance, "the weaponization of unreality against the producer") and constitutional political economy (rules versus discretion). The literary Darcy example functions as a diagnostic device for the nominalist confusion he then traces through inflation statistics, GDP, asset markets and digital-money governance.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects "decentralisation" as node or machine count: "The economic issue is not how many spectators hold copies of the rulebook. The issue is who can revise the rulebook while the game is being played."
- Developer-led protocol evolution is governance — "policy wearing the mask of protocol" — so a mutable system cannot claim "the full moral and economic status of rule-based money".
- Rejects the hoarding/speculation redefinition of Bitcoin: Nakamoto's peer-to-peer electronic cash identifies money as a transactional system; "Digital cash must be cash."
- A system advertised as rule-bound but operated as committee-bound "commits a fraud against the concept of sound money".
