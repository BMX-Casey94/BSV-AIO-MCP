# Transparency Is Not Centralisation: NAR, DAR, and the Legal Architecture of Blockchain Governance
**Date:** 2026-04-11 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/transparency-is-not-centralisation
**Subtitle:** On court-order compliance, the CLARITY Act's maturity test, and why published rules are the opposite of discretionary control

## Core thesis
A former BSV Association employee's claim that the Network Access Rules (NAR) and Digital Asset Recovery (DAR) framework make BSV a centrally controlled security is wrong on every material point of law. DAR is transparent, judicially mediated enforcement of property rights — not discretionary off-chain control — because it can only be triggered by a court order valid under English or Swiss law. Applied consistently, the tests invoked (CLARITY Act, Howey) raise far graver questions for BTC Core and Ethereum than for BSV.

## Key arguments and claims
- The NAR is a published unilateral contract: offer to an indefinite class, acceptance by performance (Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893] 1 QB 256). The Association's documentation cites Clarke v Earl of Dunraven (The Satanita) [1897] AC 59 — yacht racers "sailed into" contract by entering under published rules. Craig concedes the analogy is "not perfectly congruent", but the operative point holds: rules are published before participation, and no miner is compelled to join. The NAR is also bilateral, constraining the steward as much as participants.
- DAR operates in four steps: plaintiff litigates ownership; obtains a freeze order or injunction; a designated notary translates the order into machine-readable form broadcast via the Blacklist Manager; miners activate the freeze upon consensus among honest nodes. Foreign orders must first be recognised in England or Switzerland (Hague Convention 2005; Adams v Cape Industries plc [1990] Ch 433). Miner compliance is contractually obligated but not coerced — a refusing miner breaches the NAR and risks orphaned blocks, like a bank employee refusing a Mareva injunction.
- Procedural reality defeats the "rapid seizure" framing: English freezing injunctions (CPR Part 25; Mareva; American Cyanamid) require a good arguable case, real risk of dissipation, a return date within 7–14 days for ex parte orders, and a cross-undertaking in damages; US practice (FRCP 65; Winter v NRDC) is comparable. Trials run 12–36 months; cross-border recognition adds 12–24 months; costs run £50,000–£150,000 for a contested injunction and £1–5 million for full recovery — economic gravity towards high-value claims.
- CLARITY Act (H.R. 3633): a "mature blockchain system" is one "not controlled by any person or group of persons under common control" (§101(31)). Court-order compliance is not "control" in the securities-law sense — else every regulated financial instrument would fail — and the 20% threshold concerns token ownership, not infrastructure funding. The protocol itself is locked — "set in stone" — and cannot be altered by any party, including the Association.
- BTC Core is the unexamined centralisation: roughly five or six maintainers hold commit access (after "sedited" was added, January 2026); they shipped SegWit (BIP 141), RBF (BIP 125), kept the 1MB cap and activated Taproot via Speedy Trial — with no published governance framework and undisclosed sponsor funding (Blockstream, Chaincode Labs, HRF, Square). Angela Walch's "In Code(rs) We Trust" (OUP, 2019) reaches the same fiduciary conclusion independently; Craig's thesis argues BTC Core satisfies the Partnership Act 1890, s. 1(1) definition.
- Ethereum is worse again: Buterin — "The person deciding the new EF leadership team is me" (January 2025); the 2016 DAO fork reversed completed transactions (the DAO then held 14% of all ETH) with no court order or published process; the Merge replaced the consensus mechanism entirely; over 60% of staked ETH is reportedly controlled by a few teams.
- On Howey's fourth prong: under SEC v Ripple (SDNY, 2023) and Glenn W. Turner, the Association's stewardship is administrative and custodial — like ISO/IEEE/IETF standards maintenance — not the "essential managerial efforts" that drive profit, though the analysis is fact-sensitive.
- On-chain voting is the dangerous alternative: hashpower signalling lets a 30% miner veto and a 51% miner impose; two pools holding 55% can coordinate off-chain in an invisible "secret ballot" coalition; stake-weighted voting yields Bauwens' "netarchy". NAR/DAR instead offers four safeguards: external judicial trigger, fixed public rules, observable exercise, judicial reviewability.
- The decisive distinction: (1) altering consensus rules, (2) altering transaction-validity conditions, (3) enforcement action within an unchanged protocol. DAR operates exclusively in category (3); the critic conflates it with (1) and (2).

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal legal analysis: close reading of primary documents, case law and statutory text, paired with comparative institutional analysis across BSV, BTC and Ethereum. He argues like a lawyer rather than a partisan — conceding the points that have force (the chokepoint exists; Howey is fact-sensitive) and then distinguishing them.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- BTC offers theft victims no protocol-level recourse at all: "This is not a feature. It is a failure of design — a failure that DAR was built to remedy."
- The mainstream decentralisation narrative inverts reality: BTC Core's informal, unpublished developer authority is the actual concentrated control, while BSV's published, court-constrained framework is the accountable one.
- The UASF episode showed BTC's "decentralised governance" was "a fiction even on BTC's own terms" — developers circumvented miner signalling when miners declined to activate SegWit.
