# The Law of Controlled Amnesia
**Date:** 2026-06-29 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-law-of-controlled-amnesia
**Subtitle:** Cash, code, and the limits of bearer finality — when money can be followed, reclaimed, and forfeited, and why “code is law” was never true

## Core thesis
Cash is not a thing but an institution: a legally tolerated degree of bearer finality, granted because perpetual recoverability would destroy liquidity. That tolerance is scalar — strongest for small ordinary payments, collapsing as value, opacity and systemic risk rise — and digital systems inherit the structure wholesale. Blockchains do not abolish governance; they relocate it. “Code is law” fails for the same reason strong cash-finality fails: code may settle the ledger, but law settles the rights.

## Key arguments and claims
- Three organising claims: cash is tolerated bearer finality; the tolerance is scalar (a five-dollar coffee and a ten-million-dollar suitcase are not the same institution); and digital systems re-enact the negotiation.
- *Miller v Race* (1758): Lord Mansfield held a stolen bank note passed “fairly and honestly upon a valuable and bona fide consideration” is unrecoverable — a deliberate sacrifice of the owner’s claim to keep money moving.
- The scalar principle: “a small cash payment is presumptively transactional; a very large cash transfer is presumptively evidential” — already encoded in US currency transaction reports and the EU’s 2024 AML cash ceiling. Institutional economics (Coase, North, Williamson) explains why: small cash solves a transaction-cost problem; large anonymous cash creates a verification-cost problem.
- The private-law recovery ladder: proprietary recovery of identifiable notes (defeated by mixing); following and tracing (*Banque Belge v Hambrouck* [1921]); unjust enrichment (*Lipkin Gorman v Karpnale* [1991] — the Playboy Club was liable for stolen money received innocently, subject to the change-of-position defence); personal claims against the wrongdoer; and claims against recipients with notice or fault.
- The statutory crystallisation: POCA 2002 s 308 protects only the recipient “in good faith, for value and without notice” — innocence plus value plus absence of notice. *R (World Uyghur Congress) v NCA* [2024] EWCA Civ 715 held that adequate consideration somewhere in a supply chain does not cleanse criminal taint.
- The state’s parallel POCA toolkit: conviction-based confiscation, Part 5 civil recovery in rem without conviction (even against acquitted defendants), and cash seizure and forfeiture — aimed at disrupting criminal benefit, with a standing proportionality tension.
- Digital assets are property, not cash: *AA v Persons Unknown* [2019] applied the *National Provincial Bank v Ainsworth* test and found bitcoin rivalrous by software design; the Law Commission’s third category (Law Com No 412, 2023) became the Property (Digital Assets etc) Bill (introduced September 2024). “Digital cash” is a legal-governance category, not a technical one.
- Governance is everywhere: forks, upgrades, exchange delisting, validator censorship, stablecoin freezes, court orders, developer discretion. In *Tulip Trading v van der Laan* [2023] EWCA Civ 83 the Court of Appeal held it arguable that developers exercising real control owe fiduciary duties; whether “the decentralised governance of bitcoin really is a myth” is a question of fact, and “the internet is not a place where the law does not apply” (Birss LJ). No duty has yet been held to exist — the point is that the question is justiciable.
- *D’Aloia v Persons Unknown* [2024] EWHC 2342 (Ch): the first trial-level holding that USDT is property — yet the claimant, defrauded of about £2.5 million moved through fourteen hops to the Bitkub exchange, lost because his “black box” tracing evidence could not be transparently audited. Recovery is genuine but hard, and scales with control: custodial stablecoins freeze more easily than banknotes; transparent ledgers trace more easily than privacy coins.
- The governance counterweight: non-cash payments rose from 46% to 86% (2002–2022), yet around three million UK adults still rely on cash; the FCA access-to-cash regime (September 2024) requires designated firms to fill provision gaps. The right posture is calibration, not abolition.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal legal analysis built deliberately on primary sources — judgments and statutes read directly — fused with transaction-cost institutional economics. The organising device is the scalar “cashness” curve: tolerance of bearer finality decays as value and opacity rise, and every doctrine (tracing, change of position, s 308, civil recovery) sits on that curve.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Refutes “code is law” directly: technical and legal finality differ, and a technically final transfer can still generate proprietary, restitutionary, criminal, tax, fiduciary and sanctions consequences.
- Refutes bearer-finality maximalism by genealogy: even physical cash has never been absolutely final since *Miller v Race*.
- Refutes the decentralisation shield: genuine decentralisation is a question of fact for trial, not an immunity conferred by description.
- Rejects the “digital assets are unrecoverable” claim — recovery is real but proof-dependent, running through control points that answer to courts.
