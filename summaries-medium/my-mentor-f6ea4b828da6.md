# My Mentor
**Date:** 2019-04-07 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/my-mentor-f6ea4b828da6
**Subtitle:** Calvin Ayre is my friend. It becomes very hard to have friends when you have money. I have a few, and I would include Shadders and Jimmy…

## Core thesis
A tribute to Calvin Ayre as friend and mentor doubles as a rebuttal of Andrew O'Hagan's Satoshi profile and a declaration that "code is not law"; Craig then pivots to a technical proposal, "Smart Jurisdiction", showing how Bitcoin's native two-of-three multi-sig escrow can encode courts, arbitrators and regulators directly into contracts rather than abolishing them.

## Key arguments and claims
- Calvin Ayre is "in part my friend as he is also my mentor" — a billionaire who teaches him "street smarts" and "how to live" without a yacht collection; Craig's own spending spree taught him "what makes me happiest is working".
- Andrew O'Hagan's book is a "penny-store novella" that is perhaps "15%" true: "I was never paid $15 million"; O'Hagan was offered years of embedded access to watch nChain grow, "never accepted", and so wrote from "hearsay rumours and innuendo".
- Some inside his organisation "wanted to sell out and do a quick boom of patents sold to the highest bidder" — his rebuttal is that the organisation and he are "here, still... the best proof for such things is time".
- "Code is not law. Code will never be law." The "just sign a key" crowd who treat code as self-sufficient evidence and seek to "remove courts, judges, and law is a part of why I created Bitcoin... to ensure a path away from the fools".
- The Kleiman lawsuit is "a conman's attempt to falsely use a lawsuit to get money out of me"; he claims "I don't think there's one entirely accurate document out there in their list of evidence".
- Smart Jurisdiction: two-of-three multi-sig "was in the original version of Bitcoin"; escrow "was never about removing courts and jurisdiction" but lets parties "automate the selection of jurisdictional functions".
- Contract rules can be compiled into key selection: below a value threshold the "local court and magistrate key" is used; above it, "a District Court as per the rules" — selection automated by contract value.
- Oracles plus court certificates let contracts automate "administrative rules associated with civil procedure or... securities offerings in a particular jurisdiction"; alternative Merklised paths route consumer-law issues to a tribunal key and equity issues to a court-of-equity key — "all within a simplified transaction".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Personal loyalty and trust-testing serve as the epistemic frame for judging others (O'Hagan failed the trust test, Calvin passed), and time-in-market is offered as proof of good faith. The technical half reasons from original-Bitcoin primitives (2-of-3 escrow) outward: instead of treating law as an enemy of smart contracts, he treats legal rules as compilable logic — value thresholds, jurisdictional branches and Merklised alternative paths — so courts become oracle-backed keys inside script.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Directly attacks the "code is law" / "just sign a key" orthodoxy of the era, arguing cryptographic proof cannot replace courts, judges and evidence.
- Reframes smart contracts against the Ethereum/"remove the intermediaries" narrative: "We don't remove lawyers, we automate some of the functions" — contracts should incorporate regulators, arbitrators and courts, not route around them.
- Implicitly rejects the censorship-resistance-above-all ethos: dispute resolution and jurisdiction are features to be engineered into Bitcoin, not bugs to be routed around.
