# The labour fallacy of mining
**Date:** 2019-03-07 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/the-labour-fallacy-of-mining-c2c0f919784
**Subtitle:** I have noted a good number of people hold on to the same common fallacy as was promoted by Marx. Labour is not a goal, it is a means.

## Core thesis
The belief that labour is an end in itself — or that there is a fixed pie of work to distribute — is the Marxian fallacy underlying the small-block, every-miner-equal vision of Bitcoin. Craig argues the scaling debate began in 2008 (with James A. Donald), not 2015, and was always a struggle between "how do we break Bitcoin" and "Satoshi's Bitcoin": a commercial, lawful, auditable system that scales through corporate competition and will create megacorporations dwarfing Silicon Valley. Protocol immutability is absolute — any change splits off a new system — and the white paper's incentive model makes 51% attacks self-defeating.

## Key arguments and claims
- "Labour is not a goal, it is a means." There is an infinite amount of work and "no fixed labour pie"; the goal is consumption and real wealth — "We DO NOT work to work, but rather for what we can obtain," plus saving and investment for future return.
- Reductio: making more work is easy — "Salt the fields and make agriculture far less efficient, become Luddites, and smash the machines"; equalising labour leaves everyone "equally poor." Productivity, not distributed labour, creates wealth.
- "The scaling debate did not start in 2015. It started in 2008 before Bitcoin was even released" — with James A. Donald, "a closet socialist seeking utopian society with the pretence of being a libertarian," who wanted an anti-corporate system of equal miners and proposed layering "account money and chaumian money based on bitgold coins" on top (quoted verbatim).
- Design intent: Bitcoin was built for "logistics systems, EDI, payment, voting, and much more," with corporations and the state as users — "an evidentiary system that allowed monetary exchange with traceability that acted within the law." Its origin was audit simplification: at BDO he developed computer-aided audit tools (CAATs) and "was constantly surprised by the level of fraud and corporate misappropriation."
- The launch timing was personal, not ideological: Bitcoin launched when it did because "I was offered a golden handshake and could leave in December 2008," not as a response to the global financial crisis.
- "Bitcoin is not high-powered money" and "Bitcoin will create corporations that dwarf every organisation in existence in Silicon Valley" — without huge global corporations "around 6 billion people will die"; globalisation is here to stay.
- Named attacks: Hal Finney "didn't want a payment system, he wanted his crypto anarchist shit-show money"; regret that "I didn't stand up for Mike Hearn"; Peter Todd and associates pushed "decentralisation at all costs" in "Marxist and insidious rhetoric that could have come right out of the book 1984."
- Lightning is a regulator's dream, not a privacy tool: "the Bank Secrecy Act means that every single Lightning transaction will need to be recorded on an immutable ledger," and its near-100% uptime requirement makes nodes easy to track; he plans to give takedown methodologies "to government." "See here's the irony in calling me the Bond villain… James Bond works for the government."
- Incentive model per the white paper: a greedy attacker with majority hash power must choose between defrauding people and being paid; "The incentive model of a blockchain does not allow a protocol change" — any new addition splits from the network while SPV users continue on the original.
- Why 51% attacks fail: the attacker "needs to mislead users into thinking that the new protocol is Bitcoin"; at best he can double-spend his own transactions, leaving "an immutable evidence trail — which is admissible under law."
- Only miners run nodes ("a node creates a block"); merchants validate received transactions via SPV, keeping copies as receipts/invoices admissible in court, and can detect double-spends "within seconds" and "detain the person violating the law just as a shop will detain a shoplifter."
- Nodes vote on which transactions to build upon, never on protocol changes: "The reason the protocol doesn't change is that it is outside the purview of nodes to alter it." SegWit therefore "create[s] an alternative to Bitcoin," with an airdrop that misleads consumers.
- Patent count: "we have filed 692 patents... In the next five years, I expect to have over 10,000 patents filed."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He starts from subjective-value first principles (labour as means, consumption as end) and maps the small-block position onto Marx's labour theory, then switches to insider history — quoting James A. Donald's 2008 reply to the white paper as primary-source evidence that the scaling conflict predates Bitcoin's release. The white paper's incentive section is read as a game-theoretic proof that protocol changes are splits, and legal enforcement (BSA, admissible evidence) substitutes for cryptographic anonymity as the security backstop. Rhetorically it is combative: named opponents, profanity, and patent-count deterrence.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects the labour-flavoured egalitarianism of "one CPU one vote" home mining: distributing work lowers productivity; security comes from well-capitalised competing corporations.
- Rejects the canonical origin story: Bitcoin was not a response to the 2008 financial crisis but an audit-integrity project years in the making.
- Rejects Lightning as scaling: it is a record-deletion scheme that the BSA converts into a fully traceable, easily dismantled system.
- Rejects the 51%-attack-can-change-Bitcoin assumption: majority hash power cannot alter the protocol, only fork off it, and double-spends leave court-admissible evidence.
- Rejects node-democracy: merchants using SPV, not home full nodes, are the validation model Satoshi designed.
