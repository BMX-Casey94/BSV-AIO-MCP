# The Cost of Permission — core principles

- **Open admission is not governance.** Anyone may run a node or deploy a contract; that is a claim about entry. Rule changes originate with a client-development coalition, get ratified by validators and exchanges, and arrive at everyone else as a fact. Conflating the two is where the word "permissionless" does its damage.
- **Rule-changing coalitions recreate the residual claimant the design was meant to eliminate.** Specific investment plus residual control held by somebody else plus an ex post change in terms is ordinary hold-up. Governance concentrated in client teams, miners and stakers relocates the problem rather than solving it.
- **Architectural change can obsolete an entire category of capital overnight.** A clean engineering transition that revises how components fit together evaporates incumbent competence on a schedule those firms did not set. The firms that built on the protocol are consulted; they do not decide.
- **Forks are on the menu, not accidents.** Consensus supports multiple equilibria. Each rule change imposes uncounted hold-up costs on downstream firms — engineering months, written-off hardware, rebuilt products.
- **The mechanism is established; the magnitude is open.** Impairment lines confound price collapse, energy shocks and financing squeezes with protocol change. Developer activity is not a wage bill. Anyone who claims to know what a protocol transition cost the ecosystem is guessing until adaptation cost is measured as such.
- **Protocol immutability is the business guarantee that removes the hold-up.** If the rules cannot be revised after complementors have sunk chain-specific investment, residual control does not reconstitute itself. That is the institutional point of a fixed base.
