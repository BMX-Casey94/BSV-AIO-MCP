# Human rights and property
**Date:** 2018-09-12 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/human-rights-and-property-2cde4181c012
**Subtitle:** The most fundamental of human rights is the right to hold property. There are many who would argue this but the reality is to be free we…

## Core thesis
The right to hold property is the most fundamental human right, because freedom requires controlling one's life "on our own terms". Craig presents his team's multi-year work on "redeemable contracts" — tradable, encrypted, blockchain-linked property rights abstracted from the individual — as Bitcoin's true purpose: "A free system to transfer and maintain property". Crucially, the system is designed to satisfy tax authorities and AML/KYC law while preserving owner privacy.

## Key arguments and claims
- Property rights underpin all freedom: "to be free we need to be able to control our lives", which requires securing property and "the security and integrity of those rights".
- The core problem is eroding privacy: "Our lives are not private, and they are becoming less and less private over time" — and privacy is framed as "empowering the individual", not "protecting criminal acts".
- The proposed mechanism is "redeemable contracts": tradable, secure property rights "abstracted from the individual and the use", stored encrypted and "in a distributed manner across the Internet".
- Worked example — a house: ownership is divided into tradable shares linked over the blockchain to the encrypted contracts, so "you live in a house [but that] does not mean people need to know that you own it"; ownership is separated from occupation and use.
- The design explicitly complies with state requirements: it "can match the requirements of government tax authorities and AML/KYC provisions" and supports transfer in bankruptcy — it "works within the existing legal frameworks".
- Built-in corporate machinery: a secure voting system and revenue distribution; the homeowner pays market rent to the property, and a 100% owner has the money "pseudonymously returned to them".
- Capital and risk functions: owners can sell shares to raise capital, or swap e.g. 30% of their home's shares for shares in other properties, diversifying "what is arguably the largest investment they will have in their life" while rental income offsets their own rent "without interest".
- Political aim: the system lets people "in repressive countries" move funds and save, and keeps records of "depredation by governments in those countries that do not respect property rights".
- Announcement: papers documenting the research will be published "in the coming months"; "most of you don't know anything of what we've done".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Classical-liberal property theory rendered as mechanism design: he starts from a Lockean-style axiom (property as the foundation of freedom), then specifies a technical-financial structure (share tokenisation, redeemable contracts, pseudonymous rent flows) that operationalises it. Unlike cypherpunk arguments, his privacy model is deliberately compliance-compatible — privacy from the public, auditability for lawful authority — signalling his recurring "Bitcoin works within law, not against it" frame.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the crypto-anarchist privacy orthodoxy: privacy here is explicitly not for "protecting criminal acts" and the system is built to satisfy AML/KYC — the opposite of the anti-KYC, coin-mixing ethos prevalent in BTC culture.
- Contradicts "not your keys, not your coins" bearer-instrument maximalism: property rights are abstracted into legally enforceable redeemable contracts rather than resting on raw key possession alone.
- Contradicts the BTC store-of-value framing: Bitcoin's purpose is stated as property transfer and maintenance — utility infrastructure, not digital gold.
