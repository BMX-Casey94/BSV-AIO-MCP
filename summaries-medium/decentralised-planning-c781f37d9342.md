# Decentralised planning
**Date:** 2019-04-03 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/decentralised-planning-c781f37d9342
**Subtitle:** Governments cannot take into account wide-scale effects. You may ask why central planning always fails. No person, department, or other…

## Core thesis
Central planning fails because no person or department can predict society — "The market is society" — and Bitcoin's purpose is to remove that kind of concentrated power from money. The immediate provocation is a report that Peter Todd advocates raising the 21 million bitcoin limit: proof, for Craig, that Core developers seek to capture the system by altering its primary rules. BTC's capture of the Bitcoin name is "straight out of Orwell's 1984"; there is only one Bitcoin, fixed in stone, and developers who can change the protocol hold power — and with it, legal liability.

## Key arguments and claims
- The knowledge problem: "No person, department, or other body can predict the course that society will take"; "A market outcome is the view of society. The market is society. A few public servants are not society, and will never have a clue about society."
- Central planning as zombie failure: "Central planning does not work. It is a failed experiment that pops its zombie head up time and time again" — sustained by the self-interest of those in government, who "have strong incentives to ensure that government and all its cock-ups continue unabated".
- Bitcoin as power-removal: "It is not about miners or developers or exchanges; Bitcoin is a system that removes power. And it is important; the underlying basis of money should not be about control" — like gold, a stable base for measuring and transferring on which other systems can be built.
- The Todd provocation: citing a Trustnodes article ("Peter Todd Advocates Raising the 21 Million Bitcoin Limit"), Craig calls it "yet another slow insidious attempt to change the nature of what Bitcoin is" — Core developers "again seek to alter the primary purpose of Bitcoin and capture power".
- Name capture as Orwellian: "Bitcoin won't die. The reason is simple: BTC is not Bitcoin. Yes, the insidious attack has used the name in a manner that is straight out of Orwell's 1984. It does not mean new rules, it is an altered protocol, and Bitcoin is fixed and set in stone."
- No fork messiness: "With Bitcoin, there is no messiness that derives from forks, as the protocol is fixed."
- Cash and commodity, not currency: "Bitcoin isn't currency; rather, it is cash and a commodity. A currency is something issued and controlled by government" — though "a currency can be built within a tokenised system on top of Bitcoin".
- One Bitcoin only: "There are not two kinds of Bitcoin, there is one. There can only ever be one."
- Power implies liability: developers who can alter the protocol "have power over the system. It means they are liable for consequences that are covered by the scope of law" — and every change "show[s] their hand as they grasp for power and control".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Hayekian epistemics applied to protocol governance: the socialist-calculation argument (knowledge is dispersed, planning fails) is mapped directly onto developer governance — a small committee changing monetary rules is the same epistemic conceit as a planning ministry. The move converts a news hook (Todd's supply-cap comments) into evidence for a standing theory of developer power-seeking, and closes with a legal inversion: control brings liability, so the more developers change the protocol, the more they expose themselves to law.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Developer-led monetary policy: even *floating* the idea of raising the 21M cap (as Todd did, arguing the security budget requires it) is treated not as debate but as attempted capture — against the "all parameters are open to discussion" ethos.
- "BTC is Bitcoin by market consensus": flatly denied — the ticker and name are an Orwellian misappropriation; legitimacy comes from protocol fixity, not market or hash-rate verdicts.
- Forking as healthy governance: where mainstream crypto treats forks as legitimate exit/voice, forks are "messiness" that a fixed protocol eliminates; there can only ever be one Bitcoin.
- Bitcoin-as-currency framing: the "digital currency" label is rejected on definitional grounds — currency is government-issued; Bitcoin is cash/commodity, with tokenised currencies built on top.
- Developer impunity: against the "code is speech, developers aren't fiduciaries" position, protocol control is asserted to create legal liability.
