# The extra promise
**Date:** 2026-10-19 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/the-extra-promise | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that every customer can hold an authentic digital entitlement while the issuer has promised more than it can deliver. A credible scarcity claim needs evidence about the other outstanding promises, and a rule connecting that evidence to what the service will honour. The customer needs to know both what her entitlement permits and whether the issuer has created incompatible claims against the same resource.

## Key arguments and claims
- Hypothetical computing provider: 100 identical, exclusive execution slots for the same hour; each purchase guarantees one slot; the provider issues 110 distinct signed reservations, without cancellations or additional capacity. Every signature verifies and each reservation is presented only once. The problem appears when all 110 customers ask for the performance they bought. The article says an earlier page, *Private proof, scarce access* (`private-proof-scarce-access.html`), introduced the need to constrain issuance, and that this example develops the supply question across the issuer’s complete set of commitments. The reservation counts are hypothetical.
- A supply limit needs a unit and a period. In the example the unit is one exclusive execution slot in a specified hour. A reservation for tomorrow, a place in a queue and a best-efforts request belong to different commitments; combining them under a single token count would conceal the condition the customer cares about. If 100 reservations are issued, 20 are validly cancelled and 20 new reservations are sold, there have been 120 issuance events but only 100 outstanding reservations. A transfer between customers should preserve that outstanding total. A replacement credential should preserve the underlying claim while making the superseded credential ineffective. The article would reconcile opening obligations, new obligations and valid releases for each resource and time period. The rules must define when a release takes effect and how disputed cancellations are treated.
- Scott A. Crosby and Dan S. Wallach, *Efficient Data Structures for Tamper-Evident Logging*, USENIX Security 2009, Sections 2–3; the article links to https://static.usenix.org/events/sec09/tech/full_papers/crosby.pdf. The article says the paper studies an untrusted logger challenged by auditors, and that its tree-based construction supports compact proofs that a recorded event remains present and that a later history agrees with an earlier commitment, under the paper’s cryptographic and auditing assumptions. Applied here, a customer could retain evidence connecting her purchase to a particular published history, and an auditor could check continuity and reconstruct the outstanding total from the disclosed events and rules. The article says the application still needs its own event format, authorisation checks and accounting logic. A log entry saying that a cancellation occurred is evidence of a recorded assertion; whether the cancellation validly released the obligation requires the corresponding authority. An issuer might produce a perfectly consistent history containing 110 reservations: the history has made the excess visible, while preventing that excess, allocating the shortfall and compensating affected customers remain separate responsibilities.
- Operational example, which the article attributes to Google’s firsthand report of 18 September 2015, https://security.googleblog.com/2015/09/improved-digital-certificate-security.html. The article says Google reported discovering an unauthorised Thawte-branded Extended Validation precertificate for its domains through Certificate Transparency logs, said the issuance arose during an internal testing process, and updated Chrome’s revocation metadata. The article calls this a documented detection episode in certificate infrastructure. It establishes neither the effectiveness of every transparency deployment nor the reliability of the proposed reservation market. Its relevance, as the article states it, is that an organisation outside the issuer could observe an issuance it had not authorised and respond.
- RFC 9162, *Certificate Transparency Version 2.0* (December 2021), which the article describes as an Experimental specification, Sections 1, 2.1, 4.8, 8 and 11; link https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9162.html. The article says it distinguishes a signed promise of future inclusion from a proof that an entry is included, requires each log to commit to a Maximum Merge Delay, and assigns monitoring and auditing roles. It identifies inconsistent views served to different parties as a threat; cross-client comparison mechanisms are outside its specification. The 2015 episode predates this version.
- Suppose the provider logs 100 reservations and issues ten more through an administrator’s exception route. Customers whose purchases appear in the log can obtain valid inclusion proofs; checking one included purchase cannot discover an obligation that never entered the record. The article would make each route that can create an enforceable service claim leave evidence in the same accounting arrangement, or in another arrangement whose totals are reconciled with it, including direct sales, reseller allocations, replacements and administrative exceptions. Customers need a usable route to challenge an omitted claim. Refusing every unlogged credential at the execution boundary could limit which claims receive service, and would still leave a customer harmed if the provider had accepted payment through an unlogged route. A technical rejection cannot settle that dispute. If a design lets a newly issued reservation circulate before its inclusion is verified, the provider must specify who carries the risk during that interval.
- Gaby G. Dagher, Benedikt Bünz, Joseph Bonneau, Jeremy Clark and Dan Boneh, *Provisions: Privacy-preserving proofs of solvency for Bitcoin exchanges*, ACM CCS 2015, full version dated 26 October 2015, particularly Sections 1–2; the article links to https://www.jbonneau.com/doc/DBBCB15-CCS-provisions.pdf. The article says the authors give a construction and prototype evaluation. Their stated limitations include customers checking their own inclusion and the inability of a proof to guarantee future honest conduct. The article calls this research about a specified financial-accounting problem, with assumptions and implementation constraints, and says any implementation would need a construction-specific security review; a prototype alone cannot settle that question. For a reservation service it suggests a direction to investigate: customers verify their own claims while disclosing less about other customers. It does not supply a ready-made proof of computing capacity. A private arithmetic check is useful only when its inputs represent the obligations and resources being compared.
- Craig Steven Wright (2026), *Beyond ‘permissionless’: governance, commitment, and rule change in public blockchains*, *Journal of Institutional Economics*, 22, e21, published online 1 June 2026, DOI 10.1017/S1744137426100599. The article says an eight-system institutional comparison motivates a commitment argument: exposure when participants make system-specific investments while other actors retain authority to revise the rules. Publication does not make that argument correct by itself. The study does not estimate the effect of transparency on reservation markets. Applied here, the question is who can increase the cap, alter the definition of a slot, recognise an exceptional claim or replace the auditing arrangement. A provider could preserve every historical entry while changing the promise made to existing customers. Recording the change would make it visible without deciding whether it was permitted. Additional capacity, or a failure that requires a revised schedule, can be a reason to change a service. The relevant commitment should describe the authority, notice, customer choices and remedies. The article says rigidity has costs too.
- Possible applications named in the article: reserved cloud capacity, limited software seats, venue admission and prepaid service allowances. A firm guarantee against a fixed resource differs from disclosed overbooking based on expected non-use. An auditable issuance history could help a purchaser assess exposure or a reseller reconcile authorised inventory; those potential benefits should be compared with the cost of producing evidence, checking it, protecting commercial information and resolving exceptions. Monitoring needs someone willing and able to do it. A proposed evaluation would compare the design with an ordinary access-controlled booking database and independent reconciliation, under the same service guarantee, workload and failure scenarios: omitted sales, two inconsistent histories, false cancellations, duplicated replacement claims, delayed evidence and an authorised capacity increase. Measures named: excess outstanding commitments detected before the service deadline, valid customer claims wrongly excluded, detection and repair time, service actually delivered, information disclosed and total operating cost. A second test should remove capacity after a clean audit, to distinguish an accurate account of yesterday’s promises from the ability to perform tomorrow’s service. The sources box says the commercial arrangement, economic interpretation and evaluation are proposed analysis, not a new protocol, deployed system, measured saving or claim of journal acceptance for a separate manuscript, and that publication or peer review alone does not establish correctness. This commentary is dated 19 October 2026.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The article starts from a complete set of signed reservations that exceeds the slots, then separates issuance events from outstanding obligations. Tamper-evident logging and Certificate Transparency, including the 18 September 2015 Google report and Experimental RFC 9162, are used for what a recorded history can make visible, and an exception route is used to show an obligation that inclusion proofs do not describe. *Provisions* (CCS 2015) is introduced as a privacy-preserving accounting construction with the limitations the article attributes to it, not as a proof of computing capacity. The 2026 governance paper supplies the question of who can redefine the promise. The close is a proposed comparison against an ordinary booking database, including a test that removes capacity after a clean audit. Counts and the evaluation are labelled hypothetical or proposed.
