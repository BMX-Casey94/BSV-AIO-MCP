# The past that a backup brings back
**Date:** 2026-10-22 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/the-past-a-backup-brings-back | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a backup can restore a working service while reviving an authority that was supposed to have ended, so digital scarcity depends on which past a recovered system is allowed to accept, as well as on who may act now. In the hypothetical, Alice transfers a licence for a single reserved computing slot to Ben; the service records the transfer, refuses Alice’s credential and accepts Ben’s; the next morning an operator restores yesterday’s backup. The recovered database is authentic, and it recognises Alice again. The article says this is not a race between simultaneous requests and does not require the issuer to create an extra licence: the service has forgotten a completed change of authority.

## Key arguments and claims
- The article says a signature or authentication tag can establish that a stored record has not been altered without authorisation, and does not, by itself, establish that the record is the latest acceptable state. Restoring the programme that enforces a rule is not enough if the programme receives obsolete facts about who may use it. Copying yesterday’s database into three new locations does not supply three independent witnesses to today’s ownership, because each copy contains the same missing transfer.
- Bryan Parno, Jacob R. Lorch, John R. Douceur, James Mickens and Jonathan M. McCune, *Memoir: Practical State Continuity for Protected Modules*, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (2011), especially Sections II–IV; the article links to https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/memoir.pdf. As the article describes it: a deterministic module checks a compact request-history commitment held in protected persistent memory; recovery may repeat the matching request, not choose another continuation from an old snapshot; merely comparing counters can strand recovery when a crash separates the trusted-counter update from the durable snapshot write. The paper supplies a prototype and machine-checked safety proofs under its model. Its optimised design requires protected shutdown handling and backup power; denial of service is excluded. The article says this historical construction is not a current deployment recommendation.
- For the licence example, the article offers two architectural possibilities, which it says are not interchangeable implementations: a protected record of the accepted transfer could constrain which database state resumes service, or a recoverable external transaction history could support reconstruction. The design must identify which component remembers the accepted transition, who can reset that component and what happens when it is unavailable. A version number helps only if the comparison has a trustworthy reference; a number stored beside the data can travel backwards with it. A timestamp identifies a claimed time, not necessarily the most recent committed transfer. A hash identifies particular bytes; without a protected expectation about which bytes should be current, it does not select the right history. The commercial question the article poses: can the provider recover Ben’s recognised position without reviving Alice’s competing authority, and if the evidence cannot be recovered, does the service suspend use, investigate, or accept a defined exposure?
- Marcus Brandenburger, Christian Cachin, Matthias Lorenz and Rüdiger Kapitza, *Rollback and Forking Detection for Trusted Execution Environments using Lightweight Collective Memory*, authors’ DSN 2017 paper, arXiv:1701.00981v2, especially Sections 2–4, 5.2 and 6; the article links to https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.00981v2. As the article describes it: trusted execution combined with persistent client memory; the model assumes correct, mutually trusting clients, not arbitrary hostile customers. It provides fork-linearizability: separated, incompatible histories cannot later be joined without exposing the inconsistency. It also reports operation stability, including whether a majority of clients have observed an operation. A response does not automatically establish a history shared by everyone. An isolated branch can stop gaining stability; delay alone does not establish an attack. The 2017 key-value-store proof of concept did not use remote attestation, although the protocol requires it. The experiments distinguish asynchronous and synchronous disk writes. The article says demonstrated components and assumed guarantees must remain separate.
- The article separates prevention from evidence of a split. Discovering a split after both sides have used their slots is different from refusing the obsolete authority before use. Detection may support investigation and compensation; it does not put the consumed resource back. One arrangement treats an acknowledgement as provisional and releases the slot only after the required checks succeed, which introduces waiting. Another releases immediately within a small exposure limit and provides a contractual remedy if reconciliation later reveals a conflict, which introduces risk and a party responsible for bearing it. The article says neither policy follows automatically from the cryptographic mechanism. The same distinction matters for limited API allowances, transferable access and reserved machine time. It does not make already delivered information uncopyable. The object being protected is a defined authority to obtain performance from this service, within its stated trust boundary.
- Craig Steven Wright (2026), *Beyond ‘permissionless’: governance, commitment, and rule change in public blockchains*, *Journal of Institutional Economics*, 22, e21, published online 1 June 2026, DOI 10.1017/S1744137426100599. The article also links to the Cambridge Core record. It says the paper distinguishes admission to a system from authority over its rules, concerns commitment after participants have made system-specific investments, and does not supply a rollback-resistant storage protocol. The application here is described as a separate inference: a recovery mechanism also needs a declared authority structure. Someone may legitimately correct a mistaken transfer; that is different from quietly restoring a state in which the transfer appears never to have occurred. A correction can preserve the original event, identify the authorised intervention and establish the new position. Silent replacement can erase the evidence needed to understand whose entitlement changed. For Ben, the relevant terms include who may authorise recovery, which evidence must survive, how conflicts are resolved and whether the old operator can continue accepting requests after a migration. The article says an emergency procedure is part of the institution.
- Purely illustrative procurement comparison. The article says these invented figures demonstrate the comparison and are not estimates from the cited papers. A continuity control costs £1,200 per month in operation and adds £300 of measured waiting costs. Suppose independent incident analysis estimates that it reduces expected resource waste and disruption by £2,000 per month: the net monthly benefit is £500. If the avoided loss is only £1,000, the same arrangement has a £500 net cost. The loss measure must include competing use, legitimate access wrongly refused, time spent reconstructing positions and the cost of delayed work. Compensation reallocates a loss between parties and should not also be counted as an additional resource loss in a social-cost calculation. For the provider’s own cash-flow assessment, the liability and the price of insurance remain relevant. The article says the credible alternative may be a conventional access-controlled database with tested recovery and accountable administration, and that a hardware-assisted design has to earn its extra complexity against that comparator under the same threat assumptions.
- Proposed evaluation, which the article says is not a report of tests already performed. Begin with a completed transfer and deliberately restore an earlier snapshot in an isolated test system. Attempt use by both the former and current holders, interrupt the recovery at each durable-write boundary, and test migration while the original service remains reachable. Include client-memory loss and unavailable witnesses. Distinguish obsolete requests rejected before performance, conflicts detected only afterwards, legitimate requests blocked, and time until an authoritative position is re-established. An independent test record should retain the pre-failure events so that apparent success cannot be manufactured by losing the evidence of a competing use. The sources box says the article contributes an application and comparison, not a new protocol, an implemented entitlement system, an independent replication or a measured commercial saving. This commentary is dated 22 October 2026.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The article opens with a transfer that a restored, unaltered backup undoes, then uses Memoir (2011) for the difference between an authentic snapshot and a continuous request history, and Lightweight Collective Memory (2017) for what a split history can and cannot show under that paper’s trust assumptions. It keeps each cited limit attached to the construction: denial of service excluded; the 2017 proof of concept did not use remote attestation although the protocol requires it. The 2026 governance paper is applied only as a separate inference about who may correct or restore state. A labelled pound-sterling comparison and a restoration test plan then price and probe the whole recovery promise, with a conventional database as the comparator.
