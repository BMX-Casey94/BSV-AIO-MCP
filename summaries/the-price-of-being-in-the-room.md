# The Price of Being in the Room
**Date:** 2026-07-31T11:54:53.016Z | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-price-of-being-in-the-room
**Subtitle:** When a rule can change, firms stop building and start lobbying for protection. The bill is far larger than anyone counts — and the obvious fix makes one part of it worse.

## Core thesis
Once a rule exists and firms have sunk investment against it, the mere possibility of revision redirects engineering into influence: firms stop asking what to build and start asking who to know. The full bill for a mutable rule has four parts, not one; the most dramatic part — the transfer of market position — is the one part that is not a social loss, yet it drives everything. Because a rule-setter with its own commercial position revises when it pays, the obvious remedies (gentler migration paths; charging the rule-setter into a general fund) fail or backfire. The only instrument that works is compensation paid directly to those displaced — and in member-owned committees, where participation determines shares of the gains, even that cannot work.

## Key arguments and claims
- **The trigger moment.** A draft circulates that would change something a firm has built against. Nothing is decided, but the possibility suffices: engineering time is reallocated to the draft, senior staff attend the working group, a budget line appears — the firm shifts from building to managing exposure. The standards literature covers how rules get agreed, not who bears the cost when an existing rule changes.
- **Adaptation versus displacement.** Adaptation (rewriting code, requalifying hardware, parallel versions) *destroys* value — a real social loss. Displacement (while the firm adapts, its position is occupied by a competitor or by the party that made the change) *moves* value — a transfer netting to zero in welfare accounting. Collapsing the two into one "cost of revision" is the mistake that confuses the area.
- **Standing is a purchased good, and pure waste.** Delegates, secondees, ex-standards-body hires, membership tiers with early draft access, cultivated relationships — none of it improves any product; it only shapes drafts to do less damage. Not corruption: "Representation works even when everyone in the room is behaving impeccably." But socially the money is burned — rent-seeking in the strict Krueger (1974) sense.
- **The four-part bill:** (1) worthwhile projects not done because of exposure; (2) adaptation expenditure; (3) standing expenditure; (4) the cost of effecting the change. The transfer of position is not a social loss — but it drives everything.
- **The rule-setter revises when it pays.** If the rule-changer also holds a position in the same product space (a platform with first-party apps, a certification body whose members compete with those it certifies), revision is an economic calculation: gain proportional to displaceable exposed position, weighed against effecting cost. Counterintuitive result: as changes become *more* damaging, the rule-setter revises *less* often (more firms buy protection, shrinking the extractable base) while each remaining builder's exposure rises — the effects largely cancel. So gentler changes and better migration paths reduce damage per event but raise frequency; the burden barely moves. "Good migration paths are not a substitute for governance." What moves the burden is the rule-setter's gain from revising and the cost of effecting revision — procedural and appropriative variables, not technical ones. Footnote: Farrell and Saloner (1988) observed that firms proposing standards offer nominal-royalty patent licences, facilitating agreement — in this framework, exactly a reduction in the proposer's gain; committees found the right instrument by trial and error.
- **The same change helps large firms and hurts small ones.** A small builder's burden is mostly adaptation, which rises with severity; a large builder's is mostly displacement, which falls as revision frequency falls. So architectures that make changes more consequential (deeper integration, tighter coupling) hurt small builders and help large ones — arithmetic, not favouritism — and it compounds, because large firms can also afford representation. Greenstein (1993) found the adjacent field pattern in federal computer procurement: agencies stuck with incumbents even controlling for fit, incompatibility independently driving vendor choice; the twist is that IBM's apparent disadvantage was produced largely by incompatibilities between generations of IBM's *own* product line. Adaptation cost driving market outcomes, measured in the field.
- **The fix works only if the money goes to the right place.** Charging the rule-setter the damage, proceeds to a general fund: the revision rate becomes socially correct, but defensive spending is untouched and, its one justification removed, becomes unambiguously excessive — a second instrument would be needed. Paying the displacement component directly to affected firms as compensation: defensive expenditure collapses to what real adaptation cost justifies; the rule-setter still faces full social cost; the marginal firm's investment decision becomes socially correct. "One payment. Three margins aligned." Wrinkle: compensation aligns incentives only if it tracks actual loss (protect more, receive less); payments fixed in advance by category (firm size, membership class) are administratively easy but leave over-protection intact.
- **Committees make it worse, and compensation cannot fix them.** In a member-owned body, gains from a rule change are distributed among participants and participation determines the share. Representation then has two returns — protection plus a claim on proceeds — so standing becomes an investment, not insurance, and spending rises at every size. Compensation cannot neutralise this: it works against displacement because it is indexed to loss, while a share of proceeds rewards participation regardless of loss. The constitutional question — does participation determine your share? — is prior to any payment scheme. Not an argument against participation, but for separating participation from profit-sharing.
- **Stated limits.** A single external rule-setter with a commercial position, or a member-owned body with an explicit distribution rule; transfers treated as exactly zero-sum (a benchmark — output, quality or consumer-surplus effects break the cancellation); one revision opportunity (nothing on erosive sequences or reputation); nothing measured — the framework says what to measure (adaptation, displacement, standing as three distinct things), a separation existing empirics do not make. Greenstein said lock-in's degree can never be identified from his data: "That is still true."
- **References:** Anton & Yao (2004); Chen, Doraszelski & Harrington (2009); Farrell & Saloner (1985, 1988); Farrell & Shapiro (1988); Greenstein (1993); Krueger (1974); Smirnov & Wait (2004).

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
A mechanism-design / industrial-organisation model built by decomposition: split the "cost of revision" into adaptation (deadweight loss) and displacement (transfer), add standing expenditure as a third measurable category, then trace incentives — the rule-setter's revision calculus, the firm's protection calculus, and the severity–frequency interaction — as comparative statics with welfare accounting. Empirical anchors are used where they exist (Greenstein's procurement study; Farrell and Saloner's licensing observation), and limitations are stated explicitly. The recurring move is to show that questions argued in technical terms (migration paths, inclusive representation) are constitutional questions about gains, costs and the allocation of proceeds.

## Relevance to his Bitcoin thesis
The essay never mentions Bitcoin; its load for the fixed-protocol position is as the general machinery underneath it.
- **Micro-foundations for "competition relocates".** The four-part bill — forgone projects, adaptation, standing, effecting costs — formalises the rent-seeking claim the Bitcoin essays assert via Tullock and Krueger: a contestable base layer predictably converts engineering capital into lobbying capital. The firm that "stops building and starts attending the working group has read the situation correctly" is the agent-level story of protocol governance.
- **Proof of the concentration result.** That more consequential rule changes hurt small builders and help large ones — arithmetically, without favouritism, compounded by who can afford representation — is the formal backing for "mutability concentrates power" and for flexibility functioning as oligarchy.
- **Closure of the moderate fixes.** Better migration paths merely raise revision frequency; treasury-bound charges worsen defensive spending; and in member-owned bodies — the structure of maintainer or committee governance — no compensation scheme can stop the representation arms race, because participation itself carries a share of the gains. If every procedural remedy fails, the remaining remedy is constitutional: remove the revision margin entirely — a fixed base with nothing left to govern.
- **Design questions reframed as constitutional.** "Whether a rule should be changeable is not really about migration paths... What determines the burden on the firms building against a common rule is what the rule-setter gains from changing it, what changing it costs, and where the proceeds go." On his broader thesis, Bitcoin's original fixed design is exactly such a constitutional choice, made at setup — and the essay shows such choices are usually made implicitly and unwritten.
