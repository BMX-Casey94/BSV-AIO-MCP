# The Little Coin That Wasn’t Afraid
**Date:** 2025-06-16 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-little-coin-that-wasnt-afraid
**Subtitle:** A Children’s Picture Book (Ages 6–10) on Electronic Cash

## Core thesis
A children’s picture-book allegory arguing that the entire purpose of electronic cash is tiny, fast, permissionless payment — a penny for a poem, a tip for the baker — and that both legacy finance and hoarding culture conspire to suppress exactly this use. Real monetary freedom, the story insists, lives in small everyday transfers that need no approval, not in large institutions or speculative accumulation.

## Key arguments and claims
- Bitty, the small coin, has “one mission in life: to be useful in the tiniest, most convenient ways imaginable” — buying biscuits, tipping buskers, sending a smile across the world for just a penny.
- The “Big Coins” stand for legacy finance: they “didn’t roll—oh no. They processed. They were transferred. They required forms in triplicate just to sneeze.”
- The Controllers’ tower is decorated with “words like HODL” — hoarding culture is placed on the side of control, not freedom.
- The Controllers “didn’t fear crooks or robbers. They feared the baker. The busker. The girl who paid for tea without filling out Form 88-B.”
- Their “Freeze Machine” — “a colossal contraption of red tape, blinking lights, and official stamps” — fails because Bitty “zipped straight through the cracks.”
- The Controllers then counterfeit the very idea of digital money: cards that beep and apps that smile, but which demand “your name, your shoe size, and possibly a letter from your grandmother.” The verdict: “It didn’t feel like spending. It felt like asking for permission. With sparkles.”
- Bitty’s chain reaction: a girl sends one penny to her granddad, who buys a bun and tips the baker, who pays the fiddler, who sings for the florist — micropayment velocity as a “chain reaction of cheer”, all without asking once.
- The moral: “real freedom doesn’t wear boots. It jingles in pockets. It clinks in cups. And it never, ever asks for a receipt.”

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Allegory and satire rather than formal argument. The picture-book form maps his economic position — that cash’s value lies in use and velocity at the smallest scale — onto a fable in which banks, card networks, regulators and HODL speculators are comic bureaucrats building “Freeze Machines”, while genuine digital cash is small, quick and indifferent to permission.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- HODL appears in the Controllers’ tower: hoarding and speculation are satirised as part of the control apparatus, not celebrated as user virtues.
- The story glorifies spending and tipping (one-penny payments) rather than store-of-value accumulation or “digital gold”.
- The fake “digital money” — beeping cards and smiling apps that require approval — skewers the permissioned fintech and custodial products that mainstream adoption narratives celebrate.

## Notable quotes
- “nothing scares important people more than something small behaving like it doesn’t need permission.”
- “It didn’t feel like spending. It felt like asking for permission. With sparkles.”
- “Because real freedom doesn’t wear boots. It jingles in pockets. It clinks in cups. And it never, ever asks for a receipt.”
- “And freedom, when it’s real, doesn’t need a fanfare. It just buys the coffee. And never explains.”

## Connections
The penny-to-bun-to-fiddler chain dramatises the “electronic cash, not digital gold” thesis argued polemically in “Stablecoins and the Lost Spark” and “Set in Stone or Sold to the Highest Bidder”; the HODL jibe is developed theologically in “‘Where Your Treasure Is’”.
