# Satoshi NEVER Posted on Bitcointalk
**Date:** 2020-04-02 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/satoshi-never-posted-on-bitcointalk-dd6967d772f7
**Subtitle:** It is a myth that all the posts on Bitcointalk (bitcointalk.org) from my account (Satoshi) are in fact mine and have not been edited or…

## Core thesis
Craig claims that the "Satoshi" account on bitcointalk.org is not his: Satoshi never used Bitcointalk because the domain was only registered in mid-2011, after he had stopped posting. The original forum was a subsite of bitcoin.org, and when the database was migrated to the new domain, those controlling it could — and did — edit posts, so the Bitcointalk record cannot be trusted as his words. The Medium post is an excerpt; the body continues on craigwright.net.

## Key arguments and claims
- "It is a myth that all the posts on Bitcointalk (bitcointalk.org) from my account (Satoshi) are in fact mine and have not been edited or changed and that the login on the website belongs to me. Satoshi (I) never used Bitcointalk."
- Domain evidence: the first Wayback Machine capture of bitcointalk.org is July 2011, when "the website had not been created yet", and WHOIS shows "Bitcointalk was only registered in mid-2011".
- The original forum "was created as a subsite of the bitcoin.org website. It wasn't a separate domain"; bitcoin.org's forum linked to SourceForge and ran within the same system, "not... on a separate server outside of bitcoin.org".
- "I had stopped posting long before Bitcointalk was created. The database was taken across in part."
- Migration introduced fabrication risk: "when you control the database, you can edit what other people have said. There are a couple of posts that have been changed from the original" — via "very minor changes, ones they don't think other people will notice".
- He names names: "A new user signed up on 5th May, 2011, and helped with things. The person behind the new user was called Gregory Maxwell." Martti (Sirius) "and a few others migrated the web pages. But not everything corresponds 100%."
- His "final post" on the account "links to a domain that does not exist" — offered as internal evidence that the supposed author never made it.
- Motive ascription: "most of the same people don't want my version of Bitcoin, and think that they can do better" — the migration narrative serves those who took over.
- Personal colour: "I liked a white Bitcoin logo on black... It is a shame I would listen to other people; if I hadn't, I wouldn't have left, and the website would not have been taken over."
- The excerpt ends mid-narrative: "The migration was done in multiple steps. First came the move from the original subsite…" — continuing on craigwright.net.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The method is forensic-historical: domain-registration dates, Wayback Machine captures (with the caveat that the archive "is not perfect... and it can be gamed"), and WHOIS records are marshalled to establish a timeline in which the Bitcointalk account postdates his departure. He then infers editability from database control, and attributes the migration to named individuals (Maxwell, Martti). The argument is self-servingly structured — it preserves his Satoshi claim while invalidating any specific Bitcointalk post that contradicts his current positions, since authenticity is now contestable post by post.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the community's canonical source base: Bitcointalk posts are routinely cited as Satoshi's authentic views (on scaling, nodes, design intent); Craig declares the entire archive unreliable.
- Contradicts the "Satoshi's words settle debates" appeal to authority common in BTC discourse — the words may have been edited by successors with an agenda.
- Contradicts the mainstream migration narrative (a smooth, faithful move from the bitcoin.org forum to bitcointalk.org): he alleges partial migration with deliberate, subtle alterations.
- Selectively deployed scepticism: he simultaneously claims to be Satoshi and disowns the primary textual record of Satoshi, inverting the usual evidentiary hierarchy.
