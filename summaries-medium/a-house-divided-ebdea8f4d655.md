# A house divided
**Date:** 2018-11-24 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/a-house-divided-ebdea8f4d655
**Subtitle:** The tale of a split registry

## Core thesis
A post-split broadside: ABC's addition of OP_DataSigVerify (DSV) has changed that chain "from Bitcoin to a speculative gambling system" built for bucket shops and unregistered securities, and the idea of a "permission-less" token is a sham with no basis in common law. Fungibility is a legal property, not a technical one, so anonymous or illegally-constructed coins can never be fungible — and when a registry splits, only the fork that stays true to the original aim and acts entirely within the law is valid.

## Key arguments and claims
- DSV critique: ABC promoted WormHole and bucket shops via OP_DataSigVerify, "a poorly thought-out, new OP_Code"; corporate officers at Bitcoin.com "have specifically targeted this use as an illegal 'bucket shop'".
- Permissionlessness denied: "There is no permission-less system in the common law"; the SEC has begun ruling on these "sham securities" and issued ICO risk statements (the "three 'Rs' of ICOs: Risks, Rewards and Responsibilities").
- SEC already covers DSV's use cases: quoting the SEC's digital-asset statement — an entity using a smart contract "to bring together or execute orders, could be providing a trading facility", and one that standardises terms or sets execution priorities "could be setting rules".
- DSV's legal character: it "sets up and releases a system for gambling, derivatives exchange, and a means to create and trade securities that have not been registered" — in allowing "permission-less" trade, "it acts outside the law".
- Fungibility is legal, not technical: "Money is fungible not through technology but as a result of law"; it is a legal term of art meaning exchange for value in good faith, completed "without knowledge" of illegal aspects of the tokens' history.
- Anonymous coins cannot be fungible: "By definition, no anonymous coin can be legally fungible" — Zcash and ABC are named as chains that "can never be fungible".
- The DSV history problem: a DSV chain "is constructed with a history", requiring a registration body (e.g. a PKI certificate authority signing all transactions) and forcing merchants to validate the entire history of every coin — "This is not how Bitcoin works."
- Source-of-wealth risk: with anonymous or non-fungible tokens, existing source-of-wealth laws mean "you could lose everything, if the chain was later analysed"; "Merchants have no rights in anonymous and non-fungible systems."
- Property cannot fork: token systems cover real-world property; "you cannot split the rights to many forms of property, and if you are doing this, then none of the systems are valid".
- One valid registry: after a split, what matters is "the one registry that is the most true to the original aim and also the one that is completely acting within the law"; a registry that oversteps or accepts non-fungible payments "risks being wound up and all goods being returned to former owners".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Statutory and regulatory citation (SEC public statements quoted at length) combined with legal term-of-art precision — fungibility, good faith, "without knowledge" — used to show that technical features (anonymity, DSV) destroy rather than create legal properties. The ledger is framed as a property registry, so property-law logic (you cannot fork title to the same asset) settles the governance question; the mode is condemnatory, calling the DSV push "not just bad, it is immoral".

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- "Permissionless" as the core value proposition: dismissed outright as a sham with no common-law basis.
- Forking as legitimate governance / "let the market decide": only one registry can be valid; duplicating property rights across forks invalidates both.
- Privacy-enhances-fungibility orthodoxy: inverted — anonymity legally destroys fungibility because good-faith "without knowledge" exchange becomes impossible.
- Code-is-law: law is asserted as prior to and binding on protocol design; constructs "designed solely" for illegal use are immoral, not innovative.
