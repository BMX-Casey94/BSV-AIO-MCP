# Payment intermediaries
**Date:** 2018-11-11 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/payment-intermediaries-db46605e79f4
**Subtitle:** The difficulties in transferring cash payments over large distances and between people who may have never met and may never meet created…

## Core thesis
To understand Bitcoin you must understand what it was designed to replace: not banks, credit, or government, but "the ineffective payment systems (such as Visa and MasterCard)". The essay traces why payment intermediaries arose (distance and stranger trust), how PayPal-style P2P systems consolidated the role, and how these intermediaries double as regulatory enforcement points against illicit commerce — before concluding that Bitcoin removed the need for the middleman and enabled "true P2P electronic cash".

## Key arguments and claims
- Definition of purpose: "Bitcoin is not a means to remove banks, offer credit, or undermine government, it is a means to replace the ineffective payment systems (such as Visa and MasterCard) and the problems these create."
- Payment intermediaries exist because of "the difficulties in transferring cash payments over large distances and between people who may have never met"; they supply "both trust and some realistic means for a purchaser to transfer consideration to the seller reliably". PayPal was "one of the first".
- Worked example: a Singapore customer of Lasseters online casino in Australia needs trust accounts, stored credit-card details, or bank transfers so that winnings can be paid out and gambling debts covered — the card company or bank is "an essential actor".
- Digital-cash history: it was "originally believed" (Anderson et al., Dec 1997, "Exploring Digital Cash") that digital cash would be minted and evolve quickly; instead, in 2002 "roughly ninety percent of Internet transactions used credit cards" (citing Ronald J. Mann, *Regulating Internet Payment Intermediaries*, 82 Texas L. Rev. 681 (2004)).
- Terminology policing: in this context "P2P stands for 'person-to-person'", explicitly distinguished from peer-to-peer file sharing.
- Gatekeeper function: "A commercial site distributing child pornography from Nigeria cannot be run profitably without an economical method of receiving consideration. If the site operators cannot reliably receive payment, they will quickly shut down." Hence "payment intermediaries can be used to prevent illicit activity over the Internet — either through proactive actions or upon the receipt of court orders".
- Conclusion: "With Bitcoin, the need for a middle man was removed. It enabled the ability to have true P2P electronic cash."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
This is his academic-register mode: a footnoted, law-review-style institutional history that defines terms, cites Mann's Texas Law Review article and Anderson et al.'s 1997 digital-cash presentation, and reasons from the economic function of institutions rather than from ideology. The argument is functionalist — intermediaries exist to solve trust-at-a-distance, and any replacement must be judged against that function. Much of the text reads like an excerpt adapted from his earlier scholarly writing on Internet payment regulation.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the "Bitcoin kills banks / topples governments" rhetoric common in 2018 crypto discourse: he states plainly Bitcoin is "not a means to remove banks... or undermine government".
- Contradicts the pure "disintermediation" mantra: he dwells on the *useful* enforcement role of intermediaries as "financial gatekeepers" able to "stop illicit transactions and act as regulatory enforcement points" — a positive framing of compliance chokepoints that most of crypto treated as the problem.
- Tension he leaves unresolved: intermediaries are praised as crime-prevention points, yet Bitcoin "removed" the need for them — the enforcement question is deferred to the companion essay "Prevention is the key" (12 Nov 2018), where escrow contracts and monitoring take over that role.
