# Expectation of Profits
**Date:** 2018-12-20 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/expectation-of-profits-a56c845056a3
**Subtitle:** The US Howey case used the Expectation of Profit as an element in International Brotherhood of Teamsters vs Daniel. Daniel involved…

## Core thesis
A walk through US securities doctrine — the Howey test and its case law — aimed at demonstrating that ICOs and coin offerings fall squarely within the Securities Act. Craig argues that the apparent carve-outs in *Teamsters v Daniel* and *United Housing Foundation v Forman* are fact-specific decisions that give promoters no leeway to structure around securities law, and that the "investment of money" element is satisfied by any liquid consideration, not just cash.

## Key arguments and claims
- In *International Brotherhood of Teamsters v Daniel*, employer contributions to a non-contributory pension plan were held not to be an investment contract because the expectation-of-profit element was missing — the court focused on the "relatively small percentage of the planned assets derived from earnings rather from employer contributions."
- The *Daniel* court could instead have reasoned from a "logical predicate" of the beneficiaries' reasonable profit expectations, since "many investment schemes go wrong; they do not have any less involvement in the sale of securities for that reason."
- *United Housing Foundation v Forman* (co-op housing shares resalable only at purchase price, with commercial-lease income offsetting rent) found the profit expectation "far too speculative and insubstantial to bring the entire transaction within the Securities act."
- Neither case should be read as promoter leeway: *Daniel* was influenced by Congress having just subjected pensions to ERISA, and *Forman* by the purchasers' motivation "to attain a decent place to reside" — the courts simply declined "to find the security in a social construct."
- *SEC v Edwards* (rendered "SCC vs Edward"; payphones sold with a 5-year leaseback, management agreement and buy-back) shows the court rejecting its own earlier dicta: "a money-making scheme that offers a contractual entitlement to a fixed rather than a variable return did not prevent it from being a security."
- "Investment contract" has "no meaning in a commercial context; it is simply a construct of legislators and judges," defined by the four Howey predicates: investment of money, common enterprise, expectation of profits, solely from the efforts of others.
- On "investment of money": "the US Securities Act covers all offers and sales of securities, regardless of the form of consideration" — consideration can be cash, cheques, negotiable instruments, or "anything that would constitute liquid consideration," which squarely captures ICO contributions (e.g. ETH paid for tokens).
- On "common enterprise": courts of appeal have produced two formulations — vertical commonality (investor's fortunes linked to the manager, in strict and broad variants) and horizontal commonality (interrelated interests of the investors in a scheme).
- On "solely from the efforts of others": the question is whether investors are passive; pyramid-style schemes where recruits themselves hunt prospects can still be incorporated into the analysis.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal common-law method: he decomposes the Howey test into four "predicate subjects" and then works through the leading cases element by element, reading each decision against its motivating facts (ERISA for pensions, housing policy for co-ops) to strip them of general precedential force. The style is that of a securities-law textbook précis, with the ICO application asserted rather than argued at length — the case taxonomy is meant to do the work of showing the law already reaches token sales.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the 2017–18 ICO industry orthodoxy that "utility tokens" are not securities: by establishing that consideration need not be money and that fixed returns still count, he closes the two main structuring arguments used by token promoters.
- Contradicts the crypto-libertarian claim that legal categories can be engineered away ("code is law" adjacent structuring): his reading of *Daniel* and *Forman* is explicitly anti-evasion — the exceptions are social-policy decisions, not templates.
- Era context: published at the tail end of the ICO bust and after the SEC's DAO Report, this positions Craig on the side of maximal SEC jurisdiction — against the prevailing "our token is different" culture of crypto fundraising.
