# The cult of Decentralisation

## Core thesis
Bitcoin's purpose is stable electronic money — digital cash — and decentralisation is merely the instrument that makes the monetary system change-resistant at scale. The "cult of decentralisation" has inverted means and ends, re-purposing Bitcoin away from cash towards digital gold or a settlement layer. Craig argues that a small number of competing commercial miners (three to five or more, none holding over 50% of hash power) is sufficient decentralisation, and that at global scale no government could attack the network.

## Key arguments and claims
- Satoshi's P2P Foundation announcement is quoted as the founding brief: conventional currency fails on trust — central banks debase, banks "lend it out in waves of credit bubbles with barely a fraction in reserve", and overhead makes micropayments impossible. Bitcoin's answer was "the first true implementation of Peer to Peer currency".
- The foundation story "has been twisted to say that Bitcoin is there to create a decentralised system". In fact "the system is decentralised in order to create the first stable electronic money, not as digital gold, not as a settlement system, but as digital cash".
- Money is a measurement instrument: "We do not have many versions of a metre and nor should we have many versions of currency." Decentralisation exists so money stays difficult to change — stability arrives "with scale", not at birth.
- Miner/pool centralisation critiques commit the false-dichotomy fallacy: centralised vs decentralised is not "an all or nothing scenario"; "There are other options."
- Sufficient decentralisation is defined numerically: "The requirement is three or more parties acting in competition", none above 50% of hash power; five or more is recommended so a single bankruptcy cannot destabilise the network. A bankrupt miner raises rivals' profit, so "competition and profit dynamically maintain the balance".
- Kiyotaki and Wright's search-theoretic model of money (1989/1993) is invoked: money solves a search problem, and fiat predominates because of "Lower storage costs than all other goods and commodities" and "Non-consumability". Bitcoin mirrors fiat's monetary properties "whilst creating stability and limiting the ability for individual parties to debase the currency".
- Arrow-Hahn (1971) is cited for the claim that fiat money is "not unequivocally welfare enhancing" and that an optimum quantity of money exists; currency competition is modelled as a prisoner's dilemma converging on "a single Nash equilibrium that drives out the use of the less efficient currency".
- Gold became money because it had "practically no other use in history"; jewellery came later as a Veblen good (his India example). A medium of exchange must have "no other value or at least a use value that is far lower than any other product being transacted".
- Scale is the security budget: "As a global system, no government could ever attack bitcoin."
- Egalitarianism is explicitly disavowed: "Bitcoin is not a system designed to deliver equality, for the only way to true equality is for all to be equally destitute."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He argues from origin authority, quoting Satoshi's 2008/2009 posts as the controlling text, then formalises with academic monetary economics (Kiyotaki-Wright search theory, Arrow-Hahn general equilibrium, Nash equilibrium and prisoner's dilemma). He names the logical fallacy he is rebutting (false dichotomy) and uses measurement analogies (the metre) and commodity history (gold's uselessness) to make stability the defining property of money. The mode is corrective-exegetical: recover the original design intent from its later "myth".

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the "digital gold"/store-of-value thesis: Bitcoin's value proposition is low-cost, high-volume cash, and "It is not digital gold nor would such a system work".
- Contradicts decentralisation-maximalism (every user validating, thousands of nodes as the security model): five competing corporate miners with no 50% holder "is decentralised".
- Contradicts the settlement-layer framing that justifies small blocks and L2 offload; the goal is "micro-transactions whilst simultaneously offering negligible or low transaction fees".
- Contradicts the narrative that miner concentration is an existential flaw; concentration plus competition is the equilibrium design, and scale makes the network unattackable by states.
