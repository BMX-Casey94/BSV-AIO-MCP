# Account and Transfer Systems. — core principles

- **Conventional payments move bank credit, not stuff.** Most payment systems work by "the deposit of funds and the transfer of credit from one party's account in a bank or similar organisation to another".
- **A transfer reallocates interbank liabilities.** It "reduces the liability of the Originator's Bank to the originator, and creates a corresponding increase in the liability of the Beneficiary's Bank to the Beneficiary".
- **U.C.C. Article 4A supplies the nomenclature.** A "funds transfer" (§4A-104(a)) is "the process, procedure, and collected series of transactions" constituting the transfer; a "payment order" (§4A-103(a)(1)) is an instruction to process part of it — "a 'funds transfer' typically will be comprised of a series of one or more 'payment orders'".
- **The parties are defined by role.** "Originator" (§4A-104(1)) is the sender of the first payment order; "Beneficiary" (§4A-103(a)(2)) is the person to be paid by the beneficiary's bank.
- **Custodial crypto is an account-based system.** "In Bitcoin, we see such systems in exchanges and custodial wallets, where the account holder does not maintain direct control of their private keys" — such users hold a claim on the provider, like any bank depositor, and sit inside existing funds-transfer law.
- **Key control is the legal dividing line.** Peer-to-peer properties attach to direct control of private keys; intermediation is a consequence of custody arrangements, not of the Bitcoin protocol — "not your keys, not your coins" is a legal characterisation, not a slogan.
