# Proof of (unregistered) security — core principles

- **Proof of stake is an unregistered security.** Section 2(a)(1) of the 1933 Act covers it in all forms. The Howey test is met: money invested in a common enterprise with an expectation of profit solely from the effort of others (*SEC v W.J. Howey Co.*; *United Housing Found. v Forman*).
- **A token on a blockchain is legally nothing new.** Securities may be certificated, dematerialised or book-entry; blockchain issuance is a device "to promote the issue of securities without registration or adherence to the law."
- **Staking rewards are dividends to preferential shareholders.** Validators are "rewarded dividends for holding and not spending tokens" while ordinary holders get nothing. The claim that merely holding contributes to the network confirms the Howey expectation of profit from others' efforts.
- **Stake concentrates validation.** A high capital threshold (the essay's Ethereum example: 1,500 ETH) plus thin average holdings means a few per cent of the network can validate and "2% of the network are able to control the other 98%."
- **Sybil attack is unstoppable under stake.** A cartel holding 51% of tokens can run thousands of virtual machines voting as if they were many individuals, with "no way to stop such an attack or attribute it" — market manipulation and control.
- **Exchanges cannot safely stake customer coins.** Custody risk plus existing bailment law would preclude it, shrinking the validator set further.
- **Decentralisation is measurable, and stake fails it.** By common mathematical measures, widely held listed equities can be more decentralised than a stake-based coin — the essay's comparison is Apple stock and J.P. Morgan versus Ethereum.
- **Proof of work is the alternative that is not equity.** PoS is "proof of equity by a preferential voting mechanism"; it does not deliver the benefits of a work-based blockchain and is purpose-built for insider price manipulation while hiding that ability from regulators.
