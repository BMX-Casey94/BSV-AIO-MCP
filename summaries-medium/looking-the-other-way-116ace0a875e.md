# Looking the Other Way
**Date:** 2020-01-17 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/looking-the-other-way-116ace0a875e
**Subtitle:** Not everything of my story is good and positive. In creating Bitcoin and all the derivative systems, I looked away from a lot of things…

## Core thesis
A sweeping autobiographical confession-cum-indictment: Craig recounts losing control of Bitcoin in 2010 to "wacko and anarchist" criminals, his years rebuilding the knowledge and corporate structures to scale it back to his original vision (now BSV), and his financial/legal history — the ATO audits, liquidated companies, Seychelles trusts, gaming software, and malware-reversing income that funded it all. The through-line: Bitcoin was designed to make crime expensive and traceable, the small-block BTC crowd knew it and fought to keep it capturable-by-no-one, and 2020 is when regulators and courts catch up. The Medium post is an excerpt; the body continues on craigwright.net.

## Key arguments and claims
- In 2009 he "talked to the Australian government and the tax authorities" about replacing "low-level auditors with CAATs-based software" (computer-assisted audit technology) enabled by an immutable ledger.
- "Things started falling apart for me in the middle of 2010... I had started to lose faith in my own project": he pushed for micropayments and commercial web systems, "and yet, the first thing people developed was a heroin store" (linking the bitcointalk Silk Road thread).
- He accuses "Mr Antonopoulos" of testifying "under oath about how he wants to see child pornography rings expand because of Bitcoin", and says most people running Core (BTC) "are utterly depraved".
- The missing ingredient was scale — "Not scaling with megabytes of data, scale and price": the derivative's rising value recaptures "the same economic framework as the original idea of Bitcoin... (as BSV)"; he spent 2011–2015 "gaining enough knowledge to be able to scale Bitcoin" alone, and "[a]ll of it is now complete".
- Crime economics: in a December 2010 China presentation he argued systems that "trace money and allow privacy yet also ensure that records are immutable... make criminal activity more expensive"; "as we increase the cost of crime, we significantly decrease the amount of crime".
- "People such as Erik Voorhees and Greg Maxwell have understood that Bitcoin could be captured through regulatory control right from the early days. It is why they have fought to keep Bitcoin small" — and promoted "false narratives about using nodes as if it mattered"; "dishonest companies like Bitmain promoted a lie to sell their second-rate equipment".
- "Bitcoin is not controlled by the users. No blockchain is. A node is defined in section 5 of my white paper. Only point 3 references the solving of a hash puzzle."
- Timing myth: "Right about the time I created Bitcoin, e-gold collapsed, and... every single piece of criminal scum associated with the system jumped ship" into Bitcoin believing it allowed anonymous crime.
- He predicts "people such as Vitalik Buterin start to face perjury charges" for false claims "to Congress and organisations such as the SEC" that blockchains can't be captured or controlled.
- Financial history: a family trust "beyond anything you've seen reported"; Wright International Investments Limited; a Seychelles company (number 093344); bearer-share companies "may be illegal in the USA, but I am not in the USA"; the 2009 ATO audit forced two companies into liquidation yet he "paid every creditor 100 cents in the dollar"; he spent "over $1 million fighting a tax claim where I had actually 'recklessly underclaimed'" and won after four years.
- Income sources: gaming software for licensed casinos (Lasseters Online Casino; a Playboy Gaming casino killed by Australia's licensing moratorium and US crackdowns) and "very-low-level coding" — "MASM, Forth, and other base-level systems" — including "the development of exploits, covert channel software, and reversing malware", much of which "Dave handled".
- Dave Kleiman "became involved more in 2011" when funds ran low; assets moved "back into Tulip Trading and other companies"; "David Kleiman was never involved in such organisations" as the post-2011 trust structures — "Dave had his own companies — including ones in Panama and Costa Rica. Ira threw the paper work on them away."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Confessional revisionism: he concedes looking away ("Not everything of my story is good") to purchase credibility for the counter-narrative that his detractors are the criminals. The method mixes memoir, economic deterrence theory (crime falls as its cost rises), and authorial exegesis of the white paper (section 5's node definition). Ad hominem is load-bearing — named opponents (Antonopoulos, Voorhees, Maxwell, Buterin, Bitmain) are cast as depraved or perjuring, so their technical positions (small blocks, user nodes) can be read as criminal self-interest rather than engineering judgement.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the small-block orthodoxy: keeping blocks small was not decentralisation hygiene but, he claims, a deliberate strategy by Voorhees/Maxwell to keep Bitcoin outside regulatory capture — scale (in price and throughput) is the designed enforcement mechanism.
- Contradicts the "every user runs a node" ethos: nodes are section-5 commercial miners destined for data centres; user-node narratives were "false narratives" sold by interested parties.
- Contradicts the censorship-resistance/anonymity ethos wholesale: "Bitcoin is a system designed to promote legitimate exchange. And as such, it is easily controlled by law."
- Contradicts the community's self-image as heirs to e-gold's cypherpunk martyrdom: he casts the e-gold diaspora as "criminal scum" who misread Bitcoin as an anonymous crime tool.
- Contradicts the "code is law / governments can't touch crypto" claims made to regulators: he predicts perjury exposure for those who testified to that effect.
