# Foreign-exchange considerations & Bitcoin
**Date:** 2018-10-17 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/foreign-exchange-considerations-bitcoin-c2d112386a97

## Core thesis
Because Bitcoin is inherently international and is treated in law as a currency, anyone buying or spending bitcoin must account for foreign-exchange valuations — even for purely domestic transactions — and cross-border use further imports choice-of-law questions about which jurisdiction's contract law governs. A short note whose substance is carried largely by two footnotes: the SEC v. Shavers ruling that "Bitcoin is a currency or form of money", and a précis of proper-law-of-contract doctrine.

## Key arguments and claims
- "The inherently international nature of Bitcoin lends its use to the requirement to account for foreign-exchange valuations. This does not even need to be associated with international transactions" — FX accounting bites even domestically.
- "The treatment of bitcoin as a currency leads to the requirement to account for the exchange rates when bitcoin is purchased or exchanged for goods and services."
- Legal authority for currency status: in SEC v. Trendon T. Shavers and Bitcoin Savings and Trust, Case №4:13-CV-416 (E.D. Tex), Magistrate Judge Amos Mazzant held — applying the Howey investment-contract test — that "Bitcoin is a currency or form of money, and investors wishing to invest in BTCST provided an investment of money", since it buys goods and services, pays living expenses, and is exchangeable for "the U.S. dollar, Euro, Yen, and Yuan".
- Cross-border complication: use "as a global currency... extended into the purchases of goods and services from different jurisdictions" raises the proper law of the contract — "the system of domestic law that defines the obligations assumed by the parties".
- Choice of law is clearest by express selection: "The parties may expressly choose the body of law, which will apply to all or part of their contract including offer and acceptance."
- UK-specific detail: the Hague Uniform Law (ULIS) applies to a sale of goods only if expressly incorporated (Art. 3, s.1(3), Uniform Laws on International Sales Act 1967); where contracting parties are in different states, offer/acceptance analysis (quoting Schu, 1997) determines which law governs a website sale.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Compressed legal memorandum: a two-sentence thesis up front, with the evidentiary weight pushed into footnotes — a quoted federal-court holding (with archive.org citation "accessed 14 January 2014", indicating the text was drafted years earlier) and doctrinal summary of private international law. The method is citation of settled authority to fix Bitcoin's legal character (currency), then deduction of the compliance consequences that follow.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against "Bitcoin is outside the state money system": he uses a US federal court to establish bitcoin is money, then concludes FX and proper-law rules apply to it as to any currency — legality through existing law, not exemption from it.
- Against borderless-exceptionalism: internationality is framed not as freedom from law but as a multiplication of accounting and conflict-of-laws obligations.
- Implicitly against "number go up" hodling-as-investment framing: the quoted passage is from a securities-fraud jurisdictional ruling, tying bitcoin's currency status to regulatory reach from the outset.
