# Monetary Law and Blockchains
**Date:** 2019-06-12 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/monetary-law-and-blockchains-edad5aadd009
**Subtitle:** The first thing to realise is that blockchains do not fork in a manner that splits the chain.

## Core thesis
An excerpt arguing that blockchains cannot actually "fork" into two of the same thing: a so-called split is the creation of a new system, and marketing that new system as the original is fraudulent passing-off. He applies this to "Core coin (BTC)", which he says made radical protocol changes while leading consumers to believe they still held Bitcoin. The body continues on craigwright.net.

## Key arguments and claims
- "Blockchains do not fork in a manner that splits the chain" — the premise that forks divide an asset is a conceptual error.
- Where a split appears to happen, "the reality is the creation of a new system that differs from the original".
- If the new system "does not actively inform consumers that it is not the original, it is fraudulently passing off as something it is a mere copy of" — a consumer-law test applied to protocol governance.
- "Core coin (BTC) implemented radical changes to the Bitcoin protocol, and instead of launching as a new system defrauded consumers in leading them to believe that they had bitcoin" — BTC, not BSV, is the impostor chain.
- Orphan blocks and reorganisations are part of Bitcoin's design, "not a bad thing": "An orphan block is a game-theoretic signalling device. To the user, it's irrelevant."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He imports commercial-legal categories (passing off, consumer fraud) into protocol governance, treating protocol identity as fixed and definitional: change the rules radically and you have a new product that must not borrow the old name. He pairs this with design-history claims — orphans as game-theoretic signalling — to normalise miner competition as intended behaviour rather than network failure.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against the "BTC is Bitcoin by consensus, hash rate, and ticker" orthodoxy: Craig says identity is set by the original protocol, so BTC's changes (SegWit era) make it the copy, regardless of market recognition.
- Against "rough consensus and running code" governance: consumer-protection and passing-off law rank above developer consensus in deciding what may call itself Bitcoin.
- Against the mainstream fear of reorgs and orphan races as attacks: he reframes them as healthy, designed signalling between miners.
