# The Toll Booth Economy
**Date:** 2026-05-17 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-toll-booth-economy
**Subtitle:** What intermediaries take, what information they destroy, and why markets decay when exchange becomes permission

## Core thesis
Intermediaries are justified only when they reduce the costs of exchange more than they increase dependency, delay, rent extraction and control; the modern economy has instead built an expanding toll-booth structure in which banks, platforms, processors, compliance vendors and identity providers stand mandatorily between economic actors. The visible cost is the fee, but the deeper cost is lost information: in a Hayekian frame, markets are discovery procedures, and compulsory intermediation corrupts prices, suppresses transactions that would have revealed preferences, and converts markets into "managed corridors".

## Key arguments and claims
- The intelligent question is not "intermediary or no intermediary?" but "what problem does this intermediary solve, and at what cost?" — grounded in Coase (1937), North (1990) and Williamson (1975, 1985). Once the route is compulsory, "friction is now revenue".
- Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945): economic knowledge is dispersed, local, time-sensitive and tacit; prices are compressed signals that coordinate it. Intermediaries can corrupt the signal — inserting charges, bundling services, delaying settlement, suppressing transactions and converting price discovery into platform discovery.
- What intermediaries take beyond fees: spreads, float, time, optionality, data, bargaining power, classification power, the customer relationship, and silence — the transactions that never occur and so never appear in statistics.
- The lost-information problem: when payment costs are too high, micropayments disappear and "the market then appears to say: there is no demand. But the market said no such thing. The intermediary prevented the question from being asked." The lost knowledge is counterfactual and unrecoverable by any planner.
- Fee wedges destroy exchange: a buyer willing to pay £10 and a seller willing to accept £9 cannot trade if intermediaries insert £2 of cost — a deadweight loss on the economist's graph, a business that never existed in the world.
- Platforms enclose discovery itself, not just exchange: sellers compete for algorithmic visibility, reputation becomes platform-specific, customers become platform assets, and a seller cannot tell whether falling sales reflect demand, demotion or self-preferencing — "a knowledge problem disguised as convenience".
- Banking captures settlement and bundles services: de-risking excludes lawful but inconvenient customers (small firms, charities, migrants, cash-intensive businesses), making the bank "a private governor of market possibility". Direct settlement threatens the bundle by separating payment from custody.
- Compliance has become an intermediary industry: costs fall disproportionately on small firms, so incumbents turn compliance into a moat, and the market "no longer begins with demand. It begins with classification."
- Data extraction is hidden taxation: the intermediary observes all participants and "becomes less a facilitator and more an intelligence layer", enabling personalised, opaque pricing. "The planner has been privatised and given a dashboard."
- Identity intermediation collapses contextual trust into centralised credentialing: a person wrongly excluded from the identity-payment-platform stack "may be economically disabled". "That is not a market. That is a permissions registry with prices attached."
- Kirznerian entrepreneurship is suppressed because gatekeepers control access to experimentation; innovation "does not present itself wearing a certificate of obvious legitimacy".
- The political economy is a regulator–incumbent–intermediary triangle explained via Olson's logic of collective action: concentrated interests lobby, diffuse costs go unrepresented, and "the dead do not lobby. Neither do the unborn markets."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Explicitly Hayekian epistemics layered on transaction-cost economics, with public-choice theory for the political analysis. The essay proceeds as seventeen numbered predicates, each isolating one mechanism (wedges, float, data, identity, compliance), and closes with a "cost ledger" and a five-part counterargument section that concedes real functions — fraud, trust, scale, regulation, convenience — before reapplying the value test.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
No direct engagement — this post is a general theory of intermediation and market information aimed at banks, platforms, app stores and compliance structures; its connection to crypto is implicit, supplying the Hayekian apparatus for Craig's critique of custodial and second-layer arrangements elsewhere.
