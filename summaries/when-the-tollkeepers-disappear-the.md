# When the Tollkeepers Disappear: The Consequences of Real Digital Cash
**Date:** 2026-04-15 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/when-the-tollkeepers-disappear-the
**Subtitle:** A structural analysis of a world where intermediaries no longer extract value, and individuals regain direct control over exchange, time, and economic agency

## Core thesis
Most financial infrastructure exists not to create value but to insert itself between those who do, extracting compounded tolls through structural positioning rather than risk-bearing enterprise. A properly designed digital cash system — direct, instantly final, micropayment-capable, global, scalable and deterministically cheap — renders the intermediary layer's three core functions redundant, redistributing efficiency gains to participants and restoring individual control over exchange, time and economic agency.

## Key arguments and claims
- Intermediation is systemic erosion, not modest cost: a 3–4% fee per transaction, compounded across a chain of exchanges, can siphon off "twenty cents on the dollar" — "not through risk-bearing enterprise, but through structural positioning". "This is not an accident. It is a design."
- The necessity of intermediaries is manufactured: "The system does not function because intermediaries are necessary; rather, intermediaries are necessary because the system has been constructed to require them."
- Real digital cash is defined by six properties: direct transferability without third-party permission; instant settlement final within seconds; micropayment capability at fractions of a cent; global reach; scalability to billions of transactions per day; and a deterministic, minimal fee structure.
- The intermediary layer's three functions collapse under such a system: transaction processing becomes trivial (the network validates and settles; no clearinghouse, reconciliation or delay); risk management — "largely a function of delayed settlement and counterparty exposure" — mostly evaporates when settlement is final and immediate; information coordination becomes inherent in a unified ledger.
- Worked model: 3% average fee across 10 transactions per economic chain yields approximately 26% compounded value erosion; digital cash with fees "measured in thousandths of a cent" reduces this to near zero — yielding higher retained earnings, prices reflecting production costs rather than embedded fees, newly viable micropayment activity, and lower barriers to entry.
- Micropayments: a $0.01 payment bearing a 3% fee plus fixed processing cost is "effectively impossible"; at fees as low as $0.00003, pay-per-use services, granular content monetisation, machine-to-machine transactions and real-time dynamic pricing become operational rather than theoretical.
- Remittances: workers lose 5–10% to fees and exchange spreads — "a material reduction in living standards" at the margin. Digital cash makes transfers instant, fees negligible and exchange rates transparent, directly increasing recipients' effective income and restoring control to both parties.
- Time as a financial variable: "Settlement delays introduce uncertainty. Uncertainty introduces risk. Risk introduces cost." Intermediaries monetise this sequence — charging for acceleration, guarantees and liquidity during delays they themselves impose. Instant settlement reduces credit-line needs, lowers capital requirements and eliminates settlement risk.
- Control and agency: in the current system accounts can be frozen, transactions reversed, access restricted — a transfer of authority away from the individual. Digital cash reverses this: "Transactions, once made, are final. Access is not contingent on approval." Craig stresses this is a functional, not ideological, claim.
- The illusion of necessity is sustained by self-imposed complexity: "The system did not require intermediaries because it was complex. It became complex because it required intermediaries."
- Incumbent resistance will arrive as regulatory pressure, narrative framing and technical obstruction; "safety versus innovation, stability versus disruption" are false dichotomies — "The real issue is control over value flows."
- Scalability objections are misplaced: "scalability is an engineering problem, not a conceptual one" (the keywords reference TerraNode). "The constraint has historically been design choices, not physical limitations."
- Efficiency gains are redistributed, not destroyed: lower consumer costs, higher producer margins, greater business flexibility — accruing disproportionately to frequent transactors, low-margin operators and those previously excluded.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Structural and institutional economics: functional decomposition of what intermediaries actually do, transaction-cost arithmetic showing compounded extraction, and incentive analysis of why incumbents resist. The method is definitional first (fixing what "digital cash" means) and consequentialist second (tracing what follows when friction is removed).

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- The BTC/Core settlement that high fees, slow confirmation and layered custodial infrastructure (Lightning hubs, exchanges, ETFs) are acceptable or inevitable is rejected root and branch: intermediation is a design failure, not a necessity, and reintroducing tollkeepers on top of Bitcoin betrays its purpose.
- "The constraint has historically been design choices, not physical limitations" is a direct rebuke to small-block fee-market philosophy: throughput limits are chosen, not imposed.
- The mainstream framing of the debate as "safety versus innovation" is dismissed as a false dichotomy protecting incumbent revenue.
- BTC as "digital gold" held through custodians is the opposite of the system described here: money as "a direct instrument", not "a mediated service".
