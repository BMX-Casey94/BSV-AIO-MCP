# Digital signature rules and their relationship to bitcoin
**Date:** 2018-10-16 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/digital-signature-rules-and-their-relationship-to-bitcoin-b1faeae1f446

## Core thesis
Because "the primary transfer mechanism within bitcoin is through the use of a digital signature within the transaction", the enforceability of Bitcoin transfers depends on the digital-signature statutes of each jurisdiction — which Craig surveys across China, the EU/UK, and the US — and so those rules must be "investigated and researched fully" rather than assumed away. A footnoted corollary: where vendors offload checkout to third-party service providers, electronic agents complicate the contractual "meeting of minds" and raise agency questions.

## Key arguments and claims
- The UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Signatures (2001) "has been widely adopted", giving a common international template for e-signature validity.
- China's Electronic Signature Law recognises electronic signatures as "legal and enforceable under Article 14", with Articles 7 and 8 providing admissibility — yet "some judges have been reticent in their recognition and acceptance", so black-letter law alone does not guarantee outcomes.
- The EU's 1999 electronic signature directive (1999/93/EC) founded country-specific laws (including the UK's); it was replaced on 23 July 2014 by the eIDAS regulation (910/2014/EC).
- In the US, ESIGN and UETA provide that a signature will "not be denied legal effect or enforceability due to its form", but state-level implementation varies and some cases require "the prior consent of all parties to conduct business electronically".
- Craig argues "the joint use of bitcoin should imply consent" to electronic transacting — while flagging exclusions around consumer notices and real property transfers as complications.
- The operative claim: since Bitcoin transfers are effected by digital signatures, the signature law of each jurisdiction directly governs bitcoin's legal effectiveness — a research programme, not an afterthought.
- Footnote: electronic agents make "the traditional requirement for a 'meeting of minds' more difficult to prove"; small vendors using external service providers to offload "the Internet shopping trolley function" create uncertainty over whether the customer contracts with the facilitator or the vendor — an agency problem (citing Lim, 2002) that "may influence the process of offer and acceptance".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal comparative-law survey: he moves jurisdiction by jurisdiction (UNCITRAL → China → EU/eIDAS → US ESIGN/UETA), citing instruments by number and date, then maps the legal category onto Bitcoin's technical mechanism (the in-transaction digital signature). The footnote style mimics a legal memorandum, and the argument's force comes from treating statute, not protocol, as the source of transactional validity.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against "code is law": a Bitcoin transfer's legal effect is contingent on national e-signature statutes and judicial willingness, not guaranteed by cryptographic validity alone.
- Against consentless/pseudonymous transacting: he posits implied consent to electronic contracting from "the joint use of bitcoin", importing contract-formation doctrine into what mainstream discourse treats as bearer-instrument settlement.
- Against disintermediation rhetoric: the footnote stresses that most real-world Bitcoin commerce runs through third-party service providers whose agency status must be legally resolved — intermediaries are pervasive, not eliminated.

## Notable quotes
- "As the primary transfer mechanism within bitcoin is through the use of a digital signature within the transaction, it is important that the digital signature rules within each jurisdiction are investigated and researched fully."
- "The joint use of bitcoin should imply consent, but there are certain exclusions surrounding consumer notices and real property transfers that could likely complicate this matter."
- "Despite the clear legal support for electronic signatures within China, some judges have been reticent in their recognition and acceptance."
- "The inclusion of electronic agents makes the traditional requirement for a 'meeting of minds' more difficult to prove."
