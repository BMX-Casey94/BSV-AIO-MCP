## Problem addressed
The specification says a certification authority’s digital certificate stays valid until its printed expiry even if the key is compromised, so relying parties must fetch separately published revocation lists, which are often delayed (it mentions 24 hours or more) and hinder offline checks.

## Core mechanism
A certificate authority authenticates a requester and creates a certification transaction signed by the CA: one output is a CA-controlled P2PKH (or a multi-sig that lets other parties revoke), and a second OP_RETURN (or other information field) carries the certified public key. The transaction identifier plus that public key form the digital certificate. A verifier fetches the transaction by that identifier, checks the CA signature or CA public key on the input, confirms the information-field key matches the certificate, and checks that the CA-controlled output is still in the UTXO pool. Spending that output revokes the certificate at once. A replacement key is certified by a new transaction that spends the old output and writes the new key in OP_RETURN, so revoke and re-issue happen in one transaction. The description names Bitcoin as exemplified by the Bitcoin SV protocol.

## Independent claims (gist — not legal scope)
Independent claim 1 is a verifier method: receive a certificate that contains a public key and a certification-transaction identifier; load that transaction; confirm the first output is unspent; and confirm the information-field public key matches the certificate. Independent claims 10 and 11 are a computing device and a computer-readable medium that carry out that method.

## BSV relevance
The specification itself exemplifies Bitcoin with the Bitcoin SV protocol and uses P2PKH plus OP_RETURN. Recording CA certificates as UTXOs that are revoked by spending is directly applicable to BSV public-key infrastructure.
