# A house divided — core principles

- **There is no permissionless system in the common law.** A construct that sets up gambling, a derivatives exchange, and a means to create and trade unregistered securities acts outside the law; an entity using a smart contract to bring together or execute orders could be providing a trading facility, and one that standardises terms or sets execution priorities could be setting rules (SEC digital-asset statement).
- **Fungibility is a legal term of art, not a technical property.** Money is fungible not through technology but as a result of law: exchange for value in good faith, completed without knowledge of illegal aspects of the tokens' history.
- **No anonymous coin can be legally fungible.** By definition, anonymity destroys the good-faith "without knowledge" condition; merchants have no rights in anonymous and non-fungible systems, and source-of-wealth laws mean you could lose everything if the chain was later analysed.
- **A history-bearing opcode forces a registration body.** A DSV-style chain is constructed with a history, requiring a registration body (for example a PKI certificate authority signing all transactions) and forcing merchants to validate the entire history of every coin — that is not how Bitcoin works.
- **Property rights cannot be forked.** Token systems cover real-world property; you cannot split the rights to many forms of property, and if you are doing this then none of the systems are valid.
- **After a split, only one registry can be valid.** What matters is the one registry that is the most true to the original aim and also the one that is completely acting within the law; a registry that oversteps or accepts non-fungible payments risks being wound up and all goods being returned to former owners.
