# When Money Moves for Free, Who Gets Paid?
**Date:** 2026-03-23 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/when-money-moves-for-free-who-gets
**Subtitle:** The $205 Billion Question: What Scalable Digital Cash Does to Financial Intermediaries

## Core thesis
Scalable blockchain infrastructure collapses the cost of producing trusted transfer to a floor near $0.00000002 per transaction, creating a structural wedge — measurable in orders of magnitude — against the rents banks, card networks and remittance firms earn from settlement friction. Those friction rents face compression, but only if the new settlement layer is genuinely neutral in governance; credit judgement, liquidity transformation and consumer protection are not settlement functions and survive.

## Key arguments and claims
- The deposit franchise: in 2024 US banks held about $3.86 trillion in zero-interest accounts; at a 5.3% Federal Funds Rate the implicit yield exceeded $200 billion, none of it passed to depositors. The bundle (free debit card, branch, transfers) persisted because no unbundled alternative existed.
- Visa and Mastercard sell certainty, not credit — they take no credit risk — and earn $64 billion a year in combined revenue at net margins above 40%.
- Remittances: sending $200 from the US to Nigeria costs on average ~$33 (over 16%), extracted along a correspondent banking chain whose infrastructure has not fundamentally changed in decades.
- The benchmarked floor for cryptographic settlement on scalable blockchain infrastructure is ~$0.00000002 per transaction — a floor, not a production figure, but enough to establish that the gap to legacy pricing is economic rent, not cost recovery.
- The logic extends beyond payments: trade finance (letters of credit are conditional settlement — "pay if and only if conditions are met" — which code can provide), treasury management (banks monetise settlement dead time), and collateral management (multi-day bilateral settlement cycles) all monetise friction that instant, final settlement removes.
- What does *not* change: credit underwriting (judgement, local knowledge, covenants), liquidity transformation (borrowing short, lending long), and fraud/dispute/consumer-protection systems (chargebacks exist because irreversibility is "a feature for some purposes and a catastrophic bug for others").
- The governance catch: cheaper settlement only reorganises rents towards users if the settlement layer is credibly neutral. A chain whose developers can alter fee structures, blacklist addresses or fork at will merely substitutes a less accountable rent-extractor. "Technical decentralization is necessary but not sufficient. Governance decentralization is what actually matters."
- Adjustment is uneven: deposit-float banks face direct substitution pressure (FDIC data show non-interest-bearing deposits fell $1.66 trillion in 2022–2024 as rates rose); remittance firms in high-cost corridors face acute pressure; Visa/Mastercard are harder to contest because their revenue also reflects network effects, brand trust and fraud infrastructure.
- Incumbents already understand the stakes: banks exploring digital-asset custody, card networks running blockchain pilots, and central banks in 130+ countries developing CBDCs are all manoeuvring to control the next settlement layer and inherit its rents.
- Historical pattern: the Medici (correspondent networks), Western Union (telegraph wires) and Visa/Mastercard (authorisation networks) each earned chokepoint rent that was competed away once the cost of building the chokepoint fell towards zero.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Institutional and industrial economics: identify where price exceeds production cost (rent), ask whether technology removes the underlying friction, then separate functions that are genuinely judgment-based from those that are merely toll collection. Historical analogy (Medici → Western Union → Visa) supplies the convergence pattern, and the whole argument is conditioned on governance credibility rather than asserted from technology alone.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects the "blockchain replaces banks" maximalist claim: credit, liquidity transformation and consumer protection are explicitly defended as durable banking functions.
- Rejects token-price and upgrade-cycle discourse as beside the point — the question is whether settlement becomes neutral infrastructure ("the TCP/IP of money") or captured infrastructure.
- Implicitly indicts BTC-style governance: a protocol whose small developer group can change rules, fees and blacklists is a new chokepoint "operating under a decentralization brand", potentially less accountable than regulated banks.
- Diverges from store-of-value BTC logic by treating low-cost, high-volume settlement (micropayment-scale) as the economically decisive use case.

## Notable quotes
- "Where you find a structural wedge between the cost of producing something and the price charged for it, you have found economic rent."
- "If the blockchain that processes your payments is controlled by a small group of developers who can change the fee structure, blacklist addresses, alter transaction rules, or fork the protocol whenever they want — then you haven't escaped a rent-extracting intermediary. You've traded one for another."
- "The ones that do have the potential to deliver genuinely neutral settlement infrastructure — the TCP/IP of money, if you like: infrastructure that competition happens on top of rather than through."
- "It touches every industry where someone earns a living by standing between a payment and its destination."
