# Tax and Bitcoin — Investment in Bitcoin
**Date:** 2018-11-01 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/tax-and-bitcoin-investment-in-bitcoin-40a23e4cbda5
**Subtitle:** Investment in Bitcoin should be treated as with any other investment in an emolument “which could be converted into money might reasonably…

## Core thesis
Investment in Bitcoin should be taxed exactly like any other investment convertible into money: mining expenses are deductible where mining is a profit-seeking business, bitcoin trading is analogous to foreign-exchange trading and taxed under the same provisions, and gains on disposal fall within the traditional-securities regime of s 26BB. A short essay of three doctrinal mappings, each resting on existing Australian statute and High Court authority rather than any new law.

## Key arguments and claims
- Governing principle: Bitcoin investment is an emolument "which could be converted into money might reasonably be regarded as money", and should be "taxed under the same provisions" as any other investment.
- Mining as business: "Bitcoin mining is a competitive business. Mining only makes sense when done very efficiently and for a profit" — and mining contracts "are used in securing the network".
- Deductibility of mining expenses: under Steele v DFC of T (1999) 41 ATR 139, expenses are allowable deductions where the taxpayer "had the objective of deriving income in the future" — applied directly to a bitcoin-mining enterprise.
- General deduction principle: FCT v Finn (1961) 106 CLR 60 (Dixon CJ) — expenditure "in gaining or producing assessable income" is deductible; "It is our submission that the same rule applies to Bitcoin-based transactions."
- Trading as forex: "The trading in Bitcoin is analogous to foreign exchange trading, and should be taxed under the same provisions", citing ITAA97 s 775-30 and the principles decided in TC of T v Munro (1926) 38 CLR 153.
- Dealers and trading stock: where a taxpayer carries on a business dealing in securities, realisation gains are income under s 6-5; for financial intermediaries, commercial houses and insurers, even reorganisation of investments yields income, and in dealers' hands the instruments are trading stock.
- s 26BB traditional securities: applies to bonds, debentures and the like — not Div 16E qualifying securities, shares or trading stock — catching instruments with no "eligible return" or a return below the 1.5% benchmark, and assessing any gain realised on redemption.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The method is analogy-by-classification in the compressed voice of a tax submission ("It is our submission that…"): each bitcoin activity — mining, trading, disposal — is matched to an existing statutory regime with appellate authority, rather than argued from first principles. The brevity is the point: nothing about Bitcoin resists the existing categories, so the essay spends its length on citations, not controversy.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the "crypto gains are untaxable / invisible to the state" folklore of the era: mining, trading and disposal each already have a taxing regime with decades of precedent.
- Contradicts the claim that Bitcoin is sui generis and unclassifiable under existing law: forex-trading provisions and the traditional-securities regime suffice by straightforward analogy.
- Contradicts hobbyist-miner assumptions: mining "only makes sense when done very efficiently and for a profit", and it is precisely that business characterisation which unlocks deductibility of expenses.
