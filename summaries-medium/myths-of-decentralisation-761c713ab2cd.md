# Myths of Decentralisation…
**Date:** 2020-02-06 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/myths-of-decentralisation-761c713ab2cd
**Subtitle:** There is a completely false narrative that you do not hold bitcoin and that they are an intangible distributed across many machines around…

## Core thesis
Craig attacks the "completely false narrative" that bitcoins are intangible objects distributed across the network: individuals hold their own tokens in their own wallets, and only the ledger — a time-ordering transaction log — is distributed. Bitcoin is a peer-to-peer token exchange whose property rights exist "because of law", not code; all tokens were issued at the January 2009 launch and allocated algorithmically to commercial nodes; and the system was deliberately built as a competitive "giant node" that simulates a central trusted intermediary without single-entity fragility. The Medium post is an excerpt; the body continues on craigwright.net.

## Key arguments and claims
- "Individuals hold their own bitcoin... Your bitcoin is not held in the ledger. Your bitcoin is held in your wallet or related application"; the blockchain "acts as a transaction record and log, recording the control and movement of the tokens".
- "The ledger in Bitcoin is distributed. The ledger is a transaction log" — the ledger exists to fix the correct version of a token and ignore later double-spend attempts ("the ledger maintains a list of what was seen first").
- Bitcoin is "a set of individual and indivisible tokens... It does not consist of an account" — ownership attaches to tokens as intangible digital property, comparable to the carbon-trading units treated as property in Armstrong DLW GmbH v Winnington Networks Ltd; bitcoin can be taken as "a chose (thing) in action".
- "Every single token in the Bitcoin system has been allocated (issued)... in January 2009 upon the launch of the system" — a complete issue at launch, allocated algorithmically to commercial nodes under published rules; "The white paper acts as the equivalent of the software's EULA."
- "Bitcoin does not use any encryption... All transactions in Bitcoin are conducted in clear text" — digital signatures are not encryption and "do not stop access to files".
- Peer-to-peer means layered networks: the section-5 nodes are "highly connected commercial systems that are designed to end in data centres" competing for fees; users were catered for by SPV, and "the original client was set to use IP-to-IP connectivity" so exchanging peers communicate directly without "a widely distributed network".
- Bitcoin "used a distributed peer-to-peer system acting as a network giant node to simulate the central trusted intermediary without losing out to the typical fragility of a single entity. All companies eventually fail" — nodes come and go "in a loosely structured mutualised association", removing the PKI single-point fragility of prior timestamp authorities.
- Lineage: the white paper's second reference (Massias et al., work "dating back to 1991") created the time-chain concept — a single trusted entity publishing block headers daily in a newspaper; Bitcoin's innovation is replacing that single PKI-based timestamp authority with "a competitive group... structured as a parallelised quorum".
- Enforcement: "All information is maintained publicly and auditable, allowing a defecting or attacking system to be detected quickly", whereupon honest nodes isolate it "and use computer crime legislation including the US federal CFAA laws to ensure that the offending node is brought to justice".
- "Bitcoin does not replace banking or banks... the false assertion that code is law has long been discredited"; "Property rights in Bitcoin exist because of law. The ledger is merely a time-ordering and logging system."
- Because tokens are individually assignable intangible property, "the transfer of bitcoin may be invalid, and theft remains possible"; citing Fox, a single valid token at any point lets bitcoin "be treated as a tangible document which is capable of being possessed"; citing Harris, Bitcoin removes "the notion of artificial scarcity" used to justify IP law.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Legal-realist property theory layered over a revisionist network topology. He separates possession, control, ownership, and record — categories the community collapses into "the coins are on the blockchain" — and grounds each in case law (Armstrong v Winnington), property scholarship (Fox, Harris), and the white paper's own references (Massias et al. 1991/1999 timestamping). The method is definitional discipline: once the ledger is correctly redescribed as a notarial log and nodes as commercial data-centre actors, "decentralisation" shrinks from a political property to an engineering redundancy feature, and law re-enters as the source of rights.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the standard popular description that "bitcoins live on the blockchain / are distributed across thousands of machines": tokens are held by individuals; only the log is distributed.
- Contradicts "code is law" and the extra-legal property claim: property rights in bitcoin exist because of law; transfers can be legally invalid and theft legally remediable — the premise of his later coin-recovery litigation.
- Contradicts the "Bitcoin kills banks" rhetoric: "Bitcoin does not replace banking or banks" and "presents not a political statement".
- Contradicts the running-full-node user-sovereignty ideal: section-5 nodes are commercial data-centre competitors; users were always meant to use SPV and direct IP-to-IP exchange.
- Contradicts the "no trusted third party" origin story: Bitcoin deliberately simulates a central trusted intermediary (a "giant node" quorum), improving on the single timestamp authority of Massias et al. rather than abolishing trust.
- Contradicts the encryption framing of "crypto"currency: Bitcoin uses no encryption; everything is clear text with digital signatures.
