# Digital signature rules and their relationship to bitcoin — core principles

- **Signature statutes govern Bitcoin transfers.** "The primary transfer mechanism within bitcoin is through the use of a digital signature within the transaction", so the enforceability of a transfer depends on each jurisdiction's digital-signature rules, which must be "investigated and researched fully" — cryptographic validity alone does not guarantee legal effect.
- **International template.** The UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Signatures (2001) "has been widely adopted", supplying a common baseline for e-signature validity.
- **China.** The Electronic Signature Law recognises electronic signatures as "legal and enforceable under Article 14", with Articles 7 and 8 covering admissibility — yet "some judges have been reticent in their recognition and acceptance", so black-letter law alone does not assure outcomes.
- **EU/UK.** The 1999 electronic-signature directive (1999/93/EC) founded country-specific laws, replaced on 23 July 2014 by the eIDAS regulation (910/2014/EC).
- **US.** ESIGN and UETA provide that a signature will "not be denied legal effect or enforceability due to its form", but state implementation varies and some cases require "the prior consent of all parties to conduct business electronically".
- **Implied consent.** "The joint use of bitcoin should imply consent" to electronic transacting — subject to exclusions around consumer notices and real-property transfers.
- **Electronic agents complicate formation.** Third-party checkout providers make "the traditional requirement for a 'meeting of minds' more difficult to prove" and raise an agency question — whether the customer contracts with the facilitator or the vendor (citing Lim 2002) — which "may influence the process of offer and acceptance".
