# Protocol as Offer
**Date:** 2026-01-24 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/protocol-as-offer
**Subtitle:** Unilateral Contract Doctrine as the Legal Grammar of Proof-of-Work Performance, Finality, and Rule-Change

## Core thesis
A proof-of-work protocol publishes an intelligible bargain — any actor may undertake a costly, objectively specified act, and on completion becomes entitled to a defined reward — which is the familiar structure of a unilateral offer inviting acceptance by performance. Ordinary unilateral-contract doctrine, above all Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 45, supplies the correct legal grammar for proof-of-work performance, finality and rule-change, without romanticising code or pretending that code dissolves law.

## Key arguments and claims
- Two misdescriptions dominate: the system as purely internal technical "rules", or as a social order whose meaning is determined by community governance. Both dodge the commercial fact of a published bargain that induces costly reliance.
- Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 45 is the key device: where an offer invites acceptance by performance, beginning performance creates an option-like lock-in — the offer cannot be revoked while the offeree completes, though the duty to pay remains conditional on completion. This protects reliance without paying for mere effort.
- The invited act is not "finding a low hash" but producing a protocol-valid block, accepted under the objective selection rule (greatest accumulated proof-of-work), and held through the finality/maturity horizon (a confirmation depth after which the reward becomes spendable). Validity, acceptance and finality are rule-defined conditions precedent to entitlement.
- Orphaned blocks are misanalysed as "payment taken away": the correct analysis is that the condition precedent did not occur — the offer defined completion to include persistence through finality. Pre-maturity displacement is a risk of the bargain internalised by participants as a cost of competing.
- Public-offer doctrine: courts enforce clear, definite, explicit public offers and refuse to let offerors add "house rules" after acceptance is attempted by performance. A rule-change altering acceptance conditions after performance has begun is an attempted midstream revocation or modification, constrained by § 45's option-like effect.
- Proof-of-work is best described as a rolling series of unilateral offers on a stable specification, not one perpetual contract: each interval publishes the same invitation, induces fresh reliance, and becomes locked against opportunistic revocation once performance begins. The existence of future intervals does not justify retroactive alteration of the current interval's terms.
- A disciplined taxonomy of protocol change follows: prospective changes are new offers for future performance opportunities; retroactive rewrites of conditions governing performance already underway are legally charged modifications. Rule-change is not neutral "maintenance".
- Authority claims become justiciable: operational capacity to publish code or coordinate releases is not legal authority to bind others to retroactive term changes. Courts ask ordinary questions — who had authority to modify offer terms, whether assent was obtained where required, whether an enforceable reserved power of modification exists, and whether reliance and commencement constrain revocation.
- Invalid performance is non-acceptance, not breach and not discretionary "governance": a block violating validity rules is an attempt to perform a different act; no contract forms for that attempt, so no entitlement exists. Breach presupposes a contract.
- Illegality is handled by ordinary doctrine: an implied condition of legality, unlawful demanded performance treated as outside the invited act, and illegality defences limiting enforcement.
- Remedies are conventional: expectation damages for impermissible revocation after completion (the loss being the promised reward as defined by the offer); promissory estoppel as a targeted reliance backstop where formation is disputed; declaratory relief as often the most valuable remedy (declaring the operative offer terms, whether a change was retroactive or prospective, and whether conditions were satisfied); and narrow equitable relief against opportunistic retroactive manipulation, tailored to avoid continuous technical supervision.
- The institutional-competence objection fails: courts routinely decide disputes over technical conditions precedent, and protocol criteria are explicit and objective — rule-defined validity, numerical finality thresholds — making adjudication easier rather than harder.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
US contract doctrine applied as classification: characterise the protocol specification as offer terms, performance as acceptance, and rule-changes as revocation or modification, then let the settled machinery of conditions precedent, the § 45 option lock-in, reliance and remedies run. The stance is doctrinally conservative throughout — the medium is new, the legal structure old.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects the "social consensus" framing in which community governance determines the system's meaning: questions waved away as "governance" become justiciable questions about offer terms, acceptance conditions and modification authority.
- Rejects the characterisation of protocol rule-changes as ordinary "maintenance": retroactive rewrites are constrained modifications of offers on which miners have relied by commencing performance.
- Rejects pure "code is law" internalism (rules with purely internal effects) equally with governance romanticism — both framings dodge what the system actually does.
- Treats rule stability as legally protected reliance: a stable specification grounds a rolling series of offers, and midstream change is legally charged — the opposite of an upgrade-by-rough-consensus ethos.
