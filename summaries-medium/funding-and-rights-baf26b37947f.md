# Funding and rights
**Date:** 2019-05-20 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/funding-and-rights-baf26b37947f
**Subtitle:** My personal bitcoin and the ones owned by the company were two different things. The ones that were being used by the company were not…

## Core thesis
A confessional account of how Craig's pre-2012 fortune — about US$30 million of casino-software earnings held in Caribbean trusts — was converted into bitcoin via a Panama law firm holding "rights to bitcoin", and how "a little over 100,000 bitcoin" vanished in late 2015 when the Panama operators disappeared, partly an inside job by ex-staff. The lesson he draws is about custody, audit and control rather than "not your keys" absolutism — and that Tether, Bitfinex and Binance balances are the same kind of mere "digital rights", while Bitcoin's only real value is use as ledger and cash.

## Key arguments and claims
- Personal and company bitcoin were kept distinct; company coins were never brought into Australia. Because "we didn't know about the tax certainty of bitcoin at the time", holdings were structured as "rights to bitcoin" managed overseas — a Panama law firm instructed by Bitmessage letters made payments. "When you have rights to bitcoin that are held on a custodial wallet with an exchange, you have the right to funds but not the funds themselves."
- The money came from casino software: "In 2011, I had around US$30 million in Caribbean bank accounts... all in structured trusts and companies", kept offshore to avoid direct Australian tax; the Panama firm bought bitcoin with it through the 2011–12 bubble and crash (15–16 months to recover value).
- The loss: "In 2015, we still had bitcoin in overseas holdings... The biggest problem came when a number of people involved in Panama vanished... It's not too hard to vanish with 100,000 bitcoin in South America." "At the time, we had remaining rights to a little over 100,000 bitcoin. None of them were early bitcoin that I had mined."
- "I know it was partly an inside job": ex-staff with finance-industry contacts had access to company file shares; a backdoor persisted in his system "nearly 2 years after a staff member had no longer been working for me" and 15+ months after cease-and-desist injunctions — discovered only through the Kleiman case. "That's my biggest failing. It was my hubris which led to the loss."
- The lesson is not simplistic self-custody: "It taught me the lesson of not having control of your system. Not holding your keys. It's wrong, it misses the point. In any large organisation, shareholders are not going to be managing funds, nor are directors." The real lesson is "audit and control" — he had been an auditor and "should have known better".
- What the money bought: a US$6 million computer plus hosting, a later US$15 million-a-year computer-services deal, access to commercial core-banking source code (the Rubik/Temenos deal) and a full SCADA control suite — all to research "the exact effects of Bitcoin over time as it scales... the inflection points of my system". "The 1,300 or so white papers that we have created and which are all going to form multiple patents would not exist, if I hadn't done so."
- Scale of the windfall: "My US$30 million became US$300 million. And I wasn't ready for it... it scares me shitless even now." Current trust controls mean "no way anyone can obtain anything from the trust without the conditions I have set to be enforced — not even if I wanted someone to".
- "Things like Bitfinex, Tether, and Binance are just digital rights" — exchange balances replicate his Panama experience: hacks, embezzlement, disappearances, other people's money going missing. "There won't be an ETF or anything else involving institutional money anytime soon because we have a system of wash trading, embezzlement, and fraud."
- Value thesis: "If Bitcoin isn't used, it has no value." Digital gold already exists as gold certificates, and "digital gold is only valuable as a digital money-laundering asset. Bitcoin is only valuable when it is used fundamentally as a ledger and as cash."
- "If somebody is telling you that bitcoin will go to the moon, he is scamming you." Price-pumping via wash trades and social-media manipulation is the actual fraud — "you have to make money through the actions that you're taking", and he is asking for nothing.
- On anonymity: "When I created Bitcoin, I didn't create something to be anonymous. I wanted private, which is not true anonymity... I want to create money that is traceable" — unlike BTC, Monero and BCH, which he says were altered to help organised crime.
- Court preview: "post by post, I'm going to start talking about what is going to happen when I go to court... in court, it's not the lies and the proof of social media that work; the laws of evidence work."
- Cites Louis D. Brandeis, *Other People's Money and How the Bankers Use It*, as a favourite: "If you would only recognize that life is hard, things would be so much easier for you."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Confessional autobiography deployed as credibility strategy: admitting embarrassing losses ("my hubris", "it scares me shitless") purchases authority for the structural critique. He reasons through legal-financial form — rights versus possession, trusts, custody, duty of care — to collapse the distinction between his Panama structure and modern exchanges, then inverts the fraud accusation with a cui bono test: fraud requires profiting from others, and the pumpers, not the builder, meet that test.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Complicates "not your keys, not your coins": calls it "wrong, it misses the point" for any large organisation, substituting audit and control for self-custody absolutism.
- Demolishes the digital-gold/store-of-value narrative: digital gold already exists and serves only money laundering; Bitcoin's value is exclusively use as ledger and cash.
- "Moon" rhetoric is defined as scamming, and the ETF/institutional-money narrative is impossible atop wash trading — against 2019's institutional-accumulation orthodoxy.
- Privacy ≠ anonymity: he rejects the anonymity goal claimed by cypherpunks and attributes BTC/Monero/BCH's anonymity features to organised-crime enablement.
- Exchange balances (including Tether) are reclassified from assets to mere "digital rights" — a direct attack on the era's custodial trading culture.
