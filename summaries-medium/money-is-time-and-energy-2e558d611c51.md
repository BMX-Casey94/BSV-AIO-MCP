# Money Is Time and Energy
**Date:** 2020-05-05 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/money-is-time-and-energy-2e558d611c51
**Subtitle:** Many falsely believe that by simply producing more money or redistributing it, we would all suddenly have more wealth…

## Core thesis
A Medium excerpt of a longer essay — the captured body ends at the "Money and Bitcoin" section heading and continues on craigwright.net. Craig argues that money is not wealth but a standardised mapping of obligations through time: printing or redistributing money cannot create wealth, because money's value is set by the ratio of freely circulating money to available goods and services. Inflation follows when money growth outpaces goods growth; gold's only virtue was the discipline it imposed on money-supply expansion, a discipline undermined by unpredictable new discoveries. His conclusion for Bitcoin, reached just as the excerpt cuts off: "Bitcoin doesn't replace banks. It is an electronic cash system and not a banking system."

## Key arguments and claims
- Creating money — cash or government loan instruments "replicated across other banks and financial institutions" — does not create wealth.
- Definition: "Money is simply a means of mapping obligations. It is a way of sending obligations through time." Barter extended by futures contracts (a cow delivered today against 10 sacks of barley in six months) produces non-standard money; standardisation follows because bespoke monies make multi-party trade unworkable.
- Historical genealogy: grain stores in early Middle Eastern and Chinese societies issued tally sticks, knotted strings and clay tablets representing deposited grain; "the first writing and counting systems involved the tracking of commodity assets".
- Combinatorial argument for a unit of account: two merchants each holding three different items already face nine possible direct exchanges, and partial transfers make it worse; a common ledger unit collapses the "factorial calculation".
- "Money has formed as a universal societal contract in nearly all human societies."
- Unit weights as early money: the Hebrew bekah ≈ 6.02 grams, the shekel 11.4 grams, the talent = 3,000 shekels ≈ 75.5 pounds of silver; pre-coinage silver was weighed out and cut from stretched sheets or wire.
- Proportional-claim systems: holding 10% of tally sticks could entitle 10% of the grain store — mutual insurance where "each rights holder would suffer an equal fate". "Modern fiat money operates similarly": issued promissory rights on one side, obligations and goods on the other.
- Gold has no intrinsic monetary value: "Gold is a marker when used as money for other goods and services." At best it is a stable form of cash whose redemption requirement constrains bank lending.
- What sets money's exchange rate is not total supply but the amount of *free* money against available goods — money "held by people who are unwilling to move it" is effectively unavailable; risk preference and interest-free credit-card periods reduce the need to hold cash.
- Inflation arithmetic: if goods grow 100 → 110 → 121 units while money supply grows 10%, "the introduction of a 10% increase in the money supply will… lead to an inflation rate of zero". The danger is when "the money supply continues to increase but the amount of goods or services has failed to increase at an equal rate" — "more money can be chasing fewer goods and services". With 100 units of money and only 50 units of goods, "it will now take two monetary units to purchase one unit of goods".
- The 20th century's "previously unthought of" growth is what let governments expand money supply while maintaining low inflation; the problem arrives when growth stalls.
- "Gold was never a cash-based system": coins were too expensive and difficult for small daily payments; gold settled large-value items, and the gold in circulation "was always far more limited than the amount of money in circulation".
- "The value of gold lies in the limitations it imposes" — a natural bound on monetary expansion and a universal measuring stick — but unpredictable new gold discoveries "acted as a black swan, radically distorting the global monetary system".
- "Bitcoin doesn't replace banks. It is an electronic cash system and not a banking system… Banks don't replace cash, and Bitcoin doesn't replace banks."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Historical-anthropological genealogy (granaries, tally sticks, biblical weights) building into a quantity-theory-of-money model, which he then demonstrates with worked arithmetic examples. He defines terms precisely before deploying them (money as obligation-mapping; free vs held money), and is explicitly anti-gold-bug — rejecting intrinsic value while crediting gold's disciplinary function. The mode is didactic and monetarist rather than Austrian.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- "Bitcoin is digital gold / hard money that replaces central banks": he denies gold has intrinsic value, denies gold was ever a cash system, and casts Bitcoin as cash — not a gold analogue or a bank replacement.
- The inflation-hedge investment thesis (peak orthodoxy during the COVID-era QE of May 2020): inflation is a ratio phenomenon between free money and goods, so growth can absorb money expansion with "an inflation rate of zero"; scarcity of supply alone does not confer value.
- "Bitcoin fixes fractional-reserve banking / ends debt": Bitcoin is cash, not a banking system, so banking and credit creation are simply outside its scope.
- Crypto's anti-bank ethos: banks remain necessary; "Bitcoin doesn't replace banks."
