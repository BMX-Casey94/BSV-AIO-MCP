# The tax implications of bitcoin as money — core principles

- **The bypass fallacy.** Because Bitcoin needs no financial institution to hold or process it, "many people have concluded that they can bypass tax obligations" — disintermediation does not touch tax liability (against Omri Marian's "Are Cryptocurrencies Super Tax Havens?", Michigan Law Review 2013).
- **Heads of tax.** "There are various areas of taxation law that need to be addressed. This includes incorporation of bitcoin into Income tax, Capital gains tax and under foreign exchange rules."
- **Use-dependence principle.** "The treatment bitcoin will depend in part on its use" — the same coin attracts different tax outcomes depending on whether it is mined, held, spent as currency, used "as a token representing the sale of other assets", embedded in a smart contract, or exchanged.
- **Smart contracts inherit contract-law uncertainty.** "Contractual formation is inherently uncertain in and of itself" (Gamage & Kedem 2006), and electronic-contracts legislation "cannot in itself remove contractual uncertainty" — so smart-contract tax treatment inherits that uncertainty rather than escaping legal categories.
- **Authorities were already watching.** The US GAO's May 2013 report to the Senate Finance Committee recorded ~11 million bitcoin in circulation (1 May 2013), prices moving from $5–$20 to "$79 and over $237", and "8,000 to 70,000 transactions per day" — an evidentiary baseline for tax enforcement.
