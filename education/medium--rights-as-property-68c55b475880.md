# Rights as property — core principles

- **The simple transfer already fits existing law.** "In its simplest form, a bitcoin transaction involves the creation of an entry in a ledger" reassigning value to a new key pair and address, and "this direct assignment of value across a ledger is not difficult to represent against existing money transfer and currency rules".
- **The hard research areas.** Multi-signature and multi-party addresses; conditional smart contracts; on-chain information embedding and "the tokenisation of real property and chattel goods"; theft and fraudulent procurement; and international, cross-jurisdictional assignments.
- **Offer and acceptance is an open legal question.** "Does the transmission of a Bitcoin transaction constitute acceptance, inclusion into 6 blocks, etc?" — the software-licence model (loading software indicates acceptance) cannot be ported to Bitcoin because the user has no opportunity to view terms before acting: "This does not exist within Bitcoin."
- **Money-transmission classification.** FinCEN guidance FIN-2008-G008 (10 Sept 2008) applies the money-transmitter definition to brokers and dealers in currency and commodities, excluding persons "registered with, and regulated or examined by" the CFTC.
- **Receipt rules diverge across jurisdictions.** Parties may reside in a different state from where the wallet is accessed (Hyde v Wrench 1840; Treitel 2003); receipt-time rules differ (Art. 1335 Italian Civil Code; US Restatement 2d of Contracts §56; Germany RGZ 144, 292).
- **A transaction can take effect in two places.** Receipt occurs "when it arrives on the client's wallet or if it is accepted in the blockchain at an agreed depth" — so "the timing and even validity of an offer and acceptance to a contract may come into dispute and may even come into effect in two or more places" (Apple Corps Limited v Apple Computer, Inc. [2004]).
