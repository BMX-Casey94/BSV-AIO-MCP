# Order On-Chain, Content Off-Chain, Judgement in the Overlay
**Date:** 2026-08-11T00:44:31.517Z | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/order-on-chain-content-off-chain
**Subtitle:** A scaled token ledger cannot make a model reason, see, or know what it does not know.

## Core thesis
This capstone to a ten-part series on open problems in AI argues that every problem in the series shares one evidential defect: a claim whose value depends on when it was fixed, asserted by the party who benefits, with no way for anyone else to check the order of events. A scaled public ledger supplies exactly one missing primitive — a public, ordered, tamper-evident, independently verifiable record of commitments, cheap enough to use per event — and nothing else. The engineering question is therefore not whether to put AI on a chain, but which of three tiers (ledger, content-addressed store, overlay) each artefact belongs in, decided by one exact rule: “Does the meaning of this thing depend on when it was fixed?”

## Key arguments and claims
- Findings carried forward from the series: a frontier model solves about a third of instances in a classical planning domain and one in six hundred with names obfuscated; where a model is its own verifier the measured result is collapse, not improvement; chains of thought shift by tens of points under a bias the explanation never mentions; token probability is not claim probability; causal structure has “a price list denominated in interventions per latent variable”; continual-learning claims are evaluated as a mean over a few dozen benchmarks, biased upward by an estimator taking a maximum over noisy history.
- A public chain provides exactly four things: a total order (block height and position, agreed by mutually distrusting parties); a timestamp bound (bounds lateness, not earliness — proves existence by time T, not creation at T, and says nothing about truth); tamper-evidence (“not ‘immutability’ as a metaphysical property; it is a cost, and the cost is what you are buying”); and verification without trusting the recorder (block headers plus a Merkle path, checked in time proportional to the log of the batch size, without holding the chain).
- Six things a ledger is not: it does not make anything true (“Notarised nonsense is nonsense with a receipt”; “a hash of a lie is a durable lie”); it does not verify computation; it carries no semantics (a hash without preimage and schema is “an unbreakable promise about an unknown thing”); it is not private by default (low-entropy preimages fall to dictionary attack, so commitments must be salted); it does not delete; it does not guarantee availability.
- Scaled versus constrained ledgers differ not in primitives but in affordable granularity: “an audit trail with daily resolution cannot adjudicate a dispute about what happened at 10:04.”
- Three tiers: the ledger is public and expensive to alter, therefore small; the store is large and cheap, content-addressed so a corrupted copy is “a detectably different object” — it needs availability, not integrity; the overlay holds judgement, which must be revisable. “The joins are hashes going up and proofs coming down. That is the whole protocol.”
- The assignment rule: commit whatever’s meaning depends on when it was fixed; version whatever is bulk and immutable; compute in the overlay whatever is a judgement. What is committed for a judgement is the attestation — who computed what, over which inputs, with which checker version, producing which result.
- The commit list: the forecast before resolution; the premise set before execution; the capability register and probe set before the update; the intervention schedule before the data; model and artefact identity (hashes of weights, tokenizer, configuration, evaluation harness, checker versions); and the attestation of every verdict. “Every item is small, every item is a decision, and every item’s value would be destroyed by the ability to change it quietly.”
- The store’s discipline is a hash chain: each version names its predecessor, making history self-authenticating; anchors supply time. “Version continuously, anchor periodically”, at least as often as the finest time resolution at which a dispute could turn; the error cost is asymmetric (over-anchoring wastes a little money; under-anchoring means the record cannot answer the question eventually asked). Because content lives here, it can be destroyed: “The commitment survives the content” — the only structural answer to the deletion problem.
- Overlay checks include: a deterministic gear calculation (“a couple of hundred lines”) deciding whether a thousand-step plan can possibly work; a plan validator; constraint critics checking an itinerary against thirteen conditions; a calibration scorer; a contradiction detector maintaining a graph of incompatible claims; an invariant monitor; and a capability register reporting the distribution, not the mean. Verdicts are revisable, records are not; anyone can run an overlay, and disagreement between two overlays over the same committed inputs “is a well-formed dispute”.
- Area-by-area verdicts across the ten open problems: calibrated uncertainty is the strongest fit (“the difference between a score and an assertion”); long-horizon planning strong (post-hoc rationalisation becomes detectable); continual learning strong (pre-committed capability register and probe set “kill the selection problem”, and per-capability results give a longitudinal record); alignment/scalable oversight strong for one narrow reason — oversight requires “a record the overseen party cannot revise” — while the elicitation problem (a weak supervisor recovering half the performance gap on language tasks, scaling negatively on harder ones) is untouched; causal world models strong for provenance, silent on the science; robustness/security partial (append-only incident registry, and fee-priced access as a Sybil-resistant anti-abuse primitive — “rate-limiting by cost rather than by identity”); embodied intelligence partial but economically interesting (the largest open robot dataset pooled sixty datasets from twenty-one institutions; per-item settlement — “contribute a trajectory, be paid when it is used” — is “available and unused”); mechanistic interpretability gets no help (“Anyone selling a ledger as a contribution to interpretability is padding”); a predictive theory gains scoreable forecasts, “a sociological improvement”; efficiency is “a cost, not a benefit” — “verification has always cost more than assertion”.
- Throughput arithmetic: an agent taking a thousand actions per session across ten thousand sessions a day is ten million events; per-event commitment is absurd and unnecessary. Batched into a Merkle tree, one thirty-two-byte root covers a session; an inclusion proof for a thousand claims is ten sibling hashes (320 bytes), for a million claims twenty hashes (640 bytes) — logarithmic growth. Ten million events become ten thousand transactions: “a root per session, not a transaction per claim”.
- Why unit cost and capacity still bind: commitment must precede the act, so the batching window is an unprotected delay set by the domain; dispute resolution can only be as fine as the anchoring; the scaling is in the number of committers (ten thousand independent agents cannot batch across each other “because the whole point is that they do not trust each other”); and attribution requires per-item settlement — “A fee floor above the value of the item does not make micropayment expensive; it makes it impossible, and the entire class of designs disappears.” The honest requirement: “commitment latency measured in seconds rather than days, from an unbounded number of independent parties, at a unit cost below the value of the smallest thing worth attributing.”
- Deletion versus immutability is “opposed at the core, not at the margin”. The bounded answer: commit salted hashes, hold content off-chain, destroy content and salt together, leaving an unopenable commitment. This satisfies audit and content-erasure requirements but not any requirement to erase the fact a record existed. Beneath it sits a harder problem no ledger touches: a model trained on a datum has been changed by it; the architecture can prove which data entered which training run but cannot remove the influence — “I am not going to pretend a hash tree addresses it.”
- What the system does not buy: a committed benchmark is still not a result; a committed output is non-repudiable but unverified (“The most common design error will be committing model outputs and describing the result as trustworthy AI”); it is no substitute for an executable specification; it does not run the availability service (“A hash whose preimage is gone is a proof of your own negligence”); and it does not produce anyone who checks — “if the answer is nobody, then the record is a filing cabinet”.
- The combined system: commit plan hash, premise list, per-premise confidences and checker identities before acting; commit invariant results during execution, batched at a window set by the stakes; artefacts go to the store anchored by root; overlays recompute, score and publish attestations, disputable by second overlays. The gain is not correctness but checkability: failure becomes “attributable to a decision made at a known time on a known basis”. “That is a smaller claim than the industry usually makes about ledgers, and a much larger one than it usually makes about its own evidence.”

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The method is an information-and-verification argument executed with deliberate scope-limiting. He first enumerates exhaustively what the primitive does (four properties) and provably does not do (six non-properties), then derives a single-question assignment rule, then stress-tests it domain by domain with honest negative verdicts, and finally grounds the design in throughput arithmetic (Merkle-tree logarithmic scaling) and microeconomics (unit cost below the smallest attributable value). The proof machinery is evidential rather than computational: the ledger converts “we fixed this in advance” from assertion into checkable fact, and its value is presented as coming precisely from its narrowness.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against small blocks and fee markets: unbounded capacity and negligible unit cost are “the enabling condition”; “A fee floor above the value of the item does not make micropayment expensive; it makes it impossible.”
- Against the view that non-monetary data does not belong on a chain: high-volume commitment anchoring (forecasts, premises, attestations, Merkle roots) is treated as a legitimate, indeed necessary, use — while conversely he rejects the opposite maximalism of putting data itself on-chain (ledger carries commitments only; content stays off-chain).
- Against full-node universalism: verification works “without holding the chain and without asking the party who wrote it”, via headers and Merkle paths — the SPV model rather than universal replication.
- Against crypto-industry “on-chain so it is trustworthy” rhetoric: “Any architecture whose value proposition is ‘the data is on-chain so it is trustworthy’ is confused at the first step.”
