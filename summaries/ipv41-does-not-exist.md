# IPv4.1 Does Not Exist
**Date:** 2026-05-31 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/ipv41-does-not-exist
**Subtitle:** To widen the field is to write a new protocol.

## Core thesis
A protocol is an agreement — a set of rules strangers share exactly — so changing the shared wire format is never an "upgrade" but the authoring of a new protocol. The internet's engineers widened the address field from 32 to 128 bits and had the honesty to name the result IPv6, a successor, not "IPv4.1". Those who would widen Bitcoin's unit field want a successor that keeps the old name, history and reputation — and the bridges they build between the chains are the confession of the rupture, not its reconciliation.

## Key arguments and claims
- A protocol is not a product, brand, market capitalisation or community; the rules are the protocol and there is nothing else there. "Compatibility is not a virtue a protocol possesses. It is the only thing a protocol is."
- IPv4 (RFC 791, 1981) fixed a 32-bit address field — about 4.29 billion addresses; IPv6 (RFC 2460, 1998; RFC 8200, 2017) widened it to 128 bits and is specified in writing as "a new version of the Internet Protocol", "designed as the successor" to the fourth.
- A field cannot be widened in place: a machine expecting 32 bits reads a 128-bit address as nonsense, and no authority can update every machine at the same instant. The two protocols therefore coexist, communicating only through dual stacks, tunnels and translators — machinery whose existence proves they are two protocols, not one.
- Bitcoin's value is recorded as a fixed-width integer count of satoshis, validated by every node independently against a fixed money-range ceiling. Redefining the base unit changes the shared wire format and the ceiling, so old-rule and new-rule nodes reject each other's blocks: a hard fork — a successor by construction.
- The moral divergence: the engineers "numbered the break into the name"; the monetary reformer "wants to be called the fourth version while behaving as the sixth" — the impostor son who answers to the father's name and signs the father's will.
- The demand for the old name is the unfailing tell: "One does not fight to inherit a reputation one has not abandoned." Honesty would have renumbered; dishonesty keeps the number and changes everything beneath it.
- The bridge or peg between the old chain and the renumbered chain is the exact monetary counterpart of IPv4–IPv6 tunnels and translators: "The bridge is not the reconciliation of the schism. The bridge is the schism, rendered in machinery, signed and dated."
- Identity is decided by one thing only — fidelity to the rule. Not market size, not evangelist volume, not custody of the brand: "The original is the chain that kept the rule."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Definition-first essentialism plus structural analogy: fix the noun (a protocol is an agreement, compatibility its self-definition), import the standards-history precedent of IPv4→IPv6 as a controlled experiment, map it joint by joint onto the coin's integer field, then read naming choices morally — candour compiled into a single digit versus continuity stolen by marketing.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Denies that consensus-breaking changes are "upgrades": an agreement changed without unanimous consent is replaced, not amended — there is no third door.
- Denies that protocol identity follows market capitalisation, popularity or ticker custody: identity is fidelity to the invariant, so the chain that kept the rule is the original "however small its banner".
- Reframes bridges, pegs and wrapped-asset machinery — sold as signs of ecosystem continuity — as proof that two protocols exist where there was one.
- Implicitly grounds the BSV claim: the successor "may inherit the earth" but "is not the original, and the only honest thing it can do is… take a new number."

## Notable quotes
