# Banking on Bitcoin — core principles

- **Holding bitcoin is holding cash, not banking.** Banks allocate and distribute capital — offering loans against the promise of future repayment (home loans, education) — and Bitcoin does not do this; "just holding your money in bitcoin is just holding your money under your bed" (Japan's near-zero-rate mattress money as the analogy).
- **Banking on Bitcoin is possible, as a layer on top of cash.** Credit services must be built over the cash layer; they do not involve holding money at home.
- **Self-custody is a real, ongoing security cost.** "This myth that holding your own keys makes you secure is a fallacy" — large holdings demand multiple layers of security: backups, secure off-line storage and multiple keys.
- **No device is trustworthy by default.** "To be safe, you cannot trust a hardware wallet. All devices have a level of insecurity and risk."
- **Users are the weakest security link.** From three decades in information security: "the biggest security problem is users not understanding security. Handing off security to users is a recipe for failure."
- **Promoting self-custody as universally safe invites negligence liability.** Teaching that owning keys makes you ultimately secure is negligent; promoters of that claim may face tortious-negligence suits for false information leading to loss.
- **Layered wallet architecture for individuals.** Run multiple wallets and carry only the minimal daily amount on the phone; keep one device at home with a special backup key and locked encryption.
- **Segregation of duties for merchants.** A store should maintain a head-office wallet "that is paid to with no private keys on site"; workers are paid to a separate wallet while carrying only a small-balance one.
- **Bitcoin serves the unbanked as cash, not as a bank replacement.** Simple wallet applications let low-end users access and transact quickly, partially providing leverage into the market without substituting for credit.
- **Bitcoin does not replace banking. It is cash.** The categories — cash, custody, credit — must be kept distinct; conflating them produces both bad security practice and bad monetary analysis.
