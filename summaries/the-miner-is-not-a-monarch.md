# The Miner Is Not a Monarch
**Date:** 2026-05-20 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-miner-is-not-a-monarch
**Subtitle:** NAR, DAR, Property Rights, and the Legal-Economic Structure of Bitcoin

## Core thesis
Objections to Network Access Rules (NAR) and Digital Asset Recovery (DAR) in Bitcoin SV rest on a category error: the assumption that miners exercise sovereign governance over property rights. Miners are commercial entities inside legal jurisdictions, and compliance with valid court orders over identifiable property is mandatory legal obligation, not discretionary governance. Far from betraying Bitcoin, legally enforceable recovery is a prerequisite for institutional capital, commercial scalability and lawful market order.

## Key arguments and claims
- "Miner governance" is a political mythology layered onto a timestamp server: transaction validation no more confers political authority than operating a stock exchange makes its operator Parliament.
- The DAR debate collapses into jurisprudence's oldest distinction — possession versus title: Roman *possessio* versus *dominium*, common-law custody versus title, equity's legal versus beneficial ownership. A thief holding stolen jewellery possesses it without acquiring title.
- "A private key is evidence of control. It is not metaphysical proof of indefeasible ownership." English courts already treat crypto-assets as property: *AA v Persons Unknown* [2019] EWHC 3556 (Comm) recognised Bitcoin as property; *Ion Science Ltd v Persons Unknown* [2020] followed.
- Miners are businesses — consuming electricity, leasing premises, buying ASICs, filing taxes, using banks — and therefore legal persons subject to legal obligations. Every miner exists somewhere.
- Institutional economics: North on secure property rights as the foundation of development, Coase on legal frameworks reducing transaction costs, Alchian on ownership emerging because entitlement uncertainty destroys exchange. "No serious institution allocates substantial capital into a system where stolen assets are irrecoverable by definition"; such a system "would resemble less a financial network than a casino designed by nihilists".
- DAR is commercial necessity: without recovery, losses from insider fraud, social engineering, state theft or trustee misappropriation become permanently irreversible "because a cryptographic key was misused" — "technological fetishism masquerading as legal theory".
- Protocol versus jurisdiction: a protocol can determine whether a signature is mathematically valid; it cannot determine whether the signature was produced through extortion, fraud, theft, insanity, duress or breach of fiduciary duty. "Code is procedure. Law concerns rights." The claim "code is law" was "always philosophically primitive".
- "Voluntary consensus" concerns block validity and network coordination, not sovereignty over property. If a court determines a UTXO belongs to Party A, the miner's role is operational compliance; refusal "does not create freedom. It creates liability." Miners' capital intensity (energy contracts, banking, insurance, financing) gives strong economic incentives to comply regardless of ideology.
- De Soto's dead capital: assets that cannot be recovered, traced, inherited or adjudicated cannot integrate into mature commerce; lending, custody, trusts, insurance, probate and corporate treasury all require enforceable ownership.
- Recovery doctrine is centuries old: constructive trusts, tracing, proprietary and Mareva injunctions, Norwich Pharmacal and Anton Piller orders, equitable accounting. The UK Jurisdiction Taskforce's Legal Statement recognised crypto-assets as property; Singaporean courts have issued proprietary injunctions. DAR is "a technological extension of longstanding principles".
- Irreversibility is a trade-off, not a virtue: it reduces settlement risk while raising theft, operational, key-management and insider risk. Anti-recovery ideology perversely increases centralisation, since ordinary users cannot safely manage irreversible bearer instruments; legal recoverability can enhance distributed participation.
- Bitcoin was infrastructural — it solved double-spending through timestamped proof-of-work ordering — and Satoshi described a payment system, not a political revolution. Railways, telecommunications and the internet all integrated with law; "DAR is part of that integration process. It does not weaken Bitcoin. It normalises Bitcoin."
- Anti-DAR politics is "selectively theatrical": mining companies rely on law for contracts, premises, patents and banking while denying legal remedy to theft victims — "technologically updated feudalism: possession becomes ownership because power determines outcome".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal legal reasoning fused with institutional economics. He separates domains (protocol validity versus legal entitlement), appeals to the historical continuity of property and equity doctrine across technological media, and grounds the economic case in North, Coase, Alchian and de Soto: markets run on enforceable expectations, and confidence is itself an economic asset.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- "Code is law" is rejected outright as philosophically primitive; consensus algorithms "do not determine equitable ownership".
- The BTC-culture transformation of Bitcoin into anti-state ideology is described as a later corruption that produced "endless conceptual confusion".
- Absolute irreversibility — a sacred value in mainstream crypto — is reframed as an institutional-scale liability that drives users into custodial centralisation.
- Miner or node "governance" as a check on external authority is denied: miners comply with courts as banks, brokers and registrars do, and their legitimacy depends on it.
