# Rent seeking in economics and Crypto
**Date:** 2018-11-07 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/rent-seeking-in-economics-and-crypto-344e3d54bd81
**Subtitle:** Keynesian-ism and neo-Keynesian thought seems to be popular despite the fact it has never worked and is based on fallacies such as the…

## Core thesis
Keynesian economics survives not on merit but because economists are rational rent-seekers: government funding rewards research that supports big-government policy and buries dissent. Craig then applies the same public-choice lens to Bitcoin, claiming that social-media-driven developers with no "skin in the game" tell audiences profitable lies — a dynamic he says proof-of-work will destroy.

## Key arguments and claims
- Keynesian and neo-Keynesian thought "has never worked" and rests on fallacies: "the savings myth (assuming that savings is bad and lowers productivity)" and short-termism; yet figures like Paul Krugman keep promoting it.
- Proposed explanation: rent-seeking. Economists are "rational actors (more so than many other parts of the economy)" choosing the easy path — "big government pays for reports that support the views of big government" via endowments, research grants and other funding, while contrary reports are "relegated into obscurity together with the author".
- Case study: President Carter's energy report. Respected geologists found US oil sufficient "to at least 2,300" and "at least 4,000 years supply of natural gas"; Carter rejected it and recommissioned the work from "government officials with little past experience" who forecast only six to eight years of supply — enabling him to convince a Democratic Congress to create the Department of Energy.
- Vindication claim: 35 years on, economically recoverable oil and gas reserves are larger than in the 1970s despite far greater consumption — so the first, rejected report was right, yet "the report that mattered was the one that stated the fallacy the government wanted".
- Conclusion on economists: "rent-seeking by economists is logical and rational. Unfortunately, it is not a good outcome."
- Application to Bitcoin: "We now see rent-seeking in Bitcoin. The developers using Proof of Social Media (PoSM) and the others with an interest but little if any 'skin in the game' seek money, and to get this they tell others the lies they wish to hear."
- Resolution: "Luckily, Bitcoin is PoW, and PoSM and the fatal flaws it brings will end."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Public-choice economics applied reflexively to the economics profession itself: incentives, not truth, explain which ideas get funded and remembered. He uses a single historical case (Carter/DoE) as an existence proof of commissioned-fallacy dynamics, then argues by analogy that Bitcoin's discourse has the same structure, with PoW cast as the incorruptible incentive mechanism that social consensus lacks.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts developer-led/"rough consensus" governance orthodoxy: social-media consensus is reframed as rent-seeking by parties without skin in the game, against the era's Core-dev epistemic authority.
- Contradicts the Keynesian monetary mainstream that treated Bitcoin's fixed supply as a flaw: savings are productive, and demand-management economics is the fallacy.
- Elevates PoW hashpower as the only honest signal — a direct shot at the "users/nodes define the rules" doctrine, published the week of the BCH hash war where this was his central operational claim.

## Connections
The "skin in the game" framing echoes Taleb; the PoW-vs-social-consensus argument is the ideological core of his hash-war-era writing and connects to essays like "Bitcoin is all about incentives" and the "Myths of permission-less" line of thought. The Carter/DoE anecdote is one of his recurring anti-Keynesian set pieces.
