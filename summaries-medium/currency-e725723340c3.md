# Currency
**Date:** 2018-12-19 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/currency-e725723340c3
**Subtitle:** Coins remain legal tender without having an economic basis in specie. They hence, as with paper notes rest on the fiat declaration of a…

## Core thesis
Currency in the narrow sense is a creature of sovereign fiat — modern coins and notes are legal tender by declaration, not specie value — while bitcoin stands as commodity-based private money alongside coined gold and silver. Payment in currency is final on delivery: under the negotiability rule of *Miller v Race* the recipient takes good title without worrying about the payer's credit or the money's provenance. Applied to Bitcoin, this means a valid 0-conf transaction is already final payment, a double-spend attempt is fraud, and — the essay's sting — miners who process a thief's transaction after being notified of the theft "could now be held liable".

## Key arguments and claims
- Fiat by declaration: coins and notes "rest on the fiat declaration of a sovereign"; gold and silver are no longer legal tender but "do remain as private money". Bitcoin's place: "bitcoin, as a commodity money, forms a private money in the nature of other private specie-based monies including coined gold and silver. So, it may be held that bitcoin is not currency."
- The state monopoly on legal tender is political, not economic: making paper notes "the only category of current money… is more an issue of public policy than of use, and can be seen as a result of a political desire to control the money supply".
- Finality of currency payment: under the laws of negotiability (*Miller v Race*), "at the instant of delivery, the underlying obligation or debt is satisfied in full" — "a receiver of currency need not be concerned about the credit-worthiness of the other party", unlike a cheque, where delivery is "only the initial step" and B remains exposed to A's credit until cleared.
- 0-conf finality in Bitcoin: "we may have an instant transfer in the event a transaction is valid and sent without prior double spends"; once the recipient validates the ledger, "the only outcome is that the recipient of the currency has received a legal exchange, or there is a conspiracy to defraud them in the nature of a counterfeit note". Even before confirmation, the receiver "may be assured of good payment that has finalised" if the transaction meets miners' acceptance conditions.
- Double-spend as fraud: "an attempt to double spend is an attempt to defraud"; the analogue to payment channels and offline receivers is the cheque scenario — trust pending broadcast.
- Chattel rule for tokens: under *Brown, The Law of Personal Property* (§9.3), a transferee of chattel property gets only the transferor's title, so "The 'owner' of stolen tokens, contracts, and other commodity use cases as property on Bitcoin can thus seek to recover from adverse sale and theft" — his example: a tokenised game "magic sword" with "artefact" properties, where "The game company/operator would be obliged to allow the owner to recover the digital goods".
- Currency rule for merchants: "a merchant that receives a bitcoin as currency in payment for goods or services will not need to be concerned with the source of the monies, as long as the transaction is completed for consideration and without knowledge of the theft".
- The traceability conundrum: "Bitcoin, by its nature, is simple to map to the last known address", so a widely broadcast theft report could put miners and merchants on notice — but proximity decays fast (six blocks ≈ one hour), and "the currency principle would make the subsequent transfers good" once coins move to innocent hands.
- Miner liability scenario: if victim A proves the theft and notifies "the super majority of the mining network" before B moves the coins, "The miners could now be held liable, if it is found that they accepted and processed the transaction from B to a later party." He concedes no such notification infrastructure exists yet, but "it would not be infeasible in the future for a technology that provides such a service to be developed".
- Case support: *Chapman v. Cole*, 78 Mass (12 Gray) 141 (1858) — a minted gold token was treated under chattel rules, not money rules: "It was insufficient that the token was of gold." Form and use, not material, decide the regime.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Wright builds the argument as a common-law lawyer: define the category (legal tender as fiat declaration), state the governing rule (negotiability from *Miller v Race*), then apply the fact pattern by analogy (cheque clearing versus cash delivery; *Chapman v. Cole*'s gold token). The signature move is the dual-regime analysis — the same bitcoin is money or chattel depending on the manner of transfer — followed by a constructive-knowledge argument that converts Bitcoin's transparency into legal notice, thereby manufacturing miner liability out of the ledger's traceability.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the six-confirmation settlement orthodoxy: a valid, non-double-spent transaction is final at broadcast — "Before a single block has confirmed the transaction, a receiver of money as bitcoin may be assured of good payment that has finalised".
- Contradicts "Bitcoin is outside state money law": bitcoin is analysed entirely inside the common law of currency, negotiability and legal tender — as private money in the lineage of Bank of England notes and gold coin.
- Contradicts miner-neutrality / "miners just process valid transactions": miners with knowledge of a theft who process the thief's transaction "could now be held liable" — consensus validity does not confer legal immunity.
- Contradicts "not your keys, not your coins" absolutism: theft victims retain legal title and recovery rights against tokens (chattels), and even against coins where notice precedes transfer; possession is not nine-tenths of this law.
- Contradicts the sound-money critique of fiat as illegitimate: fiat's legal-tender monopoly is described as ordinary public policy — "a political desire to control the money supply" — not as fraud, and bitcoin competes as private money within that order rather than overthrowing it.
