# Who Actually Decides — core principles

- **Rule changes are produced by coalitions, not communities.** The rule-changing coalition is the set of parties whose coordinated action is required to move a rule from proposed to effective. Everyone outside it is observing the change, not producing it.
- **The necessity test names the coalition.** If these parties refused to act, would the change occur? If yes, they are not in the coalition; if no, they are. The set is typically small relative to the participant population.
- **Coalition components are identifiable.** Reference-implementation maintainers (merge accounts, release tagging); sponsors and funders; foundations and standards bodies; adoption-critical infrastructure (exchanges, staking providers, pools); listing authorities; wallet and indexer software; narrative and classification authorities (framing a change as "bug fix" versus "hard fork" is itself a governance act).
- **The coalition acts when its own payoff is positive.** \(\Pi_C(r) = B_C(r) + W(r) - K(r) - R(r) - I(r) - F(r)\). Participant welfare \(W\) enters only via pass-through to coalition returns. Aggregate welfare is not a reliable predictor of which changes occur.
- **Capital-at-risk disciplines only in-system positions.** Developers paid in fiat by external sponsors do not internalise confidence damage.
- **Activation design allocates authority.** Miner/validator signalling concentrates when capacity is concentrated; user-activated soft forks relocate authority to adoption-critical infrastructure; hard-coded heights shift authority to maintainers; token-weighted votes concentrate in the largest holders and their custodians; emergency procedures reduce coordination cost by design.
- **Persistence accumulates authority.** Lower marginal cost of later changes is the precondition for capture — an institution acquiring its own interests. Countermeasures: raise accountability, rotation, transparency, structural separation of authority.
- **Honest reporting names the actors.** Identify the coalition behind each historical change, the current coalition for a change of typical scope, its incentive structure, the proposals blocked and by whom, and coalition stability over time. Official process describes how the coalition coordinated; it is not a welfare assessment.
