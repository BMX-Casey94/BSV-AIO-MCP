# Crypto flim-flam
**Date:** 2019-05-13 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/crypto-flim-flam-6b4ff367b634
**Subtitle:** Unfortunately, every new technology opens up opportunities for the flim-flam man and night singer. It is no different with my technology…

## Core thesis
The crypto boom is a recycled securities fraud: the "democratisation of finance" pitch, the bucket-shop exchanges, and the pumped tokens of 2019 are the same flim-flam seen in the South Sea bubble of 1720, the 1880s–90s mail-order securities boom, 1929, and the 1990s internet bubble. ICO tokens and "decentralised exchanges" like Binance's are centralised, law-bound humbugs, and after an inevitable crash only one protocol — Bitcoin, valued by use rather than exchange price — will survive.

## Key arguments and claims
- What is sold as "Bitcoin" today is "a cheap copy"; new tokens are "fraudulently purported to have some long-term value", exactly as pump-and-dump share frauds did in 1720.
- Binance's token claims not to be a security while simultaneously promising price appreciation; the promised decentralised exchange "is a pure fraud" because "there is no such thing as a group-owned decentralised exchange" — every ICO token "is run by a group... controlled by individuals".
- "Decentralisation means the decentralisation of power"; Binance controls its coin and its exchange and "is subject to law" — specifically US law, because ".com domains have been considered to come under US territory for decades now. It is well tested in law."
- Prediction: within months, AML laws will cover all exchanges "including the mythical DEX"; exchanges must be able to report suspicious activity going back in time "if they are to trade in January 2020 at all", and operating past December 2020 without a complete AML/KYC programme "will be acting criminally". Binance, he says, hasn't started and has no intention to.
- "Democratisation of finance" is "the consistent cry of security scammers everywhere": the promise that households can get rich "without having to do the research or effort" of institutional investors. He cites Thomas Frank's *One Market Under God* (2000) on 1920s/1990s "economic democracy" and Jefferys on post-1856/1862 British company promotion.
- Historical evidence: mail-order firm G. Smith Dalby-Welch listed "more than 510,000 individual investors" and its 1911 pamphlet *Finding the Buyer* promoted securities to the average man — "It was an age of bucket shops... an age that we are seeing once again".
- Against the Austrian-populist reading of crashes: "The banks are not the problem. they are but a symptom. The misallocation of funds... starts and derives from fraud and deceit."
- The 1929 shoeshine boy with stock tips is today's "telegram group or a chat group where people falsely pump digital assets that no one in the group understands" (quoting John Rothschild, 1996, on hot-dog vendors with stock tips).
- Exchange prices are not value: "The exchange is Main Street, not Wall Street... Value is what is offered to society at large. And markets come through use." In 20 years "we will look back following the massive crash that will come and see one surviving protocol".
- Personal note: "I am not on social media... I don't sell anything publicly. If you have a deal that seems too good to be true, it's unlikely to be true."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The method is historical analogy backed by academic citation: he lines up 1720, the 1860s–1910s British promotion boom, 1929, and the 1990s against 2019 and argues the vocabulary ("democratisation of finance") is the invariant marker of the scam. He layers on a jurisdictional legal argument (.com = US territory) and a value theory (use and final consumption, not exchange price, constitutes the market), closing with a personal-credibility disclaimer.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Denies the existence of decentralised exchanges outright — the DEX narrative of Binance and DeFi is "humbug", since every such system has controllers subject to law.
- Rejects exchange price as the measure of crypto value, inverting the era's market-cap orthodoxy: "they have used the language of 1984 to tell you that such exchange was the market".
- Recasts "democratisation of finance" — a celebrated crypto talking point in 2019 — as centuries-old fraud vocabulary.
- Predicts blanket AML enforcement on all exchanges by end-2020, against the prevailing assumption that DEXs and offshore venues sit outside regulation.
- "Only one ledger will survive" — a maximalist single-protocol conclusion aimed at the whole altcoin/ICO market, including what BTC had become.
