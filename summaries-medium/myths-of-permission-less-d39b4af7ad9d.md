# Myths of permission-less
**Date:** 2018-10-28 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/myths-of-permission-less-d39b4af7ad9d
**Subtitle:** In the world of Bitcoin there are many false narratives and myths. This started with the myth of decentralisation. In this, the false idea…

## Core thesis
"Permission-less" is a collectivist myth that, under libertarian cover, attacks property rights — for "with property, there cannot be 'permission-less'". Craig argues Bitcoin was never about decentralisation or everyone validating; it is sound money settled by miners, and building on it is permitted only within the protocol's rules. The second half pivots to a legal analysis: ICOs and "utility tokens" are unregistered securities — specifically unregulated securitisations of future receivables — fully reachable by existing law.

## Key arguments and claims
- The "myth of decentralisation" — "the false idea that all users are required to run a node" — was promoted to mislead; "users do not need to, and should not have to validate anything. That is the entire point."
- Bitcoin "was never about decentralisation — it is sound money that is not easy to debase", and it is "a competitive system" in which self-validation on your own machine assures nothing.
- "Permission-less" is a "new collectivist lie" whose promoters "slyly propound to be libertarian" yet "seek to destroy property"; rights are bounded ("as long as it does not impact others"), and "you do not have a right to alter the code… it is miners who choose… it is miners who have the investment, not developers."
- The Internet itself is not permission-less: "as long as you follow the protocol, you may have permission. The same applies to Bitcoin. If you follow the rules of the network and do not see[k] to alter them, you have permission to build."
- ICOs contain "nothing decentralised nor new": a group raises capital, issues an offer, investors send funds — the token is merely a marker, and tokenised securities predate Bitcoin by decades ("EDIFACT and many other token systems date back decades"). "The difference is in the settlement, not the exchange."
- The ledger is the enforcer: "Bitcoin does not forget. The beauty of Bitcoin is that the ledger is permanent. You frauds do not vanish, and the regulators can come after you with evidence years after the fact." "Bitcoin has a long memory."
- ICO newsletter/exchange promotion is "securities scalping" — early buyers hyping tokens on social media; hidden conflict-of-interest disclosures are insufficient; he cites the SEC's 2004 action against Thom Calandra as directly applicable without any change in law.
- The "utility token" is a sham where intention shows capital-raising: "If you can [speculate on] the token, it is not a utility token." A genuine utility token is like a bus ticket — sold, used, never traded on a secondary market. "That is the purpose of Bitcoin, a utility token without the middlemen."
- ICOs are analysed as "future flow" securitisations without SPVs: "To date 98% of all utility-token ICOs have failed", and firms rarely isolate obligations in a special purpose vehicle, leaving investors exposed to insolvency and "pivots".
- Under English law the token sale works as an equitable assignment of future receivables (Law of Property Act 1925 s.136 being unavailable for want of notice), creating "a securitised (but unregulated) debt instrument over a future sale" and a consumer debt obligation under the Consumer Credit Act 1974 — with the distressed-bond consequence that a buyer at a loss may sue for face value, not the depreciated price paid.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Two registers welded together: a political-theory polemic (property rights as the boundary of permission; miners' investment as the source of governance legitimacy) and a black-letter legal exegesis (assignment of receivables, sham doctrine, CCA, LPA 1925, Dearle v Hall, Tailby v Official Receiver). His method is to deny novelty — show the "new" crypto phenomenon is an old, already-regulated legal form — so that existing enforcement applies unchanged. Rhetorically he is prosecutorial: named regulators (SEC, FCA), fraud vocabulary, and the permanent ledger as the state's future evidence locker.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Flatly inverts the "don't trust, verify" / every-user-a-full-node orthodoxy: users should not have to validate anything; that requirement is itself the myth.
- Rejects "permissionlessness" as Bitcoin's defining virtue — the word enshrined in mainstream BTC and Ethereum rhetoric alike — recasting it as a property-destroying, socialist frame.
- Rejects developer-led governance (Core's rough consensus): code change rights belong to miners by virtue of investment; developers may only propose.
- Against the 2017–18 ICO industry's core claim that utility tokens escape securities law: substance over form makes them securities/shams, and he names Ethereum and Wormhole as scams built on this lie.
- Against "code is law" and regulation-proof design: no alteration of existing law is needed to prosecute scalping and unregistered offerings — blockchain settlement is legally "irrelevant" to the offence.
