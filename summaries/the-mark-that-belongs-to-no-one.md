# The Mark That Belongs to No One
**Date:** 2026-03-05 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-mark-that-belongs-to-no-one
**Subtitle:** On the Quiet Catastrophe Hiding Inside Every Digital Signature — and the Three-Hundred-Year-Old Rule That Nobody Bothered to State Aloud

## Core thesis
A signature satisfies the signature requirement for enforcement only if it is attributable to an identifiable legal person. This is not a proposal but a description of what American private law has required since the Statute of Frauds 1677; the electronic-signature literature spent three decades analysing the *form* of marks while systematically neglecting the question on which enforceability actually depends — to whom the mark belongs. The essay expounds a recent doctrinal article that states this rule aloud and supplies the litigation-grade framework courts were already implicitly using.

## Key arguments and claims
- Five principal sources of US private-law signature doctrine converge on one requirement: the Statute of Frauds ("signed by the party to be charged"), the UCC ("executed or adopted by a person with present intention"), the Restatement (Second) of Contracts ("as that of the signer"), E-SIGN ("legally attributable to the person to be bound"), and UETA ("the act of the person"). Five vocabularies, one requirement: there must be an identifiable person.
- "Identifiable" is evidentiary, not facial: the signer need not be named on the document or known to the counterparty, but must be recoverable through evidence available to a court — including compulsory process — at the time enforcement is sought. The Schwartz/Solove continuum and Froomkin's traceable/untraceable distinction map onto this; only true non-identifiability defeats attribution.
- A three-step decision procedure describes what courts already do: Step 1 formal validity (maximally permissive — an "X", typed name, rubber stamp, fax header all suffice; failures are usually on the intent prong); Step 2 attribution (a property of the evidentiary record, not of the signing act); Step 3 consequences (if Step 2 fails there is no "party to be charged" and enforcement collapses).
- The Texas Supreme Court's 2021 decision in *Aerotek, Inc. v. Boyd* operationalises Step 2: once the proponent shows the system's security procedures (unique credentials, recorded act), a rebuttable inference arises and the burden of production shifts to the alleged signer; bare sworn denials — even word-for-word identical declarations from multiple declarants — do not create a fact issue.
- The *Cunningham*/*Khoury* split on automatic email signature blocks is entirely a Step 1 intent dispute; both sides assume Step 2 attribution, which strengthens the thesis. *St. John's Holdings* (text messages, Massachusetts Land Court) is a Step 2 failure at the agency link — the broker's signature was attributable to the broker, not the seller, exactly as UCC § 3-403's unauthorized-signature mechanism predicts.
- UCC § 3-403 treats an unauthorized signature as "ineffective except as the signature of the unauthorized signer": attribution is corrected, not eliminated; the negotiable-instrument system requires continuous attribution at every point in an instrument's life.
- Cryptographic sophistication is legally irrelevant to attribution: a 4096-bit RSA key with a self-signed certificate is "legally identical to an illiterate farmer's 'X' with no witnesses". Blockchain addresses are persistent pseudonyms, not anonymity; attribution succeeds or fails under four general evidentiary conditions (compellable domestic evidence; offshore evidence beyond process; overly inferential evidence; destroyed or expired evidence).
- Three recurring errors: treating pseudonymous signatures as unenforceable; conflating privacy-protective mechanisms (VPNs, encryption) with anonymity; and treating "anonymous" technologies as categorical legal black holes rather than conducting case-specific analysis.
- Five genuinely hard categories remain: shared credentials, compromised devices, corporate authority (two-link attribution), cross-border evidentiary failure, and heightened-formality carveouts.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal private-law analysis: close textual reading of converging statutes, synthesis of case law into a descriptive framework, and institutional reasoning about what an enforcement regime logically requires — a court can only impose obligations on persons it can identify, serve, and subject to judgment. The method treats attribution as an evidentiary property of litigation rather than a metaphysical property of identity.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects the mainstream-crypto refrain that blockchain transactions are "anonymous" and therefore sit outside legal enforceability: an address is "functionally identical to a common-law pseudonym", recoverable through chain analysis, exchange records, or compulsory process.
- Rejects technology-determinism in both directions — neither "blockchain transactions are unenforceable" nor "blockchain transactions are enforceable"; only case-specific attribution analysis is correct.
- Undermines the "code is law" intuition that cryptographic strength substitutes for legal identity: cryptography protects the integrity of the record, not the identity of its maker.
