# Bailment on a Ledger
**Date:** 2026-01-15 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/bailment-on-a-ledger
**Subtitle:** Possession, delivery, and digital tokens after the Property (Digital Assets etc) Act 2025

## Core thesis
The literature on ledger-recorded assets commits a category error by treating the private key as the asset; in truth the asset is the tokenised right constituted by the ledger state, the key is merely an instrument of control, and ledger recordation is the event of delivery. For the "third category" of digital assets confirmed by the Property (Digital Assets etc) Act 2025, exclusive practical control performs the juridical work of possession, so orthodox bailment doctrine applies without any innovation.

## Key arguments and claims
- The literature's error is twofold: confusing an instrument of control with the subject matter controlled, and misapplying "information is not property" authorities to assets whose legally relevant characteristics are exclusivity, rivalrous control and transferability.
- OBG Ltd v Allan establishes only that information as such is not property; it does not support the stronger claim that rights constituted, transferred and extinguished through an informational system are incapable of being possessed or held in custody.
- Your Response Ltd v Datateam Business Media Ltd is a boundary case, not an abolition: it denied a possessory lien over a database because data is non-rivalrous (control of one copy does not exclude others); it does not bar possession where the facts supply exclusive dominion over a recognised asset.
- Bailment has never depended on physicality: since Coggs v Bernard it concerns delivery, control and the allocation of responsibility following the assumption of custody. The inquiry is functional — does the mechanism transfer exclusive control sufficient to ground possessory consequences?
- The Property (Digital Assets etc) Act 2025 confirms certain digital assets are objects of personal property rights though neither things in possession nor things in action; Parliament deliberately declined to codify the incidents of this third category, leaving them to the courts.
- Recordation is delivery: a token transfer is a state change, not a copy. Whether output-based or account-based is irrelevant; the system enforces a single valid state, and recordation extinguishes the transferor's dispositive power while conferring exclusive dominion on the recipient. Rivalrous exclusivity is enforced operationally.
- Key-copying is not delivery: copying a credential creates concurrent capability — the opposite of delivery, which excludes the transferor. Sharing a key allocates a power over another's property, engaging fiduciary, trust or agency analysis, but not bailment.
- Mistaken transfer is the cleanest illustration that bailment arises without title passing: the recipient acquires possession but not title; subsequent unauthorised dealing is conversion under Fouldes v Willoughby — strict liability attaches to dealing as though entitled, not to mere receipt.
- Possession-based remedies transfer directly: The Winkfield (a bailee in possession may sue a wrongdoer for full value, holding proceeds on account); The Aliakmon (possessory rights and responsibilities exist independently of ownership); Morris v C W Martin & Sons Ltd (a professional bailee is liable for an employee's loss — responsibility follows assumption of control, not proprietary interest); Parker v British Airways Board and Costello v Chief Constable of Derbyshire (possession as an inference from control and context, enforceable against all but a superior claim).
- Custodial versus non-custodial is resolved without slogans: a provider is a bailee only where it has assumed exclusive practical dominion excluding the client's unilateral control. Blocked or non-recorded transfers do not crystallise bailment; "was it recorded?" is an externally verifiable evidentiary boundary.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal common-law reasoning by category correction: identify the right object (the tokenised right), identify the event that transfers exclusive dominion (recordation), then apply the existing law of delivery and possession. The method is functional rather than metaphysical — control does the work, labels do not — and the explicit payoff is continuity rather than innovation.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Rejects the "keys are the asset" rhetoric embedded in mainstream custody discourse ("not your keys, not your coins"): a key is not the asset and key-copying is not delivery.
- Rejects technological exceptionalism — the claim that digital assets leave the common law helpless until new doctrine is invented: "The common law is not helpless. It is being misdirected."
- Replaces the metaphysical framing dominant in crypto-legal scholarship ("is a digital asset really a thing?") with an operational test of exclusive, rivalrous control.
- Dissolves the custodial/non-custodial slogan debate into a factual inquiry: who can actually cause a valid disposition, and who can actually prevent it?

## Notable quotes
- "A key, password, or credential enables interaction with an asset; it does not constitute the asset."
- "Recordation is delivery."
- "The common law is not helpless. It is being misdirected."
- "Bailment fails only where delivery fails; and delivery fails only where exclusive control has not moved."
