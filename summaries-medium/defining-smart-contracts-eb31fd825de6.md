# Defining smart contracts
**Date:** 2018-09-28 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/defining-smart-contracts-eb31fd825de6
**Subtitle:** When contrasting contractual principles, it is clear that where a contract is not required to be in writing (Columbia Law Review, Apr…

## Core thesis
English law never required most contracts to be in writing, so electronic and Bitcoin-based contracts inherit full legal standing; the only live questions are evidential. Under the Electronic Communications Act 2000 an electronic contract is equivalent to writing and electronic signatures are admissible, which means "code is not law" — courts retain power to overturn or adjudicate any digital contract — but Bitcoin's digital-signature structure gives its contracts "high evidentiary value" admissible in a UK court. The essay closes by claiming these contracting capabilities "have been lost in BTC leaving BCH as not only digital cash, but Bitcoin".

## Key arguments and claims
- Writing was never the general requirement: where a contract need not be in writing (citing Columbia Law Review 1929 and 1907, McKendrick 2005), "little additional uncertainty could be created where the contract is completed electronically".
- "Electronic evidence must hold greater weight than verbal evidence" (Lord Justice Auld, Sept 2001); the weight of a given piece turns on "the strength of a digital signature algorithm and the security surrounding the mechanisms used to sign".
- An electronic contract has "a twofold structure": a sequence of numbers and code on a medium, and the perceptible rendering on an output device (Bainbridge 2000; Reed 2004; Brownsword 2000) — a dichotomy that pre-ECA "exasperated" the writing question.
- The ECA (Electronic Communications Act 2000) resolved it: "the electronic form of a contract is to be accepted as equivalent to a contract in writing".
- Contract formation still rests on the classic elements — "offer, acceptance and consideration when coupled with an intention to create legally binding relations" — with writing required only for exceptions such as real-property transfers.
- Equivalence to writing activates the parole evidence rule: the ECA "has in effect, forbid the introduction of extrinsic evidence which could change the terms of the electronic contract", though partial integration still admits extrinsic evidence (Treitel 2003; Durtschi 2002; Lim 2002).
- ECA s7(1) makes an electronic signature and its certification admissible "in relation to any question as to the authenticity of the communication or data or as to the integrity of the communication or data" — though the Act's signature provisions fell short of the EC Directive's objectives after objections such as London Borough of Newham's Smart Card Project submission.
- The legal bottom line: "while code is not law and a court retains the ability to overturn or adjudicate any contract, even those made digitally, system such as Bitcoin contracts can be easily show to have high evidentiary value and be admissible into a UK court".
- Bitcoin's signature mechanism supports real contracting: "The means of which Bitcoin used to incorporate digital signatures allows for complex structured contracts to be negotiated and saved by business."
- The split-era claim: "Unfortunately, many of these benefits have been lost in BTC leaving BCH as not only digital cash, but Bitcoin."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal statutory interpretation: the essay moves from first principles (formation elements) through the writing question to evidentiary weight, anchoring each step in named authority — statutes quoted by section, law-review articles, and standard texts (Treitel, McKendrick, Lim). The argumentative payoff is a distinction rather than a celebration: code is demoted to evidence ("code is not law"), and Bitcoin's value is recast as evidentiary robustness within courts, not escape from them. A single closing sentence converts the legal analysis into a BCH-versus-BTC technical claim.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Directly negates the "code is law" maxim: courts "retain the ability to overturn or adjudicate any contract, even those made digitally" — smart contracts are evidence before a court, not sovereign execution.
- Values Bitcoin Script's signature structures as contracting infrastructure for business, against the BTC-era deprecation of Script functionality (disabled opcodes, small OP_RETURN limits).
- Asserts BTC "lost" these contracting benefits while BCH retains them — a legal-technical argument for BCH's claim to the Bitcoin name, written weeks before the November 2018 BCH/BSV split.
- Treats legal admissibility and evidentiary weight as the metrics that matter for smart contracts, rather than trustlessness or censorship resistance.
