# The tax implications of bitcoin as money
**Date:** 2018-10-14 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/the-tax-implications-of-bitcoin-as-money-2572cf2573fc
**Subtitle:** As Bitcoin does not need to be held or processed by a financial institution[1], many people have concluded that they can bypass tax…

## Core thesis
Craig opens the tax chapter of his legal series by targeting a popular misconception: because Bitcoin needs no financial institution to hold or process it, "many people have concluded that they can bypass tax obligations" — a conclusion his analysis is built to dismantle. The post scopes how bitcoin must be brought inside income tax, capital gains tax and foreign-exchange rules, with treatment determined by the specific use (mining, holding, spending, token use, smart contracts, exchange). The Medium body is a scoping excerpt of a longer legal analysis.

## Key arguments and claims
- The bypass fallacy: disintermediation from banks leads many to think tax obligations can be evaded with bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies — cited against Omri Marian's "Are Cryptocurrencies Super Tax Havens?" (Michigan Law Review, 2013), which Craig's framing implicitly answers in the negative.
- "There are various areas of taxation law that need to be addressed. This includes incorporation of bitcoin into Income tax, Capital gains tax and under foreign exchange rules."
- Use-dependence principle: "The treatment bitcoin will depend in part on its use" — tax character follows the transaction, not the asset label (echoing the polymorphism thesis of the previous post).
- The investigation list: mining bitcoin; holding bitcoin; using bitcoin — "as currency for the purchase of goods or services", "as a token representing the sale of other assets", and "as a smart contract"; and buying, selling and otherwise exchanging bitcoin.
- Smart-contract tax uncertainty is grounded in contract doctrine: citing Gamage & Kedem (2006), "contractual formation is inherently uncertain in and of itself", and electronic-contracts legislation (the ECA) "cannot in itself remove contractual uncertainty" — so smart-contract tax treatment inherits that uncertainty.
- Evidentiary anchor: the US GAO's May 2013 report to the Senate Finance Committee, quoting its statistics — ~11 million bitcoin in circulation as of 1 May 2013; prices of $5–$20 (May 2012–Feb 2013) rising to "$79 and over $237" (April–May 2013); roughly "8,000 to 70,000 transactions per day" — to show tax authorities were already tracking the system.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Issue-spotting legal method: name the misconception, enumerate the statutory heads of tax (income, CGT, forex), then decompose "use" into a taxonomy of fact patterns each requiring its own treatment. Authority is drawn from tax scholarship (Grinberg 2012; Marian 2013), government reports (GAO-13-516) and contract-law literature, in the same footnoted thesis style as the rest of the October 2018 legal cluster.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Frontally contradicts the era's "Bitcoin is a tax haven / offshore without banks" folk belief — the cypherpunk assumption that removing financial intermediaries removes the taxman's grip. Craig treats tax liability as untouched by disintermediation.
- Contradicts the "one asset, one rule" simplification (e.g. the IRS-style single property classification): his use-dependent scheme implies different tax outcomes for the same coin depending on whether it is mined, held, spent or embedded in a contract.
- Contradicts smart-contract exceptionalism ("code is law, outside contract law"): by routing smart contracts through ordinary contractual-uncertainty doctrine, he denies they escape existing legal categories.
