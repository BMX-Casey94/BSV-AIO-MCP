# How Digital Signatures Work
**Date:** 2020-01-21 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/how-digital-signatures-work-efd303fa8f11
**Subtitle:** Way back in 2006, I wrote a legal paper as part of my work with Northumbria University and my postgraduate law studies on the topic of…

## Core thesis
Drawing on a 2006 legal paper from his Northumbria postgraduate law studies, Craig explains the UK/EU statutory framework for electronic and digital signatures — the Electronic Communications Act 2000, the Electronic Signatures Regulations 2002, and EU Directive 1999/93/EC — in order to demolish the crypto-community claim that control of a Bitcoin key is legally dispositive. His target is the Silk Road "Dread Pirate Roberts defence" (and Andreas Antonopoulos's support for it): "There is no such thing as handing keys over and saying they're someone else's, which is not how digital signatures work." The Medium post is an excerpt; the body continues on craigwright.net.

## Key arguments and claims
- The Electronic Signatures Regulations 2002 (passed 8 March 2002) implemented the EU Directive on Electronic Signatures into UK law, removing uncertainty left by the Electronic Communications Act 2000 and introducing "advanced electronic signatures".
- Statutory definitions: a basic electronic signature is "data in electronic form which are attached to or logically associated with other electronic data and which serve as a method of authentication".
- An advanced electronic signature must be "uniquely linked to the signatory; capable of identifying the signatory; created using means that the signatory can maintain under his sole control; and linked to the data to which it relates in such a manner that any subsequent change of that data is detectable".
- Advanced signatures envision "a personal digital certificate provided by a certificate service provider (CSP)", giving "certainty and non-repudiation" — identity anchoring is a feature of the legal framework, not an optional extra.
- "A digitised electronic signature is not the same as a digital certificate": a digital signature is "the unique numerical value based on the entire written document that is being signed", verifiable "with an extremely low probability of error"; an electronic signature can be as simple as a printed name or scanned autograph.
- Legal effect: courts treat qualifying electronic signatures as equivalent to manuscript signatures — they "satisfy the legal requirements of a signature" and "are admissible as evidence in legal proceedings" (mirroring Art. 5(1) of the Directive); the Land Registration Act 2002 patched formal requirements for property transactions.
- Open doctrinal question: whether a personal signature is "by nature of form or of function", citing Denning LJ in Goodman v. J Eban Ltd.
- Electronic contracting: the Internet is "primarily communication channels", fundamentally like older carriers — "a variety of different communication protocols could use a single carrier line such as a voice phone line" — so contracting law maps onto it without mystique.
- Politically, he frames the essay as pulling apart "the lie that Bitcoin is designed to promote anarchy and crime", accusing Antonopoulos of attempting "to mislead courts into believing the so-called Dread Pirate Roberts defence".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal legal analysis transplanted onto crypto: he works from primary legislation (ECA, ESR, the EU Directive) and case law (Goodman v. J Eban Ltd), carefully distinguishing categories the community conflates — electronic vs digital signatures, signature vs certificate, possession of a key vs legal attribution of an act. The authority move is biographical as much as argumentative: the 2006 paper pre-dates Bitcoin, presenting him as a lawyer-technologist whose credentials precede the system he claims to have built.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts "possession of keys = ownership/identity" folk-cryptography: key control is evidence, not identity, and signature law already supplies attribution and non-repudiation mechanisms that survive someone else using your keys.
- Contradicts the Silk Road-era defence narrative (associated in his telling with Antonopoulos) that keys could simply have "changed hands" — legally incoherent, he argues.
- Contradicts the "crypto is outside law because it's just math" stance: a mature statutory regime (ECA 2000, ESR 2002, Directive 1999/93/EC) already governs electronic signatures and admits them as evidence.
- Contradicts the anti-PKI/CA purism of the cypherpunk tradition: he presents certificate service providers and accredited CAs as the legally meaningful backbone of trustworthy digital signatures.
