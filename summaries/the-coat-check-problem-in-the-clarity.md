# The Coat Check Problem in the CLARITY Act
**Date:** 2026-01-20 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-coat-check-problem-in-the-clarity
**Subtitle:** Why “control, entitlement, and property” must be explained like a ticket and a coat before any capability-based classification can work

## Core thesis
The CLARITY Act can classify assets and allocate jurisdiction yet still fail its promise, because the central retail harm is not taxonomic confusion but the mistaking of an intermediary's ledger entry (the ticket) for the asset itself (the coat). Unless the statute welds "customer property" language to custody mechanics — segregation, enforceable withdrawal, reuse constraints and insolvency priority — it will produce clarity about labels while preserving opacity where it matters: the point at which customers discover, too late, that they were holding tickets.

## Key arguments and claims
- The triad — Control, Entitlement, Property — separates three questions routinely collapsed into the flattering word "balance": who can act on the asset (move, freeze, lend, delay, refuse); what the customer can demand and on what conditions; and what survives the intermediary's failure against the world.
- The coat check makes the separation unavoidable: the attendant has control (physical custody, decides return); the customer has entitlement (the ticket, a right to demand return only through the attendant's process); property is the decisive question — does the coat remain the customer's, or has it become part of the general inventory so that, on failure, the customer is "just another claimant holding a scrap of paper"?
- Digital-asset venues replicate the coat check at scale and conceal it behind design: they take custody, issue internal ledger entries displayed as balances, and mediate use through withdrawal windows, verification checks, risk controls, discretionary freezes, fee schedules and "temporary pauses".
- Three ordinary errors: treating a ledger entry as the asset (it is an assertion until matched to custody, segregation and enforceable rights that survive stress); trusting the user interface as a title deed (courts enforce relationships, not feelings); and mistaking a right to request withdrawal for ownership, control, segregation or priority.
- Capability is not custody; classification is not segregation; a definition does not prevent commingling; a label does not decide priority in insolvency. The Act may be "perfectly clear about what the asset is, yet remain unclear about what the customer has" — and in an intermediated market the second question decides outcomes.
- "Customer property" is meaningful only if welded to four custody facts: (1) segregation as individually identifiable assets, not mere "separate accounting" — "a spreadsheet comfort" that is no barrier against commingling, double use or estate capture; (2) enforceable withdrawal with narrow, objective limits — a right pausable whenever liquidity is tight "is not a right; it is a marketing sentence"; (3) reuse constraints forcing the business to choose between safekeeping custody with strict limits and an explicitly regulated credit product — the middle position, "calling it custody while using it like funding", is the precise abuse to be prevented; (4) insolvency priority keeping customer assets out of the intermediary's general estate, with return in kind where possible.
- Better drafting means mechanical, unmisreadable protections: custody-layer segregation that is auditable and estate-resistant; continuous, enforceable auditability (record-keeping standards, reconciliation, retention, penalties outweighing the temptation to blur lines) rather than periodic attestations or marketing dashboards; presumed prompt withdrawal with delays only under objectively testable circumstances, time limits, notice requirements and escalating obligations; and explicit insolvency treatment that a bankruptcy court cannot ignore.
- The statute should classify relationships, not merely assets, and impose custody mechanics that make misdescription expensive.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Pedagogical reduction to an ordinary-life analogy — coat check, ticket, fire at the counter — used as a discipline rather than an ornament, then translated into market-structure and drafting terms. The method is stress-testing: judge every rule by what happens "when the counter breaks", privileging insolvency-facing substance over calm-conditions appearance.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Diverges from the mainstream-crypto regulatory project of capability- or category-based classification: statutory boxes and agency lanes do not change who holds the keys.
- Rejects the industry's balance-as-ownership interface convention: the UI "collapses everything into one comforting impression: 'you have X'".
- Rejects "customer property" rhetoric unaccompanied by mechanics, and the custody-marketing/credit-reality hybrid perpetuated by venue terms of service.
- Implicitly declines the self-custody absolutist answer: the analysis starts from the fact that retail customers "rarely hold control" and use venues, so the law must regulate the coat check as it actually exists.

## Notable quotes
- "The balance is not the coat. It is the ticket."
- Control answers "who can act." Entitlement answers "what can be demanded." Property answers "who owns, even when everything breaks."
- "A right that can be paused whenever liquidity is tight is not a right; it is a marketing sentence."
- "Otherwise it will remain clear only about labels while leaving the market free to keep doing what it has always done: hand out tickets, call them coats, and let the customer discover the difference only when there is smoke in the room."
