# Stewardship in the Smallest Coin: Wesleyan Capitalism and the Moral Economy of Micropayments
**Date:** 2025-08-10 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/stewardship-in-the-smallest-coin
**Subtitle:** How John Wesley’s principles can revive capitalism’s moral compass and dismantle corporatist monopolies, one cent at a time.

## Core thesis
John Wesley's triad — gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can — is capitalism's moral spine: wealth is a trust, and profit is the residue of service to others. Micropayments operationalise that stewardship for the digital age, letting millions of individuals reward merit directly, bypass corporatist gatekeepers, and break the monopoly of institutional "charitable capital".

## Key arguments and claims
- Wesley's imperatives form a moral architecture, not platitudes: gain is bound to honesty, fairness and service; save is anti-waste reinvestment; give is voluntary and conscience-directed — "Coerced giving erodes both the virtue of the giver and the efficiency of the gift."
- Profit in a genuinely competitive market is "the market's verdict" that resources were stewarded well; stewardship makes the use of profit itself a moral responsibility.
- Corporatism is the fusion of business power with political favour — rent-seeking, monopoly protections, subsidies, weaponised licensing — until "profit is no longer the residue of value created; it is the dividend of political leverage."
- Micropayments democratise economic agency: a penny can reach a farmer in Kenya, a coder in Manila or a musician in Warsaw with no bank manager's permission and no processor devouring half its value.
- Specificity sharpens the signal: "this article, not the whole newspaper; this video explanation, not the entire course" — payments become precise, voluntary, instantaneous market signals rather than proxy rewards filtered through platform algorithms.
- Spending regains moral weight: every act of appreciation costs something, which disciplines attention and encourages discernment; attention is redeemed directly by the one who earned it instead of being seized and sold to advertisers.
- Institutional philanthropy is concentrated and politicised, its recipients shaped by "compliance checklists and PR optics"; micropayments enable decentralised stewardship — distributed giving is "a river fed by countless small streams", resilient where single-source patronage is fragile.
- Corporatism's moral danger: lock-in, bundling and ad-surveillance smother awareness of where money goes, replacing active stewardship with passive consumption and breeding the disengaged populace corporatism needs to survive.
- Cultural reformation: as the printing press broke church and crown control of knowledge, micropayments collapse the institutional bottleneck between producer and consumer — an "economic liturgy" aligning daily acts with enduring values.
- Obstacles are technical (fixed fees make a $0.05 payment irrational), regulatory (uniform AML/KYC; the fix is proportionate, tiered oversight with de minimis thresholds) and cultural (the "free" norm really means "paid for with your data and your attention"); overcoming them is framed as a moral obligation, stewardship at a civilisational level.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Moral theology fused with market economics. Wesley's ethic supplies the normative frame; standard microeconomics (profit as residual value, rent-seeking, gatekeeping) supplies the analysis; the printing-press and Reformation analogy supplies the historical warrant. The method treats mechanism design as a branch of ethics: the tools of exchange must serve people rather than bind them to institutions.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
No direct engagement — this post is a moral-economic case for micropayments and against corporatist intermediation, not an argument about BTC protocol politics.
