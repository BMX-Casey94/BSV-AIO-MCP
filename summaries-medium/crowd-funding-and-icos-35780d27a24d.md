# Crowd Funding and ICOs
**Date:** 2018-12-24 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/crowd-funding-and-icos-35780d27a24d
**Subtitle:** Things to consider: rules for registered funding portals, non-resident funding portal requirements, completion of offers, cancellations of…

## Core thesis
A notes-style checklist rather than a developed essay: Craig catalogues the regulatory dimensions of crowdfunding and ICOs, opening with the flat assertion that ICOs are "(all are securities)." The piece surveys the six crowdfunding models and flags that each carries characteristic abuses with problematic enforcement, pointing to FTC action as the available remedy.

## Key arguments and claims
- Framing claim: "The following are some notes on ICOs (all are securities)" — token offerings are to be analysed inside the securities/funding-portal perimeter, not outside it.
- Compliance checklist: know "the rules for registered funding portals, completion of offers, cancellations of offers, and withdrawals, the requirements for non-resident funding portals, and the miscellaneous provisions attached to funding portals."
- Securities issued carry "restrictions on resells and disqualification provisions."
- Rewards-based model: perks (T-shirts, movie passes, software) issued to non-accredited investors without ownership — "look at limitations and possible fraud"; Kickstarter named as the platform type to address.
- Equity-based model: "a common-share-based raising," with Kickstarter and angel funds given as examples; ICOs raise additional issues on top.
- Charity-based model: donation-based crowdfunding for charitable causes.
- Debt model: peer-to-peer crowdfunded debt — "ICOs etc." — usable "in situations where mortgage companies are unavailable."
- Litigation funding: crowdfunding sources that finance commencing and continuing litigation; LexShares named as the example to examine.
- Product pre-order model: a pre-sales variant of the rewards model where investors receive products as manufactured, ahead of the public and possibly at a discount — raising capital-raising issues.
- Enforcement reality: "There are abuses in each of the models, and enforcement can be problematic."
- Named case: *Federal Trade Commission v Eric Chevallier* — a permanent injunction sought under the FTC Act's prohibition on "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in affecting commerce," cited as the enforcement instrument for crowdfunding fraud.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Taxonomic note-taking rather than sustained argument: he enumerates the crowdfunding models as a classification scheme, attaches the relevant regulatory hook to each, and asserts the securities-law conclusion up front. The method is that of a compliance memo — the persuasive work is done by the parenthetical "all are securities" and by placing ICOs inside an existing statutory checklist.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the 2017–18 ICO-industry orthodoxy that tokens are "utility" instruments outside securities law: Craig's "(all are securities)" admits no exceptions and routes ICOs through funding-portal rules, resale restrictions and disqualification provisions.
- Contradicts the "regulation can't touch decentralised fundraising" narrative by naming a concrete enforcement lever — FTC Act §5 unfair-or-deceptive-practices actions — already used against crowdfunding operators (*FTC v Chevallier*).
- Note on form: the body is a bullet-point checklist of things to "look at," not a fully argued essay; the summary above reflects what the notes contain without inventing connective argument.
