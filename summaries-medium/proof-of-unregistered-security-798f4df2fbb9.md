# Proof of (unregistered) security
**Date:** 2019-03-04 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/proof-of-unregistered-security-798f4df2fbb9
**Subtitle:** There have been many people pushing proof of stake as an alternative to proof of work. They claim that proof of stake was somehow more…

## Core thesis
Proof of stake is an unregistered security under US law and a centralisation machine. Applying Section 2(a)(1) of the 1933 Act and the Howey/Forman line of cases to Ethereum's planned move to PoS (Casper), Craig argues that staking rewards are dividends to preferential shareholders, that fewer than 2% of holders could ever be validators, and that the result is "a sham consensus layer" hiding a manipulated, tightly controlled security — one more centralised than Apple or J.P. Morgan stock.

## Key arguments and claims
- Section 2(a)(1) of the 1933 Act (15 USC 77b) defines "security" broadly enough "to cover proof of stake in all forms"; *SEC v. W.J. Howey Co.* (1946) and *United Housing Found. v Forman* (1975) extend it via the investment-contract test: money invested "in a common enterprise and... led to expect profit solely from the effort of others."
- A token on a blockchain is legally nothing new: securities may be certificated, dematerialised or book-entry, so blockchain issuance is just a device "to promote the issue of securities without registration or adherence to the law."
- Ethereum concentration data: two pools control 53% of the network; the touted validator requirement is 1,500 ETH (over USD187,500 at USD125/ETH); average holdings are ~2.17 ETH per address (1.87 excluding the top 50) — so "under 2% of the total network" can validate, and "2% of the network are able to control the other 98%."
- Sybil attack is unstoppable: a cartel holding 51% of tokens can run "over a set of 5,000 virtual machines" voting as if they were many individuals, with "no way to stop such an attack or attribute it" — "a simple means of market manipulation and control."
- Staking rewards are economically dividends: "validators are effectively being rewarded dividends for holding and not spending tokens," making validators preferential shareholders while ordinary holders get nothing — and the promotional claim that merely holding contributes to the network confirms the Howey expectation of profit from others' efforts.
- Casper's deposit-destruction (slashing) "lead[s] to numerous breaches of US security law" and creates an attack surface: an attacker can strategically target validators who disagree on the future of the coin.
- Exchanges cannot safely stake customer ETH: custody risk plus "existing bailment laws would preclude such a thing," shrinking the validator set further — "less than 1% of the existing network would be operating as validation nodes."
- Decentralisation comparison: "Apple stock is more decentralised than the so-called decentralised cryptocurrency," and by common mathematical measures "J.P. Morgan would be considered around 500 times more decentralised than the Ethereum network."
- PoS is purpose-built for fraud: it incentivises insiders and development teams to manipulate price while "hiding such an ability from regulators" — "designed to facilitate bucket-shop trading as it manipulates fools and misleads regulators."
- Conclusion: "PoS ETH is clearly a security." Ethereum may be near a commodity now, but under proof of stake it fulfils every prong of Howey — "Yet, it is not registered…"

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Statutory and case-law application to protocol mechanics: he quotes the 1933 Act definition verbatim, runs the Howey test prong by prong, and uses Forman's buyer-motivation distinction to classify stakers as investors rather than users. This legal frame is backed by quantitative concentration analysis (pool shares, ETH-per-address distribution, validator capital thresholds) and game-theoretic attack scenarios (Sybil validator farms, targeted slashing). The rhetorical mode is reclassification — renaming PoS "proof of equity by a preferential voting mechanism."

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Directly refutes the Ethereum/PoS orthodoxy that stake-based consensus is more decentralised, more secure and more egalitarian than mining — Craig quantifies it as rule by the top 1–2%.
- Contradicts the industry-wide "utility token, not a security" line of the ICO era: PoS yields are dividends, and the whole structure is an unregistered equity offering.
- Undercuts the "no 51% problem in PoS" marketing: capital lockup plus Sybil-able validators makes majority cartels cheaper and unattributable.
- Anticipates and rejects the regulatory-ambiguity defence: the 1933 Act's definition was always wide enough; technology does not repeal securities law.
