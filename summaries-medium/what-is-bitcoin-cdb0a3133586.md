# What is Bitcoin

**Subtitle:** In any examination and analysis the contractual, regulatory and financial obligations of corporate providers, government regulators and…

## Core thesis
Bitcoin is a financial instrument whose precise legal character shifts with how the transaction is constructed — "a contractual exchange, electronic cash, a promissory note or another financial security, a bill of sale, or a simple ledger entry" — and it must be analysed inside existing law, not outside it. Drawing on English and Australian case law on the nature of money, Craig argues Bitcoin is already money and ownable intangible property, that formal recognition as currency by a government is "inevitable", and that the underdeveloped, jurisdictionally inconsistent state of digital-currency law is itself a cause of Bitcoin's limited use. The post reads as an excerpt from his law thesis and announces a series ("This shall continue tomorrow"), closing with the flat claim: "Bitcoin is cash."

## Key arguments and claims
- Legal character follows transaction construction: depending on structure, a Bitcoin transaction can be "a contractual exchange, electronic cash, a promissory note or another financial security, a bill of sale, or a simple ledger entry" — with regulatory consequences "from the creation from the Coinbase allocation of rewards to the payment and consideration for an exchange".
- The common-law definition of money is functional, not state-based: quoting Travelex v Commissioner of Taxation (via Butterworth's, Miller v Race 1758, Moss v Hancock 1899), money is what "passes freely from hand to hand throughout the community in final discharge of debts… being accepted equally without reference to the character or credit of the person who offers it" — "It is common consent and conduct that gives a thing the character of money."
- Property status is clear even where currency status is not: "Being money, Bitcoin is an intangible property that is capable of being owned. What is less clear is if Bitcoin is 'currency'."
- State recognition is coming: German acceptance of Bitcoin as a unit of account and the postulate before the Swiss Parliament to treat Bitcoin as foreign currency mean "it seems inevitable that Bitcoin will be formally accepted as currency by a government in the near future".
- Currency has a precise legal meaning beyond money: per Emmett J in Travelex, currency concerns the negotiability of money — title passes by mere delivery, exempt from nemo dat quod non habet, so the recipient need not "enquire into the title of the payer" (citing David Fox, Property Rights in Money).
- The field is legally underdeveloped and inconsistent: Bitcoin "has been seen as a virtual token, currency or even a security at separate times and places within individual jurisdictions. This uncertainty leads in part to the limitations of its use."
- US securities law already treats Bitcoin as money: in SEC v Shavers (E.D. Tex.), the defendant's argument that his Bitcoin Ponzi scheme involved no securities "as Bitcoin was not money" failed — US jurisprudence places Bitcoin "within the ordinary meaning of 'money' and perhaps 'currency'".
- The research agenda (framed as thesis questions): when is Bitcoin money; the tax implications; what securities and debt instruments can legally be created within Bitcoin; the limits of anonymity, privacy and non-disclosure rights; and Bitcoin's impact on monetary policy and the available judicial response — across UK, Europe, China and the USA.
- The one certainty amid the analysis: "That said, one thing we do know, Bitcoin is cash."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal legal method: define the subject through case law and statute, quote the operative judicial passages at length (Travelex, Miller v Race, Moss v Hancock), and let classification do the work — money, currency, property, security each have tests, and Bitcoin is run through them jurisdiction by jurisdiction. The mode is comparative and thesis-like (footnotes, FinCEN rulings, GAO reports, CNBC), explicitly framing open questions as a research programme rather than settled polemic. Yet the legal formalism serves a fixed conclusion: whatever the category puzzle, "Bitcoin is cash" — the law is a venue for confirming, not questioning, that identity.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against the cypherpunk/"code is law" self-image of 2018 crypto: Bitcoin is analysed as a creature of courts, regulators and statute — FinCEN, the SEC, the CFTC and tax authorities are constitutive of what Bitcoin is, not external threats to it.
- Against "Bitcoin is outside the state" rhetoric: state recognition as currency is welcomed and predicted as "inevitable", and legal uncertainty — not government hostility — is blamed for limiting adoption.
- Against the "digital gold, not money" drift of BTC in the high-fee era: the entire apparatus of money law (medium of exchange, final discharge of debts, negotiability) is invoked to assert "Bitcoin is cash" — a direct BCH-side claim in the pre-split weeks.
- Against regulatory-uncertainty as an excuse for lawlessness: the response to gaps and disparities is a comparative-law research agenda, implying Bitcoin businesses should expect and plan for securities, tax and monetary law to apply.
