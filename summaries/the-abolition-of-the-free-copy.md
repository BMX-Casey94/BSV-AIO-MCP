# The Abolition of the Free Copy
**Date:** 2026-06-02 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-abolition-of-the-free-copy
**Subtitle:** On true digital scarcity, the trusted enclave, and property whose laws are chosen by the one who makes it — beginning with the oldest law of all: that to give a thing is to no longer have it

## Core thesis
The governing fact of the digital age — that a copy costs nothing — quietly abolished the possibility of owning digital goods in the strong sense: nothing made of bits could be owned, given, or reliably paid for. The double-spend solution (a public ledger of title) joined to the trusted execution environment (a hardware enclave that holds a key against its own owner's will and proves remotely what it is doing) makes a file "costly and accountable to copy" — which is all scarcity has ever meant, even for gold. The deeper consequence: a maker may now compose the laws his property obeys — open, owned, or royalty-bearing — enforced by construction rather than by courts.

## Key arguments and claims
- Jefferson's 1813 letter to McPherson (the taper that lights another without darkening) states the non-rivalry of ideas; Samuelson's 1954 "Pure Theory of Public Expenditure" formalised it. For pure ideas this remains true; the digital file is a hybrid — "an abstraction wearing the costume of a thing" — that we expect to behave like property.
- Property descends from rivalry and excludability, both of which the free copy destroyed for bits — hollowing out owning (nothing scarce), giving (nothing parted with), and paying the maker (the work escapes his keeping on release).
- Money was the first domain where this became intolerable. Chaum's blind-signature ecash (1983) achieved scarcity only via a trusted mint; Nakamoto's public append-only ledger (2008) achieves uniqueness of title without a sovereign — but title is not possession of the bits: owning a token that points at a film is not controlling the film.
- The missing piece is "a place where a key can be held and used without its holder being able to extract it": the trusted execution environment or secure enclave (Intel SGX: McKeen et al. 2013; Anati et al. 2013; Costan & Devadas 2016). Sealing binds data to the same enclave on the same machine; remote attestation proves to a distant party exactly what code is running on genuine hardware.
- Honest limits: Foreshadow (Van Bulck et al., USENIX Security 2018) extracted SGX's keys via transient out-of-order execution, and any copy made before the lock is applied is beyond recall. The defensible claim is never "uncopyable" but "expensive and accountable to copy" — the only scarcity gold has ever had.
- Szabo's self-executing mechanisms (1997) joined to Lessig's "code is law" (1999) yield property that chooses its own laws: the maker composes the bundle of rights — wholly open (now a choice, not a fate), wholly owned (ERC-721 deeds joined to enclave-scarce content), or conditional (a resale royalty enforced without statute; use-but-not-modify; lend-but-not-duplicate).
- Demsetz (1967): property rights emerge when technology makes them economical to define and enforce. Engineered scarcity makes digital rights economical again — and therefore real.
- The third party, banished as a necessity, returns as an option: the maker may abolish the enforcing authority, keep it, or vest it in a chosen quorum — generalising the earlier essay's abolition of the dealer into a deliberate choice.
- Restorations: true digital gifts (to give is to be without), a used-goods market and the first-sale doctrine, the artist's automatic resale royalty, and the original/reproduction distinction. The acknowledged shadow: the same mechanism enables enclosure — architecture can silently foreclose quotation, lending, resale and fair use (Lessig). Because scarcity is now chosen, the choosing must be argued in the open.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Institutional economics (Samuelson on public goods, Demsetz on the emergence of property rights) joined to a layer-by-layer account of the cryptographic and hardware stack (ledger, encryption, enclave, attestation). He reasons by historical analogy — gold's "merely expensive to counterfeit" scarcity — and by trust-surface candour: every claim is bounded to what the mechanism delivers, with the trusted parties named.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
No direct engagement — this post is a constructive account of engineered digital scarcity and programmable property, aimed at the wider "information wants to be free" culture. Its implicit rebuke is to mainstream NFT practice: ERC-721-style tokens are a "crude grammar" for deeds that become more than pointers only when joined to an enclave that makes the underlying content genuinely scarce.

## Notable quotes
- "A thing you can give without losing was never yours."
- "Gold is not uncopyable; it is merely expensive to counterfeit and difficult to forge undetected, and on that 'merely' the whole institution of money rested for millennia."
- "The honest claim is therefore never that a file has been made uncopyable, which is a word for marketing and not for mathematics."
- "To give is to be without. That is what giving means."
