## Core thesis
Despite its title, the body is a full doctrinal maritime-law paper: it analyses how Letters of Indemnity (LOIs) substitute for Bills of Lading in international trade, and argues that the common practice of exchanging LOIs for clean or antedated Bills of Lading is fraudulent, corrosive of documentary trust, and should be condemned outright by courts and industry. The implied Bitcoin connection (developed only via the title) is that any Bitcoin-based contracting or trade-documentation system inherits this entire body of negotiable-instrument law — title, receipt, estoppel, deceit — rather than escaping it. The conclusion rejects the notion of a "proper Letter of Indemnity" and demands shippers renegotiate with consignees instead of recruiting carriers into deception.

## Key arguments and claims
- The Bill of Lading is unique because its negotiability fulfils three functions (per Bowen LJ in *Sanders v Maclean* (1883)): use as a receipt, evidence of the contract of carriage, and documentation of title in the goods — "the key to the floating warehouse". LOIs arose because trade fails when the original bill is unavailable at discharge.
- A Letter of Indemnity is "a written duty of a shipper to indemnify a carrier for the liability that the carrier could lay itself open to through the issue of a clean Bill of Lading when the cargo received was not in fact that which was stated on the bill of lading"; LOIs at discharge are also called letters of guarantee.
- Bills of lading generally incorporate the Hague or Hague-Visby Rules, which impose seaworthiness and care duties, a package limitation, and a one-year time bar; crucially, the Rules set a floor — the shipper "cannot decrease the obligations" and any clause attempting to do so is void.
- Courts condemn the LOI-for-clean-bill practice: "Antedated and false bills of lading are a cancer in the international trade… Honest commerce requires that those who put the bills of lading into circulation do so only where the bill of lading, as far as they know, represents the true facts" (*Standard Chartered Bank v Pakistan National Shipping*).
- Clean-bill LOIs and antedated-bill LOIs must be distinguished: antedating inserts a false shipment date (to satisfy a letter of credit, subsidy or quota window) without misdescribing the goods; both are fraudulent, but Hague-Visby and Hamburg Rules give estoppel protection to innocent third parties only against falsely clean bills — "Antedated bills of lading have few legislative protections".
- Against third parties acting in good faith, an LOI "have no effect"; via estoppel it cannot be used to show goods were damaged before loading; LOIs have repeatedly been held "illegal… and thus unenforceable against anyone" (*Brown, Jenkinson v Percy Dalton*).
- Between carrier and shipper the LOI is "a prima facie binding contract", but because both are "jointly parties to the same deceit", many jurisdictions refuse enforcement — leaving carriers exposed despite holding the indemnity.
- P&I Clubs (fourteen mutual, non-profit shipowner insurers, mostly in England, Scandinavia and the Caribbean) exclude all liability from ante- or post-dating bills: "All liabilities resulting from ante-dating or post-dating of a Bill of Lading are excluded from P & I Club cover", and delivery without a bill is not a reciprocal risk — an LOI "effectively replaces any P&I cover".
- Documentary credits (UCP 500/600, Art. 32) require "clean" transport documents, which creates the pressure to issue false clean bills; the ICC lists four LOI fraud types: non-existent consignments, inferior quality/quantity, double-selling one consignment, and double bills of lading.
- Liability for the practice rests in the tort of deceit (*Derry v Peek*): a false representation made "knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly"; Evans LJ held a shipowner has "no defence" where a false antedated bill was issued even in a "genuine though careless belief" that it facilitated trade.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Pure black-letter legal doctrinal method: statutory instruments (Hague/Hague-Visby/Hamburg Rules, UCP 500/600, COGSA, Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1992), a dense case list (English, Australian, US, Singapore courts), and leading commentary (Tetley, Hazelwood, Goode, Ibbetson) are marshalled to classify instruments, distinguish them (LOI at shipment vs letter of guarantee at discharge), and evaluate enforceability across parties. The rhetorical mode is law-review exegesis with a normative conclusion — condemnation of a tolerated trade custom — rather than technical or economic argument.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against the "code is law" / "trustless paperless trade" orthodoxy, the paper's premise (per its title) is that Bitcoin contracts operate inside centuries of negotiable-instrument and documentary-credit law: title, receipt, estoppel and deceit doctrine do not evaporate on a ledger.
- Against the claim that immutability solves documentary fraud: the entire analysis shows fraud here is not alteration of records but false statements entered into records (clean bills for damaged goods, antedated dates) — a "cancer" that no recording technology removes.
- Against disintermediation rhetoric: the system described runs on trusted, regulated intermediaries — banks issuing documentary credits, P&I Clubs mutually insuring, carriers as bailees — and the failure modes Craig highlights are precisely failures of those intermediaries' honesty, which law (not protocol) polices.

## Notable quotes
- "Antedated and false bills of lading are a cancer in the international trade." (quoting *Standard Chartered Bank v Pakistan National Shipping*)
- "A Bill of Lading is a commercial document of dignity and integrity based on good faith."
- "Engaging the carrier to aid in the commission of a deception on an innocent third party is deplorable."
- "Even when a Letter of Indemnity is issued in seemingly good faith, it can cause confusion and hardship." (quoting Prof. Tetley)
- "The practice of issuing clean bills of lading when goods are damaged is very reprehensible. It leads to trouble, and the people who do it ought to suffer." (quoting Wright J in *United Baltic Corp.*)
