# The Mark That Belongs to No One — core principles

- **A signature may be anything, but it must be someone's.** Enforceability requires attribution to an identifiable legal person — a description of what private law has required since the Statute of Frauds 1677, not a proposal. Remove the someone, and the mark is noise.
- **Five legal vocabularies converge on one requirement.** The Statute of Frauds ("signed by the party to be charged"), the UCC ("executed or adopted by a person with present intention"), the Restatement (Second) of Contracts ("as that of the signer"), E-SIGN ("legally attributable to the person to be bound") and UETA ("the act of the person") all demand an identifiable person; the electronic-signature statutes did not create the requirement, they made it visible.
- **"Identifiable" is evidentiary, not facial.** The signer need not be named on the document or known to the counterparty, but must be recoverable through evidence available to a court — including compulsory process — at the time enforcement is sought. Only true non-identifiability defeats attribution.
- **Courts follow a three-step decision procedure.** Step 1 formal validity is maximally permissive (an "X", typed name, rubber stamp or fax header all suffice; failures are usually on intent); Step 2 attribution is a property of the evidentiary record, not of the signing act; Step 3 consequences — if attribution fails there is no "party to be charged" and enforcement collapses.
- **System evidence shifts the burden.** *Aerotek, Inc. v. Boyd* (Texas Supreme Court, 2021) operationalises Step 2: once the proponent shows the system's security procedures (unique credentials, recorded act), a rebuttable inference arises and the burden of production shifts to the alleged signer; bare sworn denials — even identical declarations from multiple declarants — do not create a fact issue.
- **Agency is a separate attribution link.** The *Cunningham*/*Khoury* split on automatic email signature blocks is entirely a Step-1 intent dispute; *St. John's Holdings* (text messages) is a Step-2 failure at the agency link — the broker's signature was attributable to the broker, not the seller, exactly as UCC § 3-403 predicts.
- **Attribution is continuous across an instrument's life.** UCC § 3-403 treats an unauthorised signature as "ineffective except as the signature of the unauthorized signer" — attribution is corrected, not eliminated — and the negotiable-instrument system requires attribution at every point.
- **Cryptographic strength is legally irrelevant to attribution.** A 4096-bit RSA key with a self-signed certificate is legally identical to an illiterate farmer's "X" with no witnesses; cryptography protects the integrity of the record, not the identity of its maker. Blockchain addresses are persistent pseudonyms — functionally common-law pseudonyms recoverable through chain analysis, exchange records or compulsory process.
- **Attribution succeeds or fails under four evidentiary conditions.** Compellable domestic evidence; offshore evidence beyond process; overly inferential evidence; and destroyed or expired evidence. Analyse case by case rather than declaring technologies categorical legal black holes.
- **Avoid three recurring errors.** Treating pseudonymous signatures as unenforceable; conflating privacy-protective mechanisms (VPNs, encryption) with anonymity; and abandoning analysis in the face of "anonymous" technology. The genuinely hard categories are shared credentials, compromised devices, corporate authority (two-link attribution), cross-border evidentiary failure and heightened-formality carveouts.
