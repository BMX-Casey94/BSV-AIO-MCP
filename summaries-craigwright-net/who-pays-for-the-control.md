# Who pays for the control?
**Date:** 2026-10-20 | **URL:** https://craigwright.net/who-pays-for-the-control | **Source:** craigwright.net research article

## Core thesis
The article argues that a security improvement can be worth making for everyone affected and still look uneconomic to the person asked to pay. Following the costs and the avoided losses across that boundary changes the decision being evaluated. The research question it states is how to connect the capacity to prevent a loss with the incentive to bear the cost of prevention.

## Key arguments and claims
- Hypothetical software service and its customers. Invented figures, same time horizon, no other effects: an additional control costs the provider 120 units a year; it reduces the provider’s expected annual loss by 40 units and customers’ expected annual losses by 100. On the provider’s own account, the control adds 80 units to expected cost. Across the provider and customers together, it saves 20. The article says the technical action is identical in both calculations; what changes is whose consequences enter the decision. The first useful question is who can implement the control, who pays for it and who benefits if it works. A single total conceals those three roles.
- Craig S. Wright and Tanveer A. Zia (2011), *Rationally Opting for the Insecure Alternative: Negative Externalities and the Selection of Security Controls*, in *Computational Intelligence in Security for Information Systems*, LNCS 6694, pp. 206–213, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-21323-6_26. The article says the account of the conference chapter is limited to its public publisher record and abstract. That abstract, as the article reads it, frames protection in terms of costs and avoided losses, and asks how to assess costly controls in systems and software. This commentary is dated 20 October 2026.
- A separate public GIAC paper under sole authorship, the same title, which the body dates by a September 2011 acceptance date and the sources box as accepted 5 September 2011, Section 2. The article links to https://www.giac.org/paper/gslc/2178/rationally-opting-insecure-alternative-negative-externalities-selection-security-controls/107335. It says the GIAC text develops the allocation question through producer and consumer costs and distinguishes individual incentives from an outcome that is best for the parties collectively. The two publication records should remain distinct; the GIAC text is not evidence that the conference chapter contains every detail of that longer treatment.
- Ross Anderson (2001), *Why Information Security is Hard — An Economic Perspective*, introduction; the article links to https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/ftp/users/rja14/econ.pdf. The article says Anderson explains how security failures can arise when the party able to protect a system does not bear the resulting harm, and that the historical discussion of distributed denial-of-service attacks illustrates how a compromised machine can impose losses elsewhere, so that better protection of that machine benefits parties outside its owner’s immediate calculation. The article says this is an economic argument about incentives: it does not provide a present-day estimate for the hypothetical service, and the historical examples are not current legal or configuration advice.
- Suppose the customers jointly contribute 90 units towards the control. The provider now pays 30 net and avoids 40 in expected losses, a gain of 10. The customers pay 90 and avoid 100, also a gain of 10. The article says the contribution reallocates the cost and does not create another 90 units of social benefit. Counting the payment as a benefit on one side without recording it as a cost on the other would manufacture an improvement. Collecting contributions may be costly; customers may disagree about their shares; some may be difficult to identify or unable to verify that the control was installed. If arranging and checking the agreement consumes more than the 20-unit surplus, this particular solution no longer passes the same aggregate test. The article says the arithmetic does not establish a legal duty, a fair allocation or a feasible contract.
- A practical evaluation the article says could compare the existing arrangement with a clearly specified alternative. Record the cost of implementation and maintenance, the parties exposed to loss, and the evidence used to estimate how the control changes that exposure. Expected losses should use a consistent period, with uncertainty retained. The assessment would also need to test behaviour: a reimbursement promise could alter a customer’s precautions; a provider paid for installing a control might maintain it differently from a provider paid for a verified service outcome. The article says any such response changes the comparison and would need measurement, and that none is assumed to have occurred in this example. Distinguishing resource costs from payments between the parties makes the result auditable, as does retaining costs borne by people who did not sign the agreement. The sources box says no new experiment, measured commercial saving, operational deployment or current legal conclusion is reported.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The article sets one control against two boundaries: the provider’s account, where it adds expected cost, and the joint account, where it saves expected loss. It keeps a 2011 conference chapter and a separately authored GIAC paper of the same title distinct, uses Anderson (2001) for the incentive problem illustrated by historical denial-of-service cases, and then shows a customer contribution as a transfer that must be entered on both sides. Transaction costs of arranging the contribution can exhaust the 20-unit surplus. The evaluation section asks for consistent periods, retained uncertainty and measured behavioural responses, and the article assumes none of those responses in the example itself.
