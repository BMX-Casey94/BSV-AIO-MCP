# On Predicates
**Date:** 2018-12-21 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/on-predicates-b92df80f9b76
**Subtitle:** In the common law many individual fact-based decisions are set as predicates.

## Core thesis
Craig attacks the "code is law" / smart-contract-maximalist thesis by analysing the common law as a system of predicates — legal terms like *tort*, *contract*, *negligent*, *good faith* — whose application to disputed facts requires human judicial judgement. Because judges interpret semantically open-ended predicates, adapt doctrine evolutionarily, and answer to other courts and society, law cannot be reduced to deterministic code: "we cannot expect 'Code is law.' to hold true."

## Key arguments and claims
- Common-law predicates are "semantically unique": words such as *tort*, *contract* and *unconscionable* carry both everyday and specifically legal meanings, and terms like *negligent*, *good faith* and *intent* shift with context — so their range is limited but their application is judgement-laden.
- He distinguishes semantics (word meanings) from pragmatics (words in context and use, including "the scope of the speaker's purpose — social effect"), arguing legal meaning is pragmatic and therefore not mechanically computable.
- A case is modelled formally: each party presents "a set of sentences describing a set of facts," each sentence selecting a proposition describing possible worlds; the decided case is "a conjunction of all the propositions expressing the particular factual findings," and the judicial function is deciding whether that conjunction falls within a legal predicate.
- Facts are "often in dispute," and their resolution by judge or jury is itself part of the system — a layer smart contracts cannot replicate.
- Judges appear constrained by predicate systems *prima facie*, yet common-law judges "have the ability to use a variety of different means that are not open to civil-law judges," producing outcomes sometimes "seemingly inconsistent with other cases" and at odds with positive or economic-efficiency theories of adjudication.
- Authority of the predicate declaration: "if a judge says, 'This is a battery,' then unless a superior court reverses the judge… the decision stands," however "ignorant or perverse" it may seem; but decisions "need not be fixed in stone" and can be altered on appeal.
- On stare decisis: no statutory rule exists in Great Britain; the House of Lords Practice Statement 1966 is merely "a statement of an intent of a court," and even identical words diverge across cases — "printed" has meant books and journals in one case, microfilm in another.
- On "stare dictis": judges are human — "no judge likes to be overruled. Hence no judge invites reversal" — and common-law development depends on the interaction of judges' opinions rather than on statutory enactments.
- The law adapts "not in a revolutionary sweep but rather in an evolutionary manner," moulding itself "to social, political, moral, and economic requirements" — a flexibility that makes hard, fast automated predicates "easily… seen as unfair."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
He borrows the apparatus of formal semantics and predicate logic — propositions, possible worlds, conjunctions of facts — and maps it onto common-law adjudication to show the mapping fails at the decisive step: the classification of a fact-set under a legal predicate is a discretionary, socially embedded human act. The rhetorical mode is analytic-philosophical rather than polemical, building to the anti-"code is law" conclusion as a theorem about institutional design rather than a mere assertion.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Directly contradicts the Ethereum/Szabo orthodoxy that smart contracts can replace legal enforcement — "code is law" — by arguing that adjudication's predicate-application step is irreducibly human.
- Contradicts the crypto-anarchist claim that Bitcoin-style automation can route around courts: judicial bodies must retain the ability "to modify outcomes," so any system that hard-codes outcomes will collide with the legal system.
- Implicitly supports his broader position that Bitcoin's script system should implement contracts *within* law (specific performance, registries), not substitute for law — a stance at odds with both BTC's and Ethereum's governance-minimalist rhetoric of the era.
