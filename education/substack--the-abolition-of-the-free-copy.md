# The Abolition of the Free Copy — core principles

- **A thing you can give without losing was never yours.** The free copy abolished owning, giving and paying for digital goods. Rivalry and excludability, the parents of property, were destroyed for bits.
- **A public ledger of title plus a trusted enclave restores scarcity.** Nakamoto's append-only ledger achieves uniqueness of title without a sovereign. The enclave holds a key against its own owner's will and attests remotely what code is running. Together they make a file costly and accountable to copy — which is all scarcity gold has ever meant.
- **Title is not possession of the bits.** Owning a token that points at a film is not controlling the film. Tokens become more than pointers only when joined to an enclave that makes the underlying content scarce.
- **The honest claim is never "uncopyable".** Side-channel attacks have extracted enclave keys, and any copy made before the lock is applied is beyond recall. Gold is not uncopyable; it is expensive to counterfeit. On that "merely" the institution of money rested.
- **A maker may compose the laws his property obeys.** Wholly open, wholly owned, or conditional — a resale royalty, use-but-not-modify, lend-but-not-duplicate — enforced by construction rather than by courts. Property rights emerge when technology makes them economical to define and enforce.
- **The third party returns as an option.** The maker may abolish the enforcing authority, keep it, or vest it in a chosen quorum. Because scarcity is now chosen, the choosing must be argued in the open: the same mechanism can restore gifts and first-sale, or silently enclose quotation, lending and fair use.
