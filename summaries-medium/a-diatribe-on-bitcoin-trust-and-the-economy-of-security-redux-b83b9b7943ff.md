# A diatribe on Bitcoin, Trust and the economy of security (redux)
**Date:** 2018-09-07 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/a-diatribe-on-bitcoin-trust-and-the-economy-of-security-redux-b83b9b7943ff
**Subtitle:** It was April 27th, 2013 when I first published an earlier version of this, and it is timely to start bringing this to life again.

## Core thesis
Money emerges from the most marketable goods, not from government decree, and trust in a currency rests on its immunity to debasement. Bitcoin can be trusted precisely because "government cannot print more", unlike fiat, which Craig frames as a hidden mechanism of mass wealth extraction. He then extrapolates: automation and 3D printing will collapse manufacturing into pure intellectual-property commerce, which demands a borderless, non-state currency — and "it is BCH that remains true to this purpose". The essay closes as an explicit bet against state-controlled money.

## Key arguments and claims
- "The most marketable goods are what become the media of exchange." Barter at village scale quickly becomes collated obligations; divisible goods (grain, butter) acted as improvised currency, not direct chicken-for-cow swaps.
- Salt in Rome (root of "salary") was ideal early money — divisible, durable, quality verifiable by taste — but became too bulky and perishable ("A rainstorm could literally liquidate your holdings") as trade volumes grew.
- Gold succeeded because it is divisible, non-degrading, and above all "you cannot print gold" — inflation is "the hidden tax" that cannot be applied to it.
- He accuses President Obama of taking "an additional $1 trillion for his own spending experiments", driving the US dollar from above the Australian dollar to parity by 2012 — government "taxed them outside of Congress", "a theft on a massive scale" blamed on commerce.
- A post-gold-standard US dollar is "a promise of the US dollar" — hand one to the Treasury and you get a dollar back. "Basically, there is nothing to trust."
- Money "was created by merchants and commercial entities as a means of engaging in trade"; government monopolised it in "a hostile takeover". Trust in currency predates and does not require the state.
- Domestic analogy: Australia subsidises inefficient car workers; likewise in Bitcoin "Some want to scale and allow the world to have access to sound money. Others, want to be subsidised so they do not need to work and strive as hard" — a direct jab at the small-block camp.
- Automation grows exponentially while human skill grows at best linearly; 3D printing is where computing was in the 1970s–80s. Manufacturing, even agriculture, becomes a technology; "all goods will be digital" and wealth becomes intangible IP.
- IP commerce needs instant, cheap, borderless payment for digital rights (print-at-home Nikes, bespoke to your measurements); no state currency will be trusted for this. "Bitcoin is not the only solution but it is the leading one."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
A Mengerian origin-of-money account (marketability converging on a medium of exchange) built from historical anecdote — salt wages, gold — fused with Austrian-style inflation-as-theft polemic. He then pivots to techno-futurist extrapolation (exponential machines versus linear humans) to derive a functional requirement for borderless digital cash. The mode is self-declared diatribe: first-person, combative, framed as a public wager ("I've placed my bet") with his words left "as a marker and a lesson".

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against the post-2017 "digital gold / store of value first" orthodoxy: Bitcoin's purpose here is peer-to-peer electronic cash for borderless IP commerce, and the chain that serves it is BCH, not BTC.
- Against "trustlessness" rhetoric: Craig insists "The key to currency is trust" — trust grounded in non-debasement and merchant adoption, not in minimising trust via maximal node validation.
- Against small-block economics: miners who resist scaling are cast as subsidy-seekers akin to protected car workers, inverting the BTC narrative that big blocks serve corporate interests.
- Against state-money legitimacy generally: central banking is a "hostile takeover" of merchant-created money, a sharper anti-fiat stance than mainstream crypto's usual "opt out" framing.
