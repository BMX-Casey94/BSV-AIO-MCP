# Taxing Times…
**Date:** 2019-10-15 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/taxing-times-3cd2067fab8d
**Subtitle:** One of the key aspects of Bitcoin is that it is set in stone; the protocol does not change. Many have been misled to believe that Bitcoin…

## Core thesis
An excerpt — the body continues on craigwright.net. Bitcoin's protocol is "set in stone": nodes (miners) never vote on the protocol — the white paper's "vote" concerns only the ordering of transactions — and enforcing rules is not creating them. Craig then turns the IRS's own hard-fork FAQ into a weapon: by the IRS definition, BTC is "the protocol change resulting in a permanent diversion from the legacy distributed ledger" — an airdrop he dubs "CoreCoin" — while the unchanged original protocol remains Bitcoin.

## Key arguments and claims
- "Nodes do not vote to change the protocol. This is not written in the white paper at all." The misrepresented section concerns only "honest nodes voting on the ordering of transactions"; "there is no mechanism for users or the community to change the protocol. And such a mechanism was never designed or intended."
- "Enforcement is a police or regulatory function, and does not involve the creation of rules" — miners enforce, they do not legislate.
- Bitcoin is not one-person-one-vote: "As a system for democracy, Bitcoin is horrible. It is a system that allows control of the minority by the majority… Those who invest the most get to vote more." This is a feature for honest ordering: the higher investor "has more to lose, and law enables a large investor to lose large amounts of money if they act dishonestly".
- "Bitcoin is not a parliament. No blockchain-based proof-of-work system will be" — though the scripting engine can host voting systems built on top, where "the ordering of votes could be important".
- IRS FAQ Q21/A21 (cited verbatim): a hard fork is "a protocol change resulting in a permanent diversion from the legacy distributed ledger" that may create a new cryptocurrency; if you received no new coins, you have no taxable income.
- Application: "The concept of hard forks or even soft forks is not one that is part of Bitcoin. It has been added to the airdrop called BTC, or, CoreCoin."
- "The most important word in the sentence is legacy": "Bitcoin Core differs from my original vision of Bitcoin in every way" — it does not implement SPV, "has failed to comprehend what SPV is", and "is not peer-to-peer in any reasonable manner"; even BIP-70 (the payment protocol enabling P2P exchange) faces removal.
- "Core will tell you it doesn't matter what I said as Satoshi. I'm sorry, but it does."

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Legalistic definition-trapping: adopt the tax authority's own definition of a hard fork, then show BTC satisfies it while the original protocol does not — converting a technical governance dispute into a legal and tax characterisation with property consequences. This is paired with strict-constructionist reading of the white paper (what is and is not written) and the enforcer/creator distinction.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against node-democracy and UASF-style governance: no community protocol voting exists or was ever intended.
- Against "BTC is the legitimate continuation of Bitcoin": on the IRS's own terms, BTC is the divergent airdrop and the "legacy" ledger is the original protocol.
- Against "Bitcoin as democratic money" rhetoric: Bitcoin is explicitly wealth-weighted and anti-democratic, and rightly so for its purpose.
- Against BTC wallet architecture: rejection of SPV and the planned removal of BIP-70 sever BTC from peer-to-peer exchange.
