# Problems and key questions around Bitcoin
**Date:** 2018-10-13 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/problems-and-key-questions-around-bitcoin-76fc7282aae4
**Subtitle:** The first problem to be addressed concerns the nature of lawful money. In the United States, the Stamp Payments Act of 1862[1] was enacted…

## Core thesis
Craig sets out the legal research agenda of a series: Bitcoin's classification under existing law is unresolved because the system is "polymorphic" — usable as currency, security, token or commodity at any time — and the correct approach is a framework that classifies each particular transaction rather than the asset in the abstract. He further argues that Bitcoin does not sit outside law: old statutes like the Stamp Payments Act 1862 may reach electronic tokens, and cryptocurrencies "do not mitigate opportunities for fraud, but transform many old frauds" onto a global stage.

## Key arguments and claims
- Lawful money problem: the Stamp Payments Act of 1862 (18 U.S.C. §336) "was enacted to stop the circulation of private tokens that were in competition with federal postage stamps", and "the language of the statute may apply to electronic transactions", extending "note, check, memorandum, token" to digital money (citing Vartanian, Ledig & Demianczuk's 1996 analysis).
- No issuer, no obligation: "Even the notion of Bitcoin as an obligation comes into question as we need to define the concept of a third-party issuer. The peer-to-peer nature of the currency means that obligations are derived in a manner unlike that of other monetary sources."
- Classification is contested and unstable: "'Bitcoin is a form of currency'. The statement in itself is heavily contested" (citing both 19th-century currency cases and Dorfman's Forbes piece "Bitcoin is an asset, not a currency").
- The polymorphism thesis: "It is not that Bitcoin is currency, a security or a token, it is that the multifariousness of the system allows it to be used as any of these at any time" — like gold, which serves as exchange medium, electronics input and jewellery.
- The real difficulty "is not in determining whether bitcoin has value but rather implementing a framework that captures the particular transaction and allows this to be successfully classified".
- Announced research programme: securities-regulation challenges, whether traditional exchange consumer/investor protections (citing FinCEN money-transmitter guidance FIN-2008-G008) work for virtual currencies, and cross-jurisdictional enforcement (citing the ECB's 2012 Virtual Currency Schemes report).
- Fraud realism: "The use of bitcoin over the Internet increases opportunities for money-laundering and many fraudulent activities"; quoting Dr Russell Smith (Australian Institute of Criminology, 2000) that online scammers "close-down their operations quickly … use digital technologies to conceal their identities" leaving "little likelihood of success whether civil or criminal proceedings are taken".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Doctrinal legal method: start from statute and case authority (Stamp Payments Act, Griswold v. Hepburn line of cases), identify the classificatory gap, then scope a thesis-style research programme with numbered footnotes to regulators (FinCEN, ECB) and criminology literature. The mode is problem-setting rather than advocacy — he inventories open questions and positions himself as the analyst who will answer them across the series.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Contradicts the crypto-anarchist "Bitcoin is beyond law / regulation is impossible" stance: Craig's premise is that century-old money statutes already bite on electronic tokens and that classification frameworks are merely lagging, not inapplicable.
- Contradicts the "Bitcoin fixes fraud / trustless = safe" narrative: fraud is not removed but transformed — "many old frauds" gain "global prospects".
- Contradicts single-classification orthodoxies on both sides (the SEC-era "it's a security/commodity" turf war and the maximalist "it's just money"): his polymorphism claim denies that any one label fits, shifting analysis to the transaction level — a move with large regulatory consequences.
- Contradicts the "no third party needed" celebration: he treats the absence of an issuer as a legal problem (whose obligation is a bitcoin?) rather than a feature.
