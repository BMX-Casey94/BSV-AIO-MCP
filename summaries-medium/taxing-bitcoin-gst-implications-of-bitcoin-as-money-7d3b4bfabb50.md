# Taxing Bitcoin — GST implications of Bitcoin as money
**Date:** 2018-10-30 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/taxing-bitcoin-gst-implications-of-bitcoin-as-money-7d3b4bfabb50
**Subtitle:** 1.1 Overview of the Australian GST system

## Core thesis
Applying the Australian GST Act to Bitcoin yields clean results if Bitcoin is treated as money: mining is not a taxable supply at all (there is no identifiable recipient), mere holding attracts no GST, spending bitcoin is no different from spending Australian dollars, and exchange sales are input taxed or GST-free like foreign-currency dealings. The alternative — treating bitcoin as non-money — produces "anomalous and problematic" double taxation of every payment. The post is a technical tax opinion that quietly normalises Bitcoin within existing fiscal machinery.

## Key arguments and claims
- GST basics: a taxable supply under s.9–5 requires supply for consideration, in furtherance of an enterprise, connected with Australia, by a registered (or registrable) entity; what is taxed is "not consumption [but] a particular form of transaction, namely supply" (Sterling Guardian).
- Mining (12.5 BTC block-reward era) likely constitutes carrying on an enterprise: miners invest heavily in hardware, operate at scale "routinely and systematically", and intend profit — satisfying the TR 97/11 business indicators; registration is required above AUD $75,000 turnover, and voluntary registration allows input-tax-credit claims.
- But mining makes no taxable supply: per GSTR 2006/9 a supply needs an identifiable recipient ("you cannot make a supply to the world at large"), and miners validate transactions without access to the parties — so block rewards are not consideration for any supply, and "mining activities alone would not give rise to taxable supplies by the miners".
- Holding bitcoin creates no supplies and hence no GST consequences.
- Spending bitcoin as money: s.9–10(4) excludes supplies of money, so the payer has no GST liability; the supplier's liability follows the ordinary rules (nature of supply, Australian connection, enterprise, registration) — a café taking bitcoin makes an ordinary taxable supply.
- Valuation: s.9–85 requires consideration in Australian currency; bitcoin amounts should be converted by the GSTR 2001/2 foreign-currency formula using exchange rates from bitcoin exchanges or providers like OANDA and XE — "there is no reason why the rates quoted… cannot be used".
- Buying/selling bitcoin: exchange-held coins are treated as held on the user's behalf, so trades are between users; a sale of bitcoin may be input taxed (if bitcoin is a "foreign currency" under reg 40–5.09(3) item 9), GST-free (sales to non-residents or for use outside Australia, s.38–190), taxable (an anomaly if foreign-currency status fails), or outside scope (no Australian connection). GST-free treatment "may be most applicable" given major exchanges are overseas.
- The alternative view is reductio: if bitcoin is not money, every payment creates two taxable supplies (payer and payee tax each other) and AUD–bitcoin exchanges are taxable — results "inconsistent with equivalent transactions where real-world currency is used", raising complexity, compliance costs and non-compliance risk.
- Currency status is trending towards recognition: Germany's "unit of account" declaration and the Swiss parliamentary postulate make formal foreign-currency recognition "inevitable… in the near future".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Textbook Australian tax-opinion method: statutory elements first (ss.9–5, 9–10, 9–20, 9–85, 11–5), then rulings and case law (GSTR 2006/9, GSTR 2001/2, TR 97/11, Sterling Guardian, Travelex), then application to each fact pattern (mining, holding, spending, exchanging), closing with the alternative hypothesis to show its absurdity. He reasons by legal analogy to foreign exchange throughout, and his key technical move is jurisdictional: mining fails the recipient requirement, so the most politically charged activity (creating money) escapes GST entirely on doctrinal grounds.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against the "Bitcoin is property, every spend is a taxable disposal" treatment (the US IRS 2014 position and the ATO's then-approach): Craig's money analysis removes GST from payments entirely, and brands the property treatment an anomaly-producing error.
- Against the anti-state, tax-resistance strand of crypto culture: the essay's purpose is to make Bitcoin fully legible to and compliant with the tax system — miners should register, claim input credits and keep records like any enterprise.
- Descriptively against "miners are paid by the network" folklore: his GST analysis rests on miners having no counterparty — validation is a service to no one identifiable, a subtle underpinning of his broader claim that mining is a competitive commercial activity, not a commons.

## Notable quotes
- "You cannot make a supply to the world at large." (quoting GSTR 2006/9)
- "It is submitted that mining activities alone would not give rise to taxable supplies by the miners."
- "The holding of bitcoins alone would not involve the user holding the bitcoins making any supplies for GST purposes."
- "There is no reason why the rates quoted by bitcoin exchanges or foreign-exchange service providers cannot be used to convert bitcoin to Australian currency for GST purposes."
- "Both the payer and the payee would be making taxable supplies to each other" (the anomalous alternative).
