# Negotiable Instruments — core principles

- **Negotiable instrument, defined.** Per Simmons v. London Joint Stock Bank (House of Lords, 1891/92): an instrument whose property is acquired by anyone who takes it bona fide and for value, perfected notwithstanding any defect of title in the transferor — and transferable by simple delivery.
- **Money cannot be followed into innocent hands.** Miller v. Race (Lord Mansfield): money "cannot be recovered after it has passed in currency" — the bona fide taker for value keeps it even where the transferor was a thief. The "for value" element is load-bearing: a gift is not for value.
- **On-chain Bitcoin transfers are perfected on delivery.** "Bitcoin is a negotiable instrument. It is perfected when passed from one party on-chain to another" — so coins received in good faith for value remain with the recipient even if previously stolen.
- **Unconfirmed-channel balances are debts, not cash.** A channel-based transfer "is more like a cheque and is only perfected later"; delivery occurs only when the channel closes, so until then the payment remains a recoverable debt — an innocent merchant holding such a balance "can be required to return the amounts".
- **Cryptographic keys do not stop a court assigning an asset.** Refusing to comply with a court order yields four outcomes: contempt (indefinite incarceration without trial), seizure of other assets, court-ordered reassignment of the property with the ledger updated, or life as a fugitive; intentionally destroying keys "could be deemed a crime".
- **Tokens tied to real assets can be reassigned regardless of ledger state.** A token representing share equity "can be re-assigned no matter the state on a blockchain".
- **"Code is Law" is a fallacy.** "Owning your keys alone does not make you safe, it does not mean you are outside the real world" — Bitcoin is an economic system that works precisely because legal process routes around cryptography when required.
- **Merchant rule of thumb.** What you receive as cash, for value and bona fide, remains yours; card-style payments are yours "eventually (and maybe)" because they can be taken back — the perfection timing of the payment rail determines who keeps the money when the state arrives.
