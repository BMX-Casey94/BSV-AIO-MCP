# Why code must not be law
**Date:** 2019-05-18 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/why-code-must-not-be-law-438e2cafe2e4
**Subtitle:** In early 2014, I had a company called Hotwire in Australia that had just under 50 staff members. In December 2013, with 45 staff members…

## Core thesis
Through the story of his company Hotwire — 45 staff building Bitcoin core-banking software in 2013, nearly forced into liquidation by ex-staff allegedly conspiring with an AntiSec member ("Contrarian") and a Verizon mail administrator to steal its intellectual property — Craig argues that disputes over truth, documents and ownership belong in courts under rules of evidence, not in code or on social media. Bitcoin itself was designed as the anti-anarchist system: an immutable ledger where "you cannot erase the logs", built to ensure systems like bit gold and the anonymity tools now pushed by Bitcoin Core developers never enable operator-less crime.

## Key arguments and claims
- Hotwire (early 2014) had just under 50 staff, 45 on Bitcoin projects, "burning $4.6 million a year in payroll alone — just in Australia", developing "a core banking solution based on Bitcoin that used the Bitcoin ledger" covered by his patents. Exchange closures in 2013–14 left them "nominally asset rich and cash poor", using Caribbean exchanges to liquidate: "It was not the government that I was hiding from; it was the rest of the world."
- Anonymous, LulzSec and WikiLeaks are "criminal organisation[s]"; "Much of why I created Bitcoin stems from all of this. It includes fighting the AntiSec movement and the criminal groups that have come from it."
- Greg Maxwell "has been a thorn in my side for a long time"; his Zero Knowledge Contingent Payment system is "basically everything Bitcoin isn't" — its purpose is "to enable conditional payments for ransomware", i.e. "autonomous verifiable ransomware" that "does not require operators".
- The criminal's technical recipe: a Schnorr signature plus a mixer plus a conditionally provable key yields "a criminal organisation, one that distributes funds and pays members without having to be registered in any country... no head on the Hydra". Ransomware already costs the US economy over $1 billion a year; Bitcoin resists this because "the system is not anonymous" and has "an immutable audit trail".
- "When they talk about freedom, they are not talking about free society but the freedom to commit crimes."
- The Hotwire plot: ex-staff, "Contrarian" (an AntiSec/Anonymous member from 2009–2011) and a Verizon mail-system administrator in the ACT "had plotted to ensure that Hotwire would never come out of liquidation, and everything that I've been working for would be up for grabs" — the Rubik chat logs show a plan to take the software into a new financial-services company, which required liquidation.
- He refused: "We paid 100 cents on the dollar of every debt, and took Hotwire out of administration with zero debt owing" (external administrator engaged 9 April 2014), forgoing $4 million rather than let the IP be stolen. An ex-staffer "had to be injuncted and stopped from his plans to sell my intellectual property".
- Evidence claims: he retains ex-staff computers, untouched and handed "to my lawyers and the forensic investigators"; one held a copy of his wife's email store (she was Chief People Officer) and "copies of many of the documents that are now floating around the Internet". "Possession is not ownership. Possession does not lead to creation."
- Kleiman link: "some of the same backdoors led to documents ending up somehow with Mr Ira Kleiman, who is of course suing me through false pretences." A CFO he fired had tried to sell his bitcoin and assets in a ~US$300 million New York deal.
- Contract law over code: "it's not the contract as a piece of paper that is binding. It's the agreement"; courts apply hearsay rules and spot "discrepancies like different fonts" in altered documents — unlike "proof of social media (PoSM)". Metanet will store documents so the "AntiSec crowd" cannot alter them.
- Copyright point: Greg Maxwell leaked Aaron Swartz's JSTOR cache to Pirate Bay as "part of an AntiSec operation"; scanning and retyping creates a new copyright in the digital version, so Maxwell "stole JSTOR's work product".
- Satoshi-history claims: "It was the other people such as James Donald who I sparred with as Satoshi who where the die-hard cyberpunks and anarchists. I designed Bitcoin to ensure that systems like bit gold never took off." His solution to the Byzantine Generals Problem works "in part because you cannot erase the logs" — the property anonymity systems and Lightning try to remove.
- Scale of the IP pipeline: "over 700 applications have already been filed and another thousand are on the way"; he chose not to be nChain CEO because "to do one role well, you need focus".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Autobiographical narrative doubles as legal argument: the Hotwire story supplies the evidentiary frame (discovery, forensic imaging, injunctions, hearsay rules) through which all internet-circulated documents about him should be judged. He pairs this with technical threat analysis — decomposing ZKCP/Schnorr/mixers into a ransomware-enabling primitive — and a copyright-doctrine aside, all unified by the title's inversion: the "code-is-law mentality" is what licensed the theft attempt, while real law is what stopped it.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Frontal assault on "code is law", the smart-contract/cypherpunk axiom: agreements, evidence and courts govern, and code-is-law thinking "breeds" insider theft.
- Recasts Bitcoin Core developers' privacy roadmap (confidential transactions, Schnorr, ZKCP) as ransomware infrastructure — "everything Bitcoin isn't".
- Denies Bitcoin's cypherpunk parentage: the anarchists (James Donald, bit gold advocates) were his opponents, and Bitcoin was designed so their systems "never took off".
- Anonymity is not a feature but the enabler of operator-less crime; privacy claims from Core are cover for removing the audit trail that makes Bitcoin strong.
- "Proof of social media" — the court of Twitter/Reddit opinion that dominated 2019's Satoshi wars — is epistemically worthless next to rules of evidence, a direct shot at the Hodlonaut-era mob dynamics.
