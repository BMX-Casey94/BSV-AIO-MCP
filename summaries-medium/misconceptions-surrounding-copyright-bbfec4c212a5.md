# Misconceptions surrounding copyright
**Date:** 2018-09-09 | **URL:** https://medium.com/@craig_10243/misconceptions-surrounding-copyright-bbfec4c212a5
**Subtitle:** Many think that when you pay a company to create something for you that you naturally own the copyright. This is not correct. Copyright…

## Core thesis
Paying for a work does not transfer its copyright — transfer requires explicit contractual assignment, and default ownership rules (author, employer, joint authors) are more intricate than commonly assumed. Craig then pivots: Bitcoin's immutable timestamping plus nChain's smart-contract technology can register works on-chain, track component-level rights, and automate licensing of derivative works, including algorithmic management of fair use.

## Key arguments and claims
- "Copyright requires an explicit transfer of rights" — commissioning a report does not make it the commissioning organisation's property absent an express assignment clause.
- Employee-created works are "a separate matter": under s 35(4) of the Australian Copyright Act 1968, even an employed journalist retains rights for selected purposes (book compilation, photocopying) while the employer keeps the remaining rights.
- Defaults can be varied "through agreement and by assignment [ss 35(3), 97(3), 98(3), 179]"; ownership must be checked case by case — never assume it lies with creator, commissioner, or employer.
- Joint authorship: where contributions are indistinguishable, the work is jointly owned; where authors contribute separable parts (e.g. chapters), each holds copyright in their own input — and each joint author "can make derivative works using the whole or even republish a book using the material".
- nChain product plug: "nChain have developed intellectual property that can allow the employer to maintain a complete register of all copyright", with IP "recorded in hashed format" on the blockchain so an employer can "instantly know if an employee is placing them at risk by sharing or accessing unlicensed material".
- "Bitcoin, as an immutable time stamping system also acts to allow a creator to save a hash of a work on the blockchain indefinitely."
- "Bitcoin will allow the creation of complex smart contracts that can be used to allocated and assign rights within a defined and agreed set of terms that are indexed or attached in an OP_RETURN."
- Component-level licensing becomes automatic: one author's part under an MIT licence, another's under standard copyright — "Automatically and with complete autonomy" — with global monitoring of derivative use.
- Fair use can be "algorithmically agreed and maintained": academics would know when they near the fair-use boundary, and "any excess use can be instantly licensed and the rights assigned (for consideration)".

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
Black-letter legal exposition first — statutory sections, default rules, joint-authorship doctrine — then the pivot "This is where Bitcoin comes into play", mapping each legal capability onto a chain mechanism: hash timestamping for proof of existence, OP_RETURN-indexed smart contracts for rights assignment, on-chain registers for monitoring. The essay doubles as nChain marketing embedded in legal education, with the law supplying the requirements spec and Bitcoin the claimed implementation.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- Against the "OP_RETURN is spam / Bitcoin is only for money" orthodoxy: data-carrying transactions for rights registration and licensing are treated as first-class Bitcoin use cases.
- Against the crypto-anarchist "information wants to be free" current: Craig is explicitly pro-IP and pro-enforcement, proposing on-chain infrastructure to detect unlicensed use — including by employees.
- Against the "code is law / no intermediaries" instinct: this is a vision of Bitcoin as plumbing for the existing copyright system, complete with paid licensing "for consideration", not its abolition.
- Context: September 2018, mid nChain patent-portfolio build-out — the essay normalises the idea that a company like nChain supplies the IP layer for blockchain-based rights management.
