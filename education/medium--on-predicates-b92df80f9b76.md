# On Predicates — core principles

- **Law cannot be reduced to deterministic code.** Common-law predicates — *tort*, *contract*, *negligent*, *good faith*, *unconscionable* — are semantically open-ended, and applying them to disputed facts requires human judicial judgement: "we cannot expect 'Code is law.' to hold true."
- **Legal meaning is pragmatic, not merely semantic.** Words operate in context and use, including "the scope of the speaker's purpose — social effect" — so their application is not mechanically computable.
- **A decided case is a conjunction of factual propositions.** Each party presents sentences describing facts; the decided case is "a conjunction of all the propositions expressing the particular factual findings", and the judicial function is deciding whether that conjunction falls within a legal predicate — a discretionary, socially embedded act.
- **Fact-finding is a system layer code cannot replicate.** Facts are "often in dispute", and their resolution by judge or jury is itself part of the legal system.
- **Judicial declarations carry authority but are not set in stone.** "If a judge says, 'This is a battery,' then unless a superior court reverses the judge… the decision stands" — however ignorant or perverse it may seem — yet decisions "need not be fixed in stone" and can be altered on appeal.
- **Stare decisis is practice, not statute.** No statutory rule exists in Great Britain; the House of Lords Practice Statement 1966 is merely "a statement of an intent of a court", and identical words diverge across cases — "printed" has meant books and journals in one case, microfilm in another.
- **Law evolves; it does not hard-fork.** Judges adapt "not in a revolutionary sweep but rather in an evolutionary manner", moulding law "to social, political, moral, and economic requirements" — hard, fast automated predicates are easily unfair.
- **Design implication for builders.** Bitcoin's script system should implement contracts *within* law — specific performance, registries, evidence — not substitute for courts; "the judicial process is far more complex than can be noted in such a simplistic area as a smart contract".
