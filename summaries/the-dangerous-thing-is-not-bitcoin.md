# The Dangerous Thing Is Not Bitcoin, but Utility
**Date:** 2026-05-16 | **URL:** https://singulargrit.substack.com/p/the-dangerous-thing-is-not-bitcoin
**Subtitle:** Why campaigns of ridicule form around systems that threaten intermediaries, and why real peer-to-peer exchange is more radical than the revolutions that merely replace one ruling class with another

## Core thesis
The ferocity of the attacks on Bitcoin — and on Craig personally — is explained by utility, not uselessness: a working system of direct peer-to-peer exchange threatens the rents of every intermediary class, so the argument must be made socially radioactive before it is examined. Bitcoin's value lies in what it does — direct exchange, evidence, receipts, settlement, tokenised assets, micropayments — not in hoarding a scarce token. Real peer-to-peer exchange is more radical than political revolution, because revolutions merely replace one intermediary class with another while direct trade asks why anyone must stand between the parties at all.

## Key arguments and claims
- Ridicule follows a ritual pattern: first the insult, then its repetition, then "the institutional comfort of treating the insult as common knowledge". The person is rendered absurd so the argument need not be rendered false; the correct response to quarantine is the question *cui bono* — who benefits?
- Peer review is "not canonisation" but "not nothing": continued publication cracks the social story, because a campaign of derision "cannot honestly coexist with a refusal to engage the work".
- The analytical frame is institutional economics: Coase ("The Nature of the Firm", 1937) on transaction costs, North (1990) on institutions structuring incentives, and Williamson (1975, 1985) on governance of contractual relations under uncertainty, opportunism and asset specificity.
- "Digital gold" is a concession of defeat: an asset that exists merely to be hoarded "is a trinket", and the radical element of electronic cash is use, not hoarding.
- Technology does not abolish intermediation. Because BTC cannot function as cash at scale, the market routed around it with exchanges, custodians, payment processors, Lightning hubs, wallet providers, ETF issuers and brokers — "new toll collector" rebranded as "infrastructure".
- A base asset never prevented financial intermediation: the gold standard did not abolish bank credit, fractional reserve banking or clearing houses. Holding exposure through a custodian that can rehypothecate or restrict withdrawals confuses the asset with the institutional arrangement around it. "If people hold claims rather than assets, banking returns" — with "orange branding".
- The electricity analogy: the value was never the current but what it enabled. Bitcoin's lightbulb is direct trade — Alice paying Bob without "a procession of institutions", plus receipts, instant verification, settlement in minutes, tokenised assets, micropayments and B2B auditability. "Number go up" is "a casino sign".
- Speculation is absorbable by finance (brokering, leveraging, securitising); utility is not, because it bypasses the menu rather than adding to it. "A bank can survive being hated. It cannot survive being irrelevant."
- Revolutions replace intermediary classes: the French Revolution was led by lawyers, pamphleteers and administrators (citing Tocqueville, Furet, Schama); Lenin came from a family elevated into hereditary nobility. Revolution "announces emancipation and delivers management".
- False decentralisation recreates hierarchy: "Call everyone a node, and no one asks who actually controls the rules" — while a small development priesthood decides what software matters.
- Evidence replaces expensive trust: receipts, contracts and audit trails are "the memory of exchange"; privacy and accountability are compatible, since cash always permitted private exchange while tax authorities still taxed.

## How Craig reasons (his model/logic)
The method is incentive analysis within institutional economics, combined with historical analogy. He treats friction (delays, reconciliations, custody, chargebacks) as revenue and asks who profits from it; he then tests revolutionary claims against the historical record of France and Russia to show that movements nominally empowering "the people" install new governing classes. Bitcoin matters only if it escapes that pattern architecturally — by making direct exchange economically viable at scale rather than by installing new priests.

## Where this contradicts BTC-mainstream logic
- The "digital gold"/store-of-value thesis is explicitly named a defeat: scarcity rhetoric has never "fed anyone, paid anyone, settled anything, or built a commercial order".
- The hoard-and-custody model (ETFs, exchanges, custodians) is not adoption but the reconstruction of banking: "One has built a new banking layer and asked it to wear revolutionary colours."
- The non-mining full node as guardian of the network is dismissed: economic nodes "do not become legitimate because they are numerous in a sentimental map" but because they perform the system's economic function.
- BTC culture's speculative identity is the very cover under which utility is obscured: "The public is shown the casino and told the payment system does not exist."
